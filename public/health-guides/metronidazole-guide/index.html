Metronidazole

A physician's guide to a common antibiotic: what it treats, how to take it, and the alcohol warning that matters.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: September 8, 2026
11 min read

What is metronidazole and what is it used for?

Metronidazole is a nitroimidazole antibiotic that treats certain anaerobic bacteria and protozoa. Its most common uses are the sexually transmitted infection trichomoniasis and bacterial vaginosis, along with certain dental, abdominal, and parasitic infections. The two things every patient should know are to complete the full course, and to avoid alcohol during treatment and for a few days after, because the combination causes a distinctly unpleasant reaction. A licensed clinician determines whether metronidazole is the right drug and the correct dose for the specific infection.

Metronidazole is one of those medicines prescribed for a surprisingly wide range of infections, from a sexually transmitted parasite to a tooth infection to a stomach ulcer. It is effective and generally safe when used correctly, but it comes with one unusual rule that catches people off guard: no alcohol while you take it. This guide explains what the drug is, what it treats, and the few things you genuinely need to know while taking it.

What Is Metronidazole?

Metronidazole is a nitroimidazole antimicrobial, meaning it works against a specific set of anaerobic bacteria, organisms that thrive without oxygen, and certain protozoa, single-celled parasites.[1] It is available by mouth, as a topical gel or cream, and as a vaginal gel. It is not a broad-spectrum antibiotic like penicillin; it targets a narrower range, which is why it is chosen for specific infections.

Trichomoniasis and bacterial vaginosis are both common and treatable, and metronidazole is a standard prescription for each. For symptoms, testing, and how care is provided, see the trichomoniasis treatment and bacterial vaginosis treatment pages.

How It Works

Once inside an anaerobic bacterium or parasite, metronidazole is converted into a reactive form that disrupts the organism's DNA, killing it.[1] Because this conversion only happens in oxygen-poor environments, the drug spares most normal aerobic bacteria in the body.

Dosing by Condition

ConditionTypical adult regimen
Trichomoniasis (women)500 mg twice daily for 7 days[2]
Trichomoniasis (men)2 g single dose[2]
Bacterial vaginosis500 mg twice daily for 7 days[3]
Other anaerobic infectionsVaries; prescribed by a clinician[1]

The dose depends entirely on the infection, which is why it is never a good idea to reuse a leftover course. Always finish the full course as prescribed, even after you feel better.[1]

The Alcohol Warning

The rule everyone remembers about metronidazole is real: do not drink alcohol while taking it and for at least 48 to 72 hours after the last dose.[1] Combining metronidazole with alcohol can produce a disulfiram-like reaction, flushing, nausea, vomiting, a racing heart, and a throbbing headache. It is unpleasant rather than usually life-threatening, but it is simple to avoid.

Side Effects

The most common side effects are a metallic taste in the mouth and nausea.[1] Metronidazole also commonly turns urine darker, sometimes reddish-brown, which is harmless but worth knowing so it does not alarm. With prolonged or high-dose use, a more serious side effect is peripheral neuropathy, numbness, tingling, or pain in the hands and feet.[1]

Other Interactions

InteractionEffect
AlcoholDisulfiram-like reaction[1]

Tell your clinician about all medicines you take, and about any history of nerve, liver, or blood problems, before starting metronidazole.

Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

Metronidazole is used in pregnancy when indicated and is considered low risk according to available data, but any infection during pregnancy should be managed by the obstetric team rather than through general telehealth care.[2] This guide's use of metronidazole assumes a non-pregnant adult.

When to Contact a Clinician

Contact a clinician if

You develop numbness, tingling, or pain in the hands or feet, severe nausea that prevents completing the course, or signs of an allergic reaction such as a rash, swelling, or difficulty breathing.[1]
