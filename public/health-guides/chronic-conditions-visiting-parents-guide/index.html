Chronic Conditions While Your Parents Visit the US

A physician's evidence-based guide for adult children hosting visiting parents: how to keep blood pressure, diabetes, thyroid, and heart medications on track across a long flight, when to worry after jet lag, and how a US telehealth visit fits in without disrupting care back home.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: August 14, 2026
15 min read

How can I keep my visiting parents' chronic conditions stable during a US trip?

For older international visitors with chronic conditions like hypertension, type 2 diabetes, hypothyroidism, atrial fibrillation, COPD, or asthma, the four things that keep a US trip uneventful are a pre-travel visit with the home clinician at least 4 to 6 weeks before departure, a time-zone plan for daily medications agreed with that clinician, all prescriptions packed in original containers in carry-on luggage with a written medication list, and a low threshold to use US telehealth for a same-day check-in when something feels off. The CDC Yellow Book advises pre-travel consultation at least 4 to 6 weeks before departure and says travelers' chronic conditions should be controlled and managed optimally. For trips of about 5 time zones or fewer, or under 3 days, a practical review advises keeping watches on home time and taking basal insulin as usual; for 5 or more time zones with a stay longer than 3 days, it describes adjusting the basal insulin dose by about 4 percent per time zone crossed (westward travel leads to missed doses and eastward travel to excess doses, per Forst et al. 2021). In a randomized crossover trial, bedtime levothyroxine improved thyroid hormone levels compared with morning dosing (Bolk et al. 2010 Arch Intern Med), so bedtime dosing is an option to discuss with the prescriber for the trip. Evidence on melatonin for jet lag in older travelers is mixed: the CATMAT Statement on Older Travellers says it cannot be routinely recommended because of lack of evidence of benefit, while the CDC Yellow Book notes that 0.5 to 1 mg is often sufficient and that doses above 5 mg are not recommended.

Key Takeaways

  • A pre-travel visit with the home clinician at least 4 to 6 weeks before departure gives time to get conditions and medications in order. The CDC Yellow Book says travelers' chronic conditions should be controlled and managed optimally, and the CATMAT Statement on Older Travellers says chronic health conditions should be under optimal control prior to travel.[1][2]
  • Pack medication for the entire trip plus extra in case of unexpected delays, in original containers, all in carry-on luggage. Carry a written medication list using generic (not trade) drug names.[1][3]
  • For trips crossing about 5 time zones or fewer, or lasting under 3 days, a practical diabetes review advises keeping watches on home time and taking basal insulin as usual.[4]
  • For travel across 5 or more time zones with a stay longer than 3 days, the same review describes adjusting the basal insulin dose by about 4 percent per time zone crossed. Westward travel lengthens the day and may require more insulin; eastward travel shortens the day and may require less.[4][8]
  • Bedtime levothyroxine is an option to discuss with the prescriber. In a randomized crossover trial (Bolk et al. 2010), bedtime dosing gave lower TSH and higher free T4 than morning dosing, although the FDA label directs morning dosing on an empty stomach.[5][13]
  • Evidence on melatonin for jet lag in older travelers is mixed. The CATMAT older-traveller statement says melatonin cannot be routinely recommended because of lack of evidence of benefit; the CDC Yellow Book notes that 0.5 to 1 mg is often sufficient, doses above 5 mg are not recommended, and melatonin is not regulated by the FDA.[2][6]
  • Clot risk is negligible on flights under 4 hours. Older age and prior venous thromboembolism (VTE) are risk factors; for long-distance travelers at increased risk, American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) guidance summarized by the CDC suggests properly fitted below-the-knee compression stockings giving 15 to 30 mmHg at the ankle (a weak recommendation). Medication to prevent clots is a decision for the treating clinician.[14][1]

Hosting a parent or older relative on a long US visit is one of the most common reasons adult children reach out to a US clinician for guidance. The care itself is usually not complicated. What is complicated is the sequence: a long flight across many time zones, a stable but medicated 70-year-old, a bag of foreign-branded prescriptions with unfamiliar names, and a two-week window in which anything from mild dehydration to a missed thyroid dose can quietly nudge things off course.

This guide is the practical, evidence-based playbook for that specific situation. It supplements our broader pillar guide on medical care for international visitors and our satellite on running out of medication in the US. It focuses on the four conditions we see most in visiting parents (hypertension, type 2 diabetes, hypothyroidism, and asthma or COPD), with brief notes on anticoagulation and atrial fibrillation.

For visitors from India specifically, see the country-focused companion, Medical Care in the US for Visitors From India, which covers Indian brand-to-US mapping (Eltroxin, Amlong, Storvas, Glycomet, Cardace), Indian medications that are controlled substances in the US, insulin timing for the 9- to 10-hour shift from Delhi, Mumbai, or Bangalore, and the South Asian diaspora clinical risk profile; for visitors from Mexico, see Medical Care in the US for Visitors From Mexico.

The Pre-Travel Visit With the Home Doctor Matters More Than Anything Else

The CDC Yellow Book advises that travelers with chronic conditions seek pre-travel consultation before paying for non-refundable trips and at least 4 to 6 weeks before departure.[1] The Canadian Committee to Advise on Tropical Medicine and Travel (CATMAT) Statement on Older Travellers makes the same point, with a recommendation graded B-III: "Older travellers should seek pre-travel advice early to allow for appropriate pre-travel assessments" and "Chronic health conditions should be under optimal control prior to travel."[2]

Practically, this visit produces four things that no US telehealth visit can substitute for:

  1. A letter on the home clinic's letterhead outlining existing medical conditions and the medications prescribed, including generic names and doses.[1] This becomes the most useful document if your parent needs any US care.
  2. A short "fitness to travel" letter confirming the conditions are stable, including any devices or supplies (CPAP, inhalers, glucose meter, insulin pens, needles). A clinician's letter may be needed to take injectable medication through airport security.[9]
  3. Enough medication for the entire trip plus extra in case of unexpected delays.[1]
  4. An explicit conversation about what to do if something changes: which US symptoms should trigger a telehealth visit, which should trigger urgent care, which should trigger the ER, and how to communicate back to the home clinician.

Aerospace Medical Association guidance summarized by the CDC Yellow Book adds a specific list of conditions that should be formally re-evaluated before travel: "cardiovascular disease, diabetes, chronic lung disease, mental illness, seizures, stroke, recent surgery, or a history of deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism."[7]

Packing Medications for a Visiting Parent

The CDC Yellow Book gives specific packing advice for travelers with chronic illnesses:[1]

  • Medications for the entire trip plus extra in case of unexpected delays.
  • All medications in their original containers, not decanted into pill boxes or unlabeled bottles.
  • All medications in carry-on (hand) luggage, not checked bags. Baggage stored in cargo holds can be exposed to extreme temperature changes that may alter the potency of insulin.[8]
  • A doctor's letter on office letterhead outlining existing medical conditions and the medications prescribed, including generic names and doses.
  • A written medication list using generic names. The CDC travel health kit guidance lists "regular medications (generic, not trade names)" among the documents to carry.[3] A US pharmacist may not recognize a foreign brand name but can work from the generic name.

For visitors on injectable medications (insulin, GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide and dulaglutide, or anticoagulants like enoxaparin), keep them in the original labeled packaging, carry a copy of the prescription, and bring a signed letter from the home clinician documenting the need; a primary care review notes that such a letter may be needed to take injectable medication through airport security.[3][9]

For a full walkthrough of foreign-to-US brand name mapping and what a US pharmacy will and will not fill, see our satellite guide, Running Out of Medication in the US.

Time-Zone Dosing: The Simple Rule First

For basal insulin, a 2021 practical review gives a simple rule of thumb:[4]

If the trip crosses about 5 time zones or fewer, or the stay is under 3 days, keep watches set to home time and take basal insulin as usual. For once-daily tablets, the CDC Yellow Book advises discussing with the clinician when to take medications if travel involves changes in time zones.[1]

Insulin dose adjustments are described when both of the following are true:

  1. The time zone shift is 5 or more hours.
  2. The stay in the new time zone is longer than 3 days.

For a visitor coming from India to the US East Coast (9.5 to 10.5 hours of shift, weeks at destination), from Europe to the US West Coast (8 to 9 hours, weeks at destination), or from East Asia to the US (clocks typically 12 to 16 hours behind, weeks at destination, but these flights travel east across the date line, so see the Tokyo to Los Angeles row in Table 1), both conditions are true and a specific plan is needed. That plan is the subject of the next two sections.

Insulin Across Time Zones: The 4-Percent-Per-Hour Rule

For patients on long-acting basal insulin who are traveling across 5 or more time zones and staying in the new time zone longer than 3 days, the 2021 Diabetes/Metabolism Research and Reviews practical review of basal insulin in type 2 diabetes by Forst and colleagues describes a 4 percent adjustment to the insulin dose for each time zone crossed. One hour is 4 percent of a 24-hour day, so the ratio scales with the size of the time-zone jump.[4]

Direction of travel matters:

  • Westbound travel means a longer day. The traveler is awake for extra hours before the next basal insulin dose. That extra insulin-free period risks hyperglycemia unless a small extra dose is added or the next dose is timed slightly earlier. In simple terms: expect to need more insulin going west.[4][8]
  • Eastbound travel means a shorter day. The next basal insulin dose lands closer to the previous one than usual, so total insulin exposure is compressed. That risks hypoglycemia unless the dose is trimmed or delayed. In simple terms: expect to need less insulin going east.[4][8]

The 2003 Clinical Diabetes article "Have Insulin, Will Fly" puts it this way: "Traveling east will shorten one's day, and, in general, may necessitate a reduction in insulin" and "In contrast, westward travel means a longer day, and so insulin doses may need to be increased." The same article cautions that this rule of "westward = more insulin; eastward = less insulin" may not always hold true, and that adjustments to insulin doses are generally unnecessary when crossing fewer than five time zones.[8]

For patients on once-daily longer-acting basal insulins such as insulin degludec, which provides a consistent level of insulin for more than 24 hours after a dose (about 42 hours), the dose may simply be taken at the usual time on the day of and after travel.[9] Patients on insulin detemir (clinical activity of about 24 hours) may need to adjust the timing of the dose around travel, and twice-daily regimens on medium- and long-haul trips need more frequent monitoring and adjustments to the timing of daily doses.[9]

The same article advises testing blood glucose at least every 4 to 6 hours while traveling, even for people who do not test often at home.[8] Trips crossing the international date line gain or lose a calendar day, but the direction of flight and the clock-time shift are what matter for insulin dosing.

Oral Daily Medications: Simpler Than Insulin

Many chronic-condition medications visiting parents take are once-daily oral pills. Common examples include:

  • Most blood pressure medications: amlodipine, lisinopril, losartan, valsartan, hydrochlorothiazide, ramipril, telmisartan.
  • Statins: atorvastatin, rosuvastatin, simvastatin, pravastatin.
  • Once-daily oral diabetes medications: metformin extended release, sitagliptin, empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, glimepiride.
  • Aspirin 81 mg, clopidogrel, apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran.
  • Levothyroxine (special-cased below).
  • Once-daily inhaled controllers like fluticasone furoate/vilanterol or tiotropium.
  • PPIs like omeprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole.

Published guidance on shifting these tablets across time zones is thin. The CDC Yellow Book advises that clinicians discuss with travelers when to take medications if travel involves changes in time zones.[1] Before departure, ask the home clinician or pharmacist for a written plan for the travel day, especially for anticoagulants (apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran) and for twice-daily or three-times-daily regimens.

Levothyroxine: Ask About Bedtime Dosing to Simplify the Trip

Levothyroxine is a common medication for visiting parents, and it is fussy about timing: the FDA label directs a single daily dose on an empty stomach, one-half to one hour before breakfast, taken at least 4 hours before or after calcium, iron, and antacids.[13] A systematic review also found limited evidence that coffee and calcium or iron supplements reduce its absorption.[10] A home routine of "morning, before breakfast, no coffee" becomes hard to keep on a 14-hour flight and during the first week in a new time zone.

An evidence-based workaround is bedtime dosing at destination time, to discuss with the prescriber because it differs from the label's directions. The randomized double-blind crossover trial by Bolk and colleagues in Archives of Internal Medicine (2010) enrolled 105 patients with primary hypothyroidism (90 completed and were analyzed) and compared morning with bedtime levothyroxine for 3 months each. Bedtime dosing produced lower TSH, higher free T4, and higher total T3 than morning dosing, with no significant change in quality of life.[5] A 2015 review of four studies found inconsistent results (one study showed higher TSH with bedtime dosing) and concluded that bedtime administration is an option for patients who want to avoid taking the medication with food.[11] A 2021 systematic review concluded that morning and bedtime ingestion are equally effective.[10]

Practically, for a visiting parent this means:

  1. Before the trip, ask the prescriber whether bedtime dosing is acceptable for the visit.
  2. Continue the usual morning dose until departure.
  3. At destination, take it at one consistent time each day, keeping the same gap from food, coffee, and calcium or iron supplements.[13][10]
  4. Return to the home morning schedule after returning home, if that is the patient's preference.

Missing a single dose of levothyroxine is not urgent. The American Thyroid Association says it is safe to make up a missed dose by taking a double dose the next day.[12] The FDA label lists a levothyroxine half-life of 6 to 7 days in people with normal thyroid levels.[13]

Jet Lag vs Real Warning Signs After a Long Flight

Jet lag is real, and older adults may be more susceptible to it than younger people because of underlying illness, medication, or primary sleep disorders.[2] The CDC Yellow Book describes jet lag disorder as a sleep disorder caused by rapid travel across time zones that can show up as sleep disturbances, cognitive impairment, daytime sleepiness, and general malaise.[6] Westward travel is easier to adapt to than eastward travel, with an average adaptation of about 1.5 hours per day after westward travel and 1 hour per day after eastward travel.[6]

The problem is that several of those symptoms overlap with warning signs of decompensation in a chronic condition. Adult children hosting a parent should be able to tell the two apart. Table 2 lists what is jet lag and what is not.

Table 2. Jet lag symptoms versus warning signs of a decompensating chronic condition after a long flight.

For persistent jet lag in an older visitor, the evidence on melatonin is mixed. The CDC Yellow Book notes that American Academy of Sleep Medicine guidelines list timed melatonin among standard treatments for jet lag disorder in frequent travelers, that "0.5–1 mg is often sufficient to produce a circadian shift," and that high-dose melatonin (more than 5 mg) is not recommended. For eastward travel it suggests taking melatonin before bedtime at the destination.[6] The CATMAT older-traveller statement, by contrast, concludes that "Melatonin cannot be routinely recommended as a means of mitigating the effects of jet lag because of lack of evidence of benefit," and that benzodiazepines should not be used for jet-lag sleep disruption in the elderly because of increased risk of adverse effects.[2]

Melatonin is not appropriate for everyone. CATMAT notes that responses vary widely from person to person and that some users experience excessive drowsiness.[2] The CDC adds that melatonin is not regulated by the Food and Drug Administration, so the amount in a product may vary from what is printed on the label, and that data on how melatonin interacts with other medications are limited.[6] A US telehealth clinician can review the parent's regimen and confirm suitability before recommending.

Blood Clot Prevention on Long Flights

Long flights are a recognized risk factor for venous thromboembolism (VTE). The CDC Yellow Book notes that for flights under 4 hours the risk of symptomatic VTE is negligible, that the magnitude of risk correlates with the duration of travel and with preexisting risk factors, and that risk factors include older age (increasing after age 40), previous VTE, recent surgery or hospitalization, active cancer, and thrombophilia.[14]

Practical guidance for visiting parents on long flights:

  • Properly fitted below-the-knee graduated compression stockings providing 15 to 30 mmHg of pressure at the ankle during travel, for travelers at increased risk. The ACCP recommendation summarized by the CDC is weak and based on low-quality evidence, and for travelers without risk factors the American Society of Hematology (ASH) advises against stockings, low-molecular-weight heparin, or aspirin.[14]
  • Regular movement: frequent walking and calf muscle exercises are reasonable, and aisle seats make it easier to move around.[14]
  • Adequate hydration: reasonable and unlikely to cause harm, though not proven to prevent travel-related clots; there is no direct evidence that avoiding alcohol is protective.[14]
  • Medication to prevent clots is reserved for higher-risk travelers and is a decision for the treating clinician before the trip. When the benefits outweigh the risks, anticoagulants rather than aspirin are recommended, and the CDC chronic-illness chapter advises discussing the need for anticoagulation with the treating specialist for travel of 4 hours or more.[14][1]

Most air travel-associated VTE occurs within the first 1 to 2 weeks after the flight, and risk returns to baseline by about 8 weeks.[14] If new one-sided calf swelling, calf pain, or warmth develops after a long flight, the visitor should be evaluated for DVT. This is a same-day US urgent care or ER visit rather than a telehealth question, because imaging studies are needed for diagnosis.[14]

When US Telehealth Is Genuinely Useful for a Visiting Parent

US telehealth is a good fit for a specific set of situations during a visiting parent's stay:

  • Bridge prescription for a chronic non-controlled medication (blood pressure, thyroid, oral diabetes, statin, SSRI, asthma controller). Covered in detail in the sister satellite guide, Running Out of Medication in the US.
  • Persistent jet lag or sleep disturbance beyond the first week, especially if considering melatonin.
  • Minor symptoms clearly attributable to the flight or the trip: mild dehydration, transient constipation, mild upper respiratory symptoms, minor musculoskeletal pain.
  • Uncertainty about a home-medication question: is this dose right, is this a side effect, is this an interaction with something added in the US.
  • A stable-but-slightly-off finding: blood pressure or blood glucose running somewhat higher than usual without warning symptoms, or mild ankle swelling without breathing difficulty. These are worth reviewing with a clinician before they drift further.
  • Coordinating with the home clinician: a US telehealth clinician can produce a written note describing what was seen and what was recommended, which the visitor can share with the home doctor on return.

When to Go In Person

Some symptoms need physical examination, imaging, or lab work that telehealth cannot provide. Go to a US urgent care, emergency department, or (for severe symptoms) call 911:

  • New chest pain or pressure, especially with shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, or radiation to the arm or jaw. This is a 911 call.
  • New unilateral weakness, facial droop, or slurred speech. This is a 911 call. Use the FAST protocol: Face, Arms, Speech, Time.
  • Severe headache, worst of life, or with neurological symptoms. ER.
  • Difficulty breathing, orthopnea, ankle swelling with weight gain: signs of a heart failure exacerbation. ER or same-day urgent care.
  • Unilateral calf swelling, warmth, or tenderness after a long flight. Same-day urgent care for DVT evaluation.
  • Severe hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia with confusion, ketones, or persistent symptoms. Urgent care or ER.
  • Persistent vomiting or diarrhea with signs of dehydration (dry mouth, decreased urination, dizziness on standing) in an older adult. Urgent care.
  • Fall with head injury or possible fracture. ER.
