Burning When You Pee: UTI, Yeast Infection, STI, or Something Else?

Understand the common causes of burning or painful urination, how to tell a bladder infection from a yeast infection or an STI, and when symptoms need urgent care.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed August 30, 2026
13 min read

What causes burning when you pee?

Burning or painful urination, called dysuria, most often comes from a urinary tract infection (UTI), but it can also be caused by a yeast infection, a sexually transmitted infection (STI), interstitial cystitis, a kidney stone, or chemical irritants. The pattern of symptoms points to the cause. Burning felt inside with urinary frequency and urgency, without vaginal discharge or irritation, suggests a bladder infection. Burning felt when urine touches irritated external tissue, with itching and a thick, curdy discharge, suggests a yeast infection. Burning with urethral discharge and a new partner suggests an STI such as Chlamydia trachomatis or Neisseria gonorrhoeae. Because the treatments differ, the cause should be identified rather than assumed. Seek urgent care for fever, flank pain, nausea, or vomiting, and get prompt evaluation if you are pregnant.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated August 30, 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • Dysuria, or burning urination, has several causes, most commonly a UTI, but also yeast infection, STI, interstitial cystitis, kidney stones, and chemical irritants.[15][16][10]
  • Internal burning with frequency and urgency, without vaginal discharge, suggests cystitis; external burning with itching and a thick discharge suggests a yeast infection.[1][8]
  • Chlamydia and gonorrhea can cause urethritis with burning urination and discharge, and they need specific antibiotics plus evaluation and treatment of sex partners.[17][5][6]
  • Bladder infections are less common in men than in women, and in men clinicians also consider urethritis and prostatitis, so burning urination in a man warrants clinical evaluation.[20][4]
  • Fever, chills, flank pain, nausea, or vomiting can signal pyelonephritis, a kidney infection, and need urgent care.[21][22]
  • Phenazopyridine relieves burning but does not treat infection; a bacterial UTI needs antibiotics.[12][13]
  • Pregnancy lowers the threshold for evaluation; untreated urinary infection can progress to kidney infection and affect the pregnancy.[11][23]

Is It a UTI or a Yeast Infection?

Burning urination is the symptom that most often brings a bladder infection and a yeast infection into the same conversation, but the two feel different and are treated differently. A urinary tract infection (UTI) causes internal dysuria, a burning felt inside the urethra, usually with new urinary frequency, urgency, and suprapubic pressure or discomfort.[14][4] A yeast infection, or vulvovaginal candidiasis, causes external dysuria, a burning felt when urine passes over irritated vulvar tissue, together with itching, soreness, and a thick, curdy discharge.[8]

The classic review of this question, the Bent 2002 JAMA rational clinical examination, found that a history of vaginal discharge or vaginal irritation makes a UTI less likely, while the combination of dysuria and frequency without vaginal discharge or irritation raises the probability of a UTI to more than 90 percent.[1] Women with vulvovaginal symptoms should be evaluated for vaginitis.[15] In a healthy, nonpregnant woman with typical symptoms and no vaginal discharge, the diagnosis can be accurate enough that treatment for cystitis may be started on the basis of symptoms alone.[15][4]

The organisms also differ. Acute uncomplicated cystitis is caused mainly by Escherichia coli, with occasional other bacteria such as Proteus mirabilis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Staphylococcus saprophyticus.[2] Vulvovaginal candidiasis is usually caused by Candida albicans and is associated with a normal vaginal pH below 4.5, whereas a vaginal pH above 4.5 is one of the diagnostic criteria for bacterial vaginosis.[8][7]

Can an STI Cause Burning Urination?

Yes. Chlamydia and gonorrhea are well-established causes of urethritis, an inflammation of the urethra. When urethritis causes symptoms, they include burning urination, urethral itching, and a mucoid, mucopurulent, or purulent discharge.[17] In women, chlamydia and gonorrhea are the most common causes of cervicitis, which is often silent but can cause an abnormal vaginal discharge and bleeding between periods.[17] Both infections often cause no symptoms at all; gonorrhea in women is commonly asymptomatic.[5][6]

The CDC treatment guidelines use nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT) to diagnose both infections; urine is the preferred specimen for men, and vaginal swabs are used for women.[5][17] The recommended chlamydia treatment for adolescents and adults is doxycycline 100 mg by mouth twice daily for 7 days.[5] For uncomplicated gonorrhea, the recommended regimen is a single intramuscular dose of ceftriaxone 500 mg for people weighing less than 150 kg (1 g for those weighing 150 kg or more), with doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days added when chlamydia has not been excluded.[6] Recent sex partners should be referred for evaluation, testing, and presumptive treatment.[5][6]

Other infections can also produce burning. Genital herpes is a recognized cause of painful urination.[16] Trichomoniasis can cause a vaginal discharge, with or without vulvar irritation, in women and symptoms of urethritis in men.[18] Bacterial vaginosis typically causes a thin, milky discharge with a fishy odor, and the CDC lists burning when peeing among its possible symptoms, so BV also belongs on the list when burning comes with a discharge.[7][19]

If there is any chance of sexual exposure, a new partner, discharge, pelvic pain, or bleeding between periods, tell the clinician so STI testing can be included rather than assuming a simple bladder infection.[14][17]

Burning Urination in Men vs Women

The same symptom has a different set of likely causes depending on sex. Females have a short urethra located close to the anus, so bacteria can more easily enter the urinary tract, and bladder infections are more common in women than in men; urinary tract infections are the most common bacterial infections in women.[20][3] In men with urinary symptoms, clinicians should also consider urethritis and prostatitis, and urine culture results guide the choice of antibiotic.[4] When urethritis is possible, men are tested for chlamydia and gonorrhea, with urine as the preferred specimen.[17]

This is why the clinical approach differs. Women with dysuria who have no complicating features can be treated for cystitis without further testing, while male sex is one of the risk factors for a complicated urinary tract infection, and complicating features warrant a urinalysis and urine culture.[15]

Other Causes: Interstitial Cystitis, Stones, Irritants, and Medications

When urine testing is negative and symptoms persist, other causes move up the list. Interstitial cystitis, also called bladder pain syndrome, is a chronic disorder of bladder or pelvic pain, pressure, or discomfort associated with urinary frequency and a strong urge to urinate. The American Urological Association guideline says the diagnosis requires a careful history, physical examination, and laboratory testing to exclude infections or other confusable disorders, with symptoms present for at least six weeks and documented negative urine cultures.[9]

Kidney stones can also cause burning. Symptoms of a kidney stone include pain while urinating, sharp pain in the back, side, lower abdomen, or groin, blood in the urine, and a constant need to urinate.[10]

Chemical irritants are an easily overlooked cause. Irritation of the vaginal tissue from bubble bath, perfumes, or lotions can cause painful urination, and bladder irritants and some medications can also cause dysuria without any infection.[16][14][15]

When Is Burning Urination a Sign of a Kidney Infection?

Burning urination by itself points to the lower urinary tract, but when it is joined by systemic symptoms the infection may have moved upward to the kidneys. Symptoms of pyelonephritis, or kidney infection, include fever and chills, pain in the back, side, or groin, nausea or vomiting, and frequent, painful urination.[21] Acute pyelonephritis should be suspected in people with flank pain and laboratory evidence of a urinary tract infection. Outpatient treatment is appropriate for uncomplicated cases in people who can tolerate oral antibiotics; patients admitted to the hospital receive intravenous antibiotics, and pregnant patients with pyelonephritis should be admitted because of their elevated risk of severe complications.[23][2]

The key distinction is severity. A bladder infection is uncomfortable but generally uncomplicated; a kidney infection is a systemic illness that, although uncommon, can lead to sepsis. Fever, shaking chills, flank pain, vomiting, or feeling markedly unwell with urinary symptoms should prompt urgent in-person evaluation rather than waiting.[21][22]

How Is the Cause Diagnosed?

Diagnosis starts with the history and a focused exam, then uses targeted testing. One of the most useful questions is whether there is vaginal discharge or irritation, because these were the two most powerful findings for lowering the likelihood of a UTI.[1] Most people with dysuria should have a urinalysis, which looks for white blood cells, red blood cells, bacteria, and chemicals such as nitrites; a urine culture identifies the organism and guides antibiotic choice, especially for recurrent or suspected complicated infection.[14][22]

When an STI is possible, nucleic acid amplification testing on urine or a swab detects chlamydia and gonorrhea.[5][6][17] For suspected vaginitis, a vaginal pH test and a wet mount distinguish yeast from bacterial vaginosis.[7][8]

What Relieves Burning Urination?

Relief depends on the cause, which is why identifying it matters. For a bacterial UTI, antibiotics are the treatment; guidelines name nitrofurantoin, fosfomycin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (where local resistance does not exceed 20 percent) as first-line options for uncomplicated cystitis.[2][3] For a yeast infection, a single 150 mg oral dose of fluconazole or a vaginal azole such as clotrimazole or miconazole is recommended.[8] For bacterial vaginosis, the recommended regimens are oral metronidazole, metronidazole vaginal gel, or clindamycin vaginal cream.[7] For chlamydia and gonorrhea, doxycycline and ceftriaxone are used as described above.[5][6]

Phenazopyridine, sold over the counter under brands such as AZO and also available by prescription, has a pain-relieving effect on the lining of the urinary tract and relieves pain, burning, urgency, and frequency, but it is a symptom reliever, not a treatment, so the cause still needs prompt treatment.[12][13] It turns urine reddish-orange. The over-the-counter label says not to use it for more than 2 days without consulting a doctor, and when it is taken with an antibiotic for a UTI, the prescription label limits it to 2 days.[12][13]

While waiting for treatment to work, stop using bubble bath, perfumes, or lotions that may be irritating the area.[16]

When to Get Urgent Care

Go to the emergency room for visible blood in the urine or any burning with urination during pregnancy (Dr. Bhavsar's cautious standard for these guides). Get same-day or urgent in-person care for fever, chills, flank or upper-back pain, nausea or vomiting, inability to keep fluids down, severe pain, or symptoms that are worsening despite treatment. These can signal pyelonephritis or another condition that needs prompt evaluation.[21][22][10][23]

Pregnancy lowers the threshold for action, and these guides advise the emergency room for painful urination in pregnancy. Pregnant people with any painful urination should be evaluated promptly,[16] because pyelonephritis in pregnancy is associated with complications such as septicemia, low birth weight, and preterm birth, and pregnant patients with pyelonephritis are at significantly elevated risk of severe complications.[11][23] Screening for asymptomatic bacteriuria with urine culture is recommended during pregnancy.[11]

Also seek evaluation if painful urination lasts more than a day,[16] if symptoms do not resolve or come back within two to four weeks after finishing treatment, or if the symptoms are not typical of a simple bladder infection.[3] Men with urinary symptoms should be evaluated, with a urine culture to guide treatment.[4]

When Should a UTI Send You to the ER?

Go to the emergency room or call 911 for any of these:

  • Fever with shaking chills and pain in your back or side
  • Vomiting that keeps you from holding down fluids or medicine
  • Confusion, a racing heart, fast breathing, or feeling faint
  • Visible blood in your urine
  • Any urinary symptoms while you are pregnant

Get in-person care the same day for fever on its own, back or side pain on its own, urinary symptoms in a man, symptoms that are not improving 48 hours after starting an antibiotic, or symptoms that come back soon after treatment.

This red-flag list is the same on every UTI guide on this site. Sending visible blood in the urine and urinary symptoms in pregnancy to the emergency room is Dr. Bhavsar's deliberately cautious standard for these guides.
