Pre-Travel Medical Visit: When to Book, What to Bring and Typhoid Vaccines

An adult guide to what a pre-travel visit covers, how far ahead to book, what to bring, travel vaccines, and how the oral and injectable typhoid vaccines compare.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MDLast reviewed November 22, 202612 min read

What does a pre-travel medical visit cover, and which typhoid vaccine fits?

A pre-travel visit reviews your health and itinerary and plans vaccines, medicines and prevention advice; CDC advises booking at least 4 to 6 weeks before departure, though it is never too late. Typhoid vaccine is recommended for travel to areas with recognized risk, highest in South Asia. The oral vaccine is 4 capsules every other day, finished at least 1 week before exposure; the shot is one injection at least 2 weeks before travel. Both protect 50% to 80% of people.[4][3][2]

If you get a fever and feel very ill during or after international travel, see a doctor. Tell the doctor where and when you traveled.[5] Breathing problems, confusion or fainting are emergency warning signs: call 911 or go to an emergency department.[12]

Key Takeaways

  • CDC advises booking a pre-travel appointment at least 4 to 6 weeks before an international trip, because some vaccines need more than one dose.[4]
  • The CDC Yellow Book says it is never too late for a pre-travel consultation, even when departure is days or hours away.[3]
  • Two typhoid vaccines are licensed in the United States: an oral capsule vaccine for people 6 and older and an injectable vaccine for people 2 and older.[2]
  • Typhoid vaccines protect 50% to 80% of people who get them, so safe food and water habits still matter after vaccination.[2]
  • The oral typhoid vaccine is 4 capsules taken every other day, and the course should be finished at least 1 week before possible exposure.[2]

What is a pre-travel medical visit, and who needs one?

A pre-travel medical visit is an appointment to plan for the health risks of one specific trip. The clinician assesses your risk, explains the likely hazards and manages them with vaccines, medicines and prevention advice.[1]

The CDC Yellow Book says a consultation with a travel medicine specialist is indicated for all international travelers.[1] It calls this particularly important for people with a complicated health history, pregnant travelers, people taking special risks such as high-altitude travel, and people with long or complicated itineraries.[1]

Travelers visiting friends and relatives are at higher risk of typhoid, partly because they may be less careful with food and water and may skip pre-travel care and vaccination.[2]

How far in advance should you see a travel doctor?

CDC advises an appointment at least 4 to 6 weeks before you leave. Some vaccines need more than one dose, so it is best to start as soon as possible.[4]

For typhoid specifically, CDC's traveler page says to see a doctor or travel clinic at least 4 weeks before you travel, and lists at least 2 weeks before travel as the timing for either typhoid vaccine.[5] The Yellow Book and the oral vaccine's label set the capsule course's own deadline: finish all 4 capsules at least 1 week before possible exposure.[2][7]

Leaving soon? A visit is still worth it

The CDC Yellow Book says it is never too late for a pre-travel consultation. It defines last-minute travelers as people leaving on short notice, typically within 2 weeks, and says they should still get a full assessment, all indicated vaccines as time permits and focused counseling.[3]

Even when departure is imminent, clinicians can still give prevention counseling by telehealth or secure messaging.[3] People with a weakened immune system are the exception to last-minute planning: the Yellow Book advises them to seek pre-travel care 3 to 4 months before the trip, because vaccine timing needs special consideration.[3]

What happens at a pre-travel visit?

The clinician reviews your health background and the trip itself, including the itinerary, trip length, purpose and planned activities, and matches vaccines, medicines and advice to those risks.[1] A typical pre-travel consultation does not include a physical exam.[1]

Topics a visit usually covers:

  • Vaccines: catching up on routine vaccines and choosing travel vaccines for the destination.[1]
  • Malaria: for malaria areas, how it spreads, mosquito bite prevention, prevention pills, symptoms and the need for prompt care if symptoms start.[1]
  • Self-treatment plans: medicines to carry for common problems such as travelers' diarrhea, altitude illness and motion sickness.[1]
  • Medicine review: checking your current medicines for interactions with travel medicines.[1]
  • Everyday risks: injury, food and water infections, insect-borne disease, respiratory and sexually transmitted infections, and how to get travel medical insurance.[1]

What should you bring to a pre-travel appointment?

Bring the details the clinician will ask about: your vaccination history, current medicines and allergies, medical conditions, and a trip plan with the countries and regions in order, dates, accommodations and activities.[1]

  • Vaccine records, including routine and past travel vaccines.[1]
  • A medicine and allergy list, plus pregnancy or breastfeeding status.[1]
  • Your itinerary: countries and regions in order, layovers, rural or urban stays, trip length and the reason for travel.[1]
  • Planned activities such as diving, high-elevation travel or animal contact.[1]

What to pack for the trip itself

CDC suggests carrying copies of your immunization records, prescriptions and health insurance documents, plus proof of yellow fever vaccination if your trip requires it.[6] Keep medicines in carry-on luggage in their original prescription bottles, with copies of the written prescriptions.[1]

A basic travel health kit can include pain and fever medicine, diarrhea medicine, insect repellent with DEET or picaridin, sunscreen, hand sanitizer with at least 60% alcohol, and any travel medicines your clinician prescribes.[6] Some countries restrict which medicines you can bring, so CDC suggests checking with the US Embassy for your destination.[6]

Which travel vaccines might you need?

It depends on your destination within a country, the purpose of travel, the season and how long you stay.[1] The visit is also a chance to update routine vaccines; for example, the Yellow Book recommends the current-season flu vaccine for all travelers and measles-mumps-rubella vaccine for travelers without documented immunity.[1]

  • Hepatitis A: one of the most common vaccine-preventable diseases acquired during travel.[1]
  • Typhoid: highest risk for travelers to South Asia, including Bangladesh, Pakistan and India.[1]
  • Yellow fever: risk in parts of sub-Saharan Africa and the Amazon region of South America, and some countries require proof of vaccination for entry.[1] Only authorized yellow fever vaccine centers can give it.[4]
  • Others by itinerary: CDC's list of possible travel vaccines also includes cholera, Japanese encephalitis, meningococcal, polio and rabies vaccines.[4]

Proof of yellow fever vaccination becomes valid 10 days after the dose, so a late booking can affect entry to countries that require it.[3] Check CDC's page for your destination to see which vaccines and medicines it suggests.[4]

Do you need a typhoid vaccine?

You likely do if you are going to an area with a recognized typhoid risk. CDC recommends typhoid vaccine for travelers 2 years and older going to those areas, and the risk is greatest in South Asia.[2]

Typhoid fever is a bacterial infection spread through food or water contaminated by the feces of an infected person or a carrier.[2] About 85% of typhoid cases in the United States occur in international travelers, and more than 70% of those follow travel to South Asia, mainly Bangladesh, India and Pakistan.[2]

Short trips are not risk-free: travelers have caught typhoid on visits of less than 1 week to Bangladesh, India and Pakistan.[2] CDC says all travelers to Pakistan are at risk of extensively drug-resistant typhoid fever, which does not respond to most antibiotics used to treat the disease.[5]

Routine typhoid vaccination is not recommended in the United States. It is recommended selectively for travelers to areas with a recognized risk, household contacts of a typhoid carrier and laboratory workers who handle the bacteria.[7] On CDC's typhoid vaccine map, Mexico is shaded as a country where the vaccine is advised, while most of Europe and the rest of North America are not.[2]

Oral typhoid vaccine vs the typhoid shot: which is better?

Neither is clearly better for every traveler. Both protect 50% to 80% of people who get them, and they differ in age limits, how far ahead they must be given, how long they last and who should not take them.[2]

In a Cochrane review of trials done almost entirely in typhoid-endemic countries, three doses of the oral vaccine probably prevented around half of typhoid cases over about 3 years (50% cumulative efficacy, moderate-certainty evidence), and one dose of the injectable vaccine prevented around two-thirds in the first year (69%, high-certainty evidence).[10] The review had no data on travelers, and the US oral schedule uses 4 capsules rather than the 3 tested in those trials.[10][2]

Which typhoid vaccine needs an in-person visit?

The oral vaccine is 4 capsules you swallow yourself on alternate days, while the injectable vaccine is a shot into the muscle, so it has to be given in person.[7][2] Because the shot is a single dose, the Yellow Book lists injectable typhoid vaccine among the single-dose vaccines that can start protection for last-minute travelers.[3]

How do you take the oral typhoid vaccine?

Take 1 capsule every other day, for example on days 1, 3, 5 and 7, about 1 hour before a meal with a cold or lukewarm drink no warmer than body temperature.[7]

  • Swallow each capsule whole; do not chew it.[2][7]
  • Keep the capsules refrigerated, not frozen, and put unused capsules back in the refrigerator between doses.[2][7]
  • Take each capsule at least 2 hours after a previous meal, with liquid no warmer than 98.6°F.[2]
  • Avoid alcohol from 1 hour before to 2 hours after each capsule, because alcohol can break down the capsule's coating.[2]
  • Take all 4 capsules and finish at least 1 week before possible exposure; people who do not complete the course might not get full protection.[2]

What if you miss a dose?

There is no established rule. The Yellow Book says the approach to a missed or late dose is undefined; some experts think a dose 1 day late may not matter, but no studies show this, so ask the prescriber.[2]

Antibiotics, malaria pills and other vaccines

Antibiotics may keep the live oral vaccine from working.[7] Wait more than 72 hours after any antibiotic before starting the capsules, and avoid antibiotics until 72 hours after the last capsule.[2]

Atovaquone-proguanil (Malarone) should be taken only if 10 days or more have passed since the last capsule, while mefloquine and chloroquine can be taken with the vaccine.[2] If you will take atovaquone-proguanil for malaria, finish the last capsule at least 10 days before your first dose of it.[2] Our malaria prevention pills guide covers the pill options.

The oral vaccine can be taken at the same time as, or at any interval from, live virus vaccines such as measles-mumps-rubella or yellow fever.[2]

What are the side effects of typhoid vaccines, and who should not get them?

Side effects of the oral vaccine are rare and mainly stomach discomfort, diarrhea, fever, headache, nausea, vomiting and rash. With the shot they are mainly headache, injection-site reactions, fever and general discomfort.[2]

In the label's safety data from 483 people who took 3 doses of the oral vaccine, the most common symptoms were abdominal pain (6.4%), nausea (5.8%) and headache (4.8%), and in the placebo-controlled trial only nausea was significantly more common than with placebo.[7] The label says reactions reported after marketing have been infrequent and mild.[7]

Who should not take the oral vaccine

  • People with a fever or an acute stomach illness at the time.[2]
  • Pregnant people.[2]
  • People with a weakened immune system, including people with HIV or those taking medicines that suppress the immune system.[2][7]
  • People who took an antibiotic within the past 72 hours.[2]
  • People allergic to any part of the vaccine or its capsule.[7]

For immunocompromised travelers, the Yellow Book calls the shot a theoretically safer alternative, and it says the shot can be considered in pregnancy when the benefits outweigh the risks.[2] Household contacts of immunocompromised people can take the oral vaccine; spread of the vaccine bacteria to others has not been documented.[2] The only contraindication to the shot is a history of a severe reaction to a previous dose, and like the oral vaccine it should not be given during an acute fever.[2]

Is there a typhoid vaccine shortage?

FDA does not list one. FDA's list of current shortages of vaccines and other biologics did not include either typhoid vaccine when checked on November 21, 2026.[8]

FDA lists one earlier interruption of the oral vaccine as resolved: in January 2021 its US maker paused manufacture and distribution because the COVID-19 pandemic cut international travel, and FDA marks that shortage resolved in June 2022.[9] An FDA list does not show local stock, so it is worth calling the pharmacy or clinic before you go.

Does a typhoid vaccine protect you completely?

No. Typhoid vaccines protect 50% to 80% of people who get them, a large dose of bacteria can overwhelm vaccine protection, and no vaccine prevents paratyphoid fever, so food and water precautions still matter.[2]

  • Wash hands with soap and water, especially after using the bathroom and before eating, or use hand sanitizer with at least 60% alcohol if soap and water are not available.[5]
  • Drink unopened, factory-sealed bottled water, or boil water for at least 1 minute.[5]
  • Avoid ice unless it is made from sealed bottled water or boiled water.[5]
  • Eat well-cooked food that is still hot and steaming, and avoid raw fruits and vegetables that cannot be peeled.[5]
  • Avoid food and drinks from street vendors unless they are steaming hot.[5]

What other medicines might you plan for at the visit?

Depending on the trip, the visit may also cover malaria prevention pills, medicines for altitude illness and motion sickness, and a plan for travelers' diarrhea.[1]

For travelers' diarrhea, the Yellow Book says most symptoms can be managed with fluids plus loperamide or bismuth subsalicylate, and clinicians may consider an antibiotic to carry for incapacitating diarrhea.[1] Seek medical care if symptoms last 24 to 36 hours or come with high fever, dehydration or bloody diarrhea.[1]

For more detail, see our guides on malaria prevention pills, altitude sickness prevention and motion sickness treatment.

The Yellow Book advises bringing quality medicines from home, because substandard and counterfeit drugs are sold in pharmacies in some countries.[1]

What can a video visit cover, and when do you need in-person or emergency care?

A video visit can cover the conversation at the center of a pre-travel consultation, which usually involves no physical exam: your history, itinerary, risks, travel medicines and prevention plan.[1] The Yellow Book notes that clinics can offer telemedicine to accommodate last-minute travelers.[3]

Because the oral typhoid vaccine is capsules you take yourself, a video visit can also cover a prescription for it when it is appropriate.[7]

Vaccines given by injection need an in-person visit, and yellow fever vaccine is given only at a vaccine center authorized to give it.[4] A separate appointment might be needed to assess fitness for travel.[1]

Fever during or after travel

See a doctor if you have a fever and feel very ill, and tell the doctor where and when you traveled.[5] Typhoid symptoms usually start 6 to 30 days after exposure, with tiredness and a fever that climbs each day to about 102 to 104°F by the third or fourth day.[2] Serious complications, including life-threatening intestinal bleeding and perforation, occur in 10% to 15% of people hospitalized with typhoid.[2]

After travel to a malaria area, symptoms can start as late as a year or more after infection, and CDC advises travelers with malaria symptoms to see a healthcare provider as soon as possible, even if still traveling.[11]

Call 911 or go to an emergency department

Breathing problems, confusion or another change in mental status, fainting, severe abdominal pain, severe or persistent vomiting or diarrhea, and coughing up or vomiting blood are warning signs of a medical emergency in adults.[12] Call 911 when a condition is life threatening or could become life threatening on the way to the hospital.[12]

This guide is for adults 18 and older. It does not cover diagnosis or treatment of typhoid or other illness after travel, which needs in-person testing such as a blood culture.[2]
