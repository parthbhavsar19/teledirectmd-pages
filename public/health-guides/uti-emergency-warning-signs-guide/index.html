When Is a UTI an Emergency? Kidney Infection and Red Flags

Learn the warning signs a urinary tract infection has reached the kidneys, when fever and flank pain mean the emergency room, and how to tell a bladder infection from a kidney infection.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed August 30, 2026
13 min read

What are the warning signs a UTI has reached the kidneys?

A UTI has likely reached the kidneys when fever, chills, flank or back pain, nausea or vomiting, and a feeling of being systemically unwell join the usual urinary symptoms. Those are the classic signs of a kidney infection (pyelonephritis), and they call for prompt medical evaluation. The 2025 IDSA guideline on complicated UTI states that systemic signs such as fever suggest the infection has extended beyond the bladder, and it classifies pyelonephritis as a complicated UTI. Seek urgent or emergency care for those symptoms regardless of what a home dipstick shows, because a urine strip detects markers of inflammation and bacteria but cannot tell whether an infection is confined to the bladder or has moved into the kidneys. Pregnancy, immune suppression, older age, and inability to keep fluids down all lower the threshold for urgent assessment.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated August 30, 2026.

Bladder Infection vs. Kidney Infection at a Glance

Most urinary tract infections are bladder infections (cystitis), which typically cause painful or burning urination and frequency, sometimes with tenderness over the lower belly.[1] A kidney infection is more serious: the infection moves up from the bladder to one or both kidneys and can cause fever, chills, and nausea or vomiting.[2][3]

Key Takeaways

  • Fever, chills, flank or back pain, nausea or vomiting, and feeling systemically unwell are the classic signs that a UTI has reached the kidneys.[3][6]
  • A home dipstick tests only for leukocyte esterase and nitrite, markers of inflammation and bacteria, so it cannot tell a bladder infection from a kidney infection; that is judged from signs such as fever and flank pain.[7][4]
  • Seek urgent or emergency care for kidney-infection warning signs regardless of whether a home test is positive or negative.[3][6]
  • A kidney infection can lead to sepsis, which is uncommon but is a life-threatening medical emergency.[3][8]
  • Older adults may show confusion or other mental changes instead of classic fever and pain, which can delay recognition.[6][9]
  • Urinary symptoms in men, pregnancy, immune suppression, and inability to keep fluids down all lower the threshold for urgent evaluation.[5][6][10]

What Does a Kidney Infection Feel Like?

A kidney infection usually starts as a bladder infection that spreads upward, and it often feels like a whole-body illness. Typical symptoms include fever and chills, pain in the back, side, or groin, frequent or painful urination, and nausea or vomiting.[2][3] MedlinePlus lists fever above 101°F (38.3°C), chills and shaking or night sweats, and fatigue and a general ill feeling among the signs that a UTI has spread to the kidneys.[6]

The pain is a key clue. Kidney infection pain is felt in the back, side, or groin,[3] while a bladder infection more often causes tenderness over the lower belly.[1] Flank pain together with signs of a urinary infection is the pattern that should make a clinician suspect a kidney infection.[5]

Not every kidney infection announces itself loudly. Fever is typically present in kidney infection but is not universal.[5] In older people, mental changes or confusion are often the only signs of a UTI,[6] and many older adults do not have the usual urinary symptoms.[9] That is one reason infection in older adults is easy to miss and why a change in mental status should be taken seriously.

Fever and Flank Pain: When to Go to the ER

Fever with flank pain is the combination that most strongly suggests a kidney infection, and it is a reason to seek same-day care. The 2025 IDSA guideline on complicated UTI states that systemic signs such as fever suggest the infection has extended beyond the bladder, and it classifies kidney infection (pyelonephritis) as a complicated UTI.[4] Flank pain with signs of a urinary infection should raise suspicion of kidney infection,[5] and NIDDK advises seeking care right away for kidney infection symptoms.[3]

Go to the emergency room rather than waiting when any of these are present:

  • Fever with shaking chills and flank or back pain[6][3]
  • Nausea or vomiting that prevents keeping fluids or oral medicine down[6][5]
  • Signs of sepsis, such as confusion, fast heart rate, fast breathing, or feeling faint[8][10]
  • Pregnancy with fever or flank pain,[5][6] or any other urinary symptoms during pregnancy
  • Visible blood in the urine
  • Severe pain or inability to urinate

Outpatient treatment is appropriate for uncomplicated kidney infection in people who can tolerate oral medicine,[5] so a person who can keep fluids down, has a fever but is otherwise stable, and can be seen the same day may be managed urgently rather than in the emergency department, but this decision should be made with a clinician. When in doubt, err toward a higher level of care, especially for older adults, pregnant people, and anyone with a weakened immune system.[6][11]

Do not wait for a home test result. Fever, chills, flank pain, vomiting, or feeling very unwell are reasons to seek urgent or emergency care on their own. A dipstick cannot confirm or rule out a kidney infection, and a negative strip should never delay care for these symptoms.[3][12][7]

Can a UTI Cause Sepsis?

Yes. The urinary tract is one of the places where infections that lead to sepsis most often start. Sepsis is the body's extreme response to an infection and a life-threatening medical emergency.[8] A kidney infection leading to sepsis is uncommon, but it can be life threatening.[3] Sepsis can progress to septic shock, a medical emergency in which blood pressure falls.[11]

Warning signs of sepsis include confusion or disorientation, shortness of breath, a high heart rate or weak pulse, fever, shivering or feeling very cold, extreme pain or discomfort, and clammy or sweaty skin.[8] Lightheadedness from low blood pressure, mottled skin,[10] and little or no urine[11] can also signal sepsis or septic shock. These symptoms, especially in someone with a known or suspected UTI, warrant immediate emergency care.[8]

Septic shock occurs most often in the very old and the very young, and it may also occur in people with weakened immune systems; urinary catheters are among its risk factors.[11] MedlinePlus also lists older age, diabetes, kidney stones, and an enlarged prostate or anything that blocks the flow of urine among UTI risk factors.[6] When diagnosed early and treated properly, most kidney infections do not lead to complications.[2]

UTI Red Flags in Older Adults

Older adults often do not show the textbook signs of a kidney infection. Many do not present with localized urinary symptoms; instead they may have confusion or other changes in mental status, or generalized symptoms such as lower abdominal pain, back pain, or constipation.[9][6] This is sometimes called an atypical presentation, and it is a major reason UTIs in older adults are both common and easy to miss.

At the same time, older adults have a high rate of asymptomatic bacteriuria, meaning bacteria in the urine without any symptoms. For that reason, a positive urine test, cloudy urine, or urine odor alone does not establish a UTI in an older person. The evaluation should focus on new symptoms and systemic changes, not on a screening test result.[13][9]

Red flags that should prompt urgent evaluation in an older adult include new confusion, a fall, fever, shaking chills, flank or back pain, vomiting, a drop in blood pressure, or a significant change from their usual baseline.[6][3][10] Because these signs overlap with many other illnesses, a clinician should sort out the cause rather than assuming a UTI from a positive strip alone. Falls, for example, are a common reason UTI is suspected in nursing home residents, but they have not been shown to be significantly associated with bacteria plus inflammation in the urine.[9]

UTI Warning Signs in Men

Kidney infections are more common in females than in males.[2] Under the 2025 IDSA guideline, both men and women can have an uncomplicated UTI, but acute prostatitis should be considered and excluded before a man's UTI is classified as uncomplicated.[4] A man with urinary symptoms such as burning, frequency, urgency, or new difficulty urinating should be evaluated clinically, because the cause can include prostate infection, an enlarged prostate, a kidney stone, or a structural problem, not just a simple bladder infection.[14][6]

In older men, the same warning signs that apply to older adults generally also apply: fever, chills, flank pain, confusion, and systemic illness all point to a possible upper-tract or prostate infection that needs prompt care. Acute bacterial prostatitis can start quickly with chills, fever, difficulty starting to urinate or emptying the bladder, and pain in the lower back or the area between the genitals and anus (perineum), and its possible complications include a prostate abscess and sepsis.[14] A man with fever, chills, and difficulty urinating, or with pain in the lower back or perineum, should seek urgent evaluation.

Male sex is often considered a complicating factor in kidney infection because of possible prostate involvement and the need for longer antibiotic treatment,[5] and the 2025 IDSA guideline notes that men with a febrile UTI in whom acute bacterial prostatitis is suspected may benefit from a longer course (10 to 14 days).[15] Urine culture is necessary for complicated UTIs.[16] A home dipstick is not a substitute for that evaluation.

When Is a Home Test Not Enough?

A home UTI test strip detects two urine markers, leukocyte esterase and nitrite, which suggest inflammation and certain bacteria.[7] It does not identify the organism or show which antibiotics will work; for suspected kidney infection, urine culture with susceptibility testing is recommended in all patients.[5] A strip also cannot show where the infection is located or distinguish a bladder infection from a kidney infection. Spread beyond the bladder is judged clinically, from signs such as fever, flank pain, and systemic illness, not from a strip.[4][5]

A home test is not enough in these situations:

Even a negative strip should not reassure someone with classic or worsening symptoms. In a review of women with UTI symptoms, history, examination, and dipstick testing could not reliably lower the probability of infection enough to rule out a UTI.[12] A meta-analysis found that a strip negative for both nitrite and leukocyte esterase is useful for excluding infection in many groups, but negative predictive values were lower in family medicine settings.[7] Kidney-infection warning signs need care right away whatever the strip shows.[3]

What Happens if a Kidney Infection Is Untreated?

An untreated kidney infection can progress in several ways. Although uncommon, it can lead to sepsis, which can be life threatening.[3] Infection can also form a perirenal abscess, a pocket of pus around one or both kidneys; most are caused by a urinary tract infection that starts in the bladder, and the pus may need to be drained through a catheter placed through the skin or with surgery.[18] In rare cases, kidney infections can cause kidney scarring that can lead to chronic kidney disease, as well as high blood pressure or kidney failure.[2]

In pregnancy, kidney infection is associated with complications including septicemia, respiratory distress, low birth weight, and spontaneous preterm birth,[17] and pregnant patients with kidney infection are at significantly elevated risk of severe complications and should be admitted to the hospital for initial treatment.[5] That is why fever or flank pain in pregnancy is treated as urgent.

The good news is that prompt treatment is highly effective. Kidney infection responds to appropriate therapy within 48 to 72 hours in more than 95% of cases,[5] and when diagnosed early and treated properly, most kidney infections do not lead to complications.[2] The key is not to wait out fever, flank pain, or vomiting at home.

When to Get Urgent Care vs. the Emergency Room

The choice between urgent care and the emergency room comes down to how sick the person is and what they need. Urgent care can evaluate and treat many uncomplicated urinary symptoms, but a suspected kidney infection with significant systemic symptoms often belongs in the emergency department, where IV antibiotics, fluids, blood tests, and imaging are immediately available.[6][5]

SettingGood forNot ideal for
Urgent careBurning, frequency, urgency without fever; mild symptoms in an otherwise healthy adult; same-day evaluation when a primary clinician is unavailableHigh fever with flank pain, vomiting, confusion, or signs of sepsis[3]
Emergency roomFever with flank pain, vomiting, pregnancy with fever, older adults with confusion or decline, any signs of sepsis, inability to keep fluids down[5][6][8]; also any urinary symptoms in pregnancy and visible blood in the urineRoutine, mild bladder symptoms that can be managed as an outpatient

As a practical rule, if the person has fever plus flank pain, cannot keep fluids down, is pregnant, has visible blood in the urine, is older with confusion or a fall, or shows any sign of sepsis, the emergency room is the safer choice. If symptoms are limited to the bladder and the person is otherwise well, urgent care or a same-day clinical visit is often appropriate.[5][6]

Pregnancy note: Fever or flank pain in pregnancy should be evaluated promptly,[5] and pregnant people should not rely on a home dipstick for urinary symptoms. The USPSTF recommends screening pregnant persons for asymptomatic bacteriuria using urine culture,[17] and these guides advise the emergency room for any urinary symptoms during pregnancy.

When Should a UTI Send You to the ER?

Go to the emergency room or call 911 for any of these:

  • Fever with shaking chills and pain in your back or side
  • Vomiting that keeps you from holding down fluids or medicine
  • Confusion, a racing heart, fast breathing, or feeling faint
  • Visible blood in your urine
  • Any urinary symptoms while you are pregnant

Get in-person care the same day for fever on its own, back or side pain on its own, urinary symptoms in a man, symptoms that are not improving 48 hours after starting an antibiotic, or symptoms that come back soon after treatment.

This red-flag list is the same on every UTI guide on this site. Sending visible blood in the urine and urinary symptoms in pregnancy to the emergency room is Dr. Bhavsar's deliberately cautious standard for these guides.
