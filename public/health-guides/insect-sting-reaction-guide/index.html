Bee and Wasp Sting Treatment: The First 48 Hours

A physician's evidence-based guide to local swelling, first aid, and the symptoms that need emergency care.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MDLast reviewed October 15, 202625 min read

How do you treat a bee or wasp sting in the first 48 hours?

A bee or wasp sting has 3 main reaction patterns: a small local reaction, a large local reaction, or a systemic allergic reaction. For symptoms confined to the sting area, remove a visible stinger promptly, wash the skin, and use a cloth-wrapped cold pack. Large local swelling may increase for 24 to 48 hours and usually subsides over 3 to 10 days; swelling alone does not prove infection. Trouble breathing, throat or tongue swelling, faintness, or rapidly developing symptoms away from the sting require emergency action. Use your prescribed epinephrine according to your action plan and call 911; do not wait for a video visit or an antihistamine. See the US stinging-insect practice parameter, CDC first aid, and World Allergy Organization emergency guidance.

Emergency symptoms come first. Trouble breathing, tongue or throat swelling, faintness, collapse, or rapidly developing systemic symptoms after a sting requires emergency action. Use prescribed epinephrine as directed and call 911. Do not wait for a video visit.[1][2][4]

Key Takeaways

  • Where symptoms appear matters more than the size of a red patch. Swelling contiguous with the sting is a local reaction; symptoms away from the sting point to a systemic reaction.[1]
  • A large local reaction can keep increasing in size for 24 to 48 hours. That swelling is usually caused by mast cell mediators, not infection, so it is not by itself a reason to start antibiotics.[1][9]
  • Cold compresses, antihistamines and pain relievers might help comfort. The evidence behind them is limited, and controlled-study proof that oral steroids work is lacking.[1][7]
  • Epinephrine is the first-line treatment for anaphylaxis. Antihistamines have only a limited role and should not delay it.[2][4]
  • After a systemic sting reaction, emergency treatment comes first, then referral to an allergist. Venom testing and venom immunotherapy decisions belong with the specialist.[1][4]

How do you treat a bee or wasp sting?

Wasp sting treatment: what should you do first?

First decide whether the symptoms are only at the sting site. If breathing becomes difficult, the throat feels tight, the tongue swells, you feel faint, or symptoms are spreading through the body, stop reading the home-care instructions and take emergency action. Use your prescribed epinephrine according to your emergency plan and call 911. Do not wait for an online appointment.[1][2][4]

For a localized sting, move away from the insects to prevent more stings. Check whether a stinger remains, remove it promptly if visible, and wash with soap and water. Apply a cold pack wrapped in cloth or a clean cloth soaked in cold water, and keep the area raised if you can. These measures are comfort care for pain and swelling.[1][8][10]

Avoid scratching, squeezing, or repeatedly probing the puncture. Avoiding scratching may reduce inflammation and the risk of infection, and squeezing a retained stinger can squeeze more venom out of it.[8][9][10]

Bee sting treatment: what changes if a stinger is left behind?

Honeybees usually leave a barbed stinger and attached venom sac in the skin; other insects, particularly ground-nesting yellow jackets, can also leave stingers. If you see a retained stinger, remove it quickly: the speed of removal appears to matter more than the method. A fingernail can scrape it sideways. Do not delay emergency treatment for a careful skin inspection if systemic symptoms are already present.[1][5][6][10]

The rest of first aid is similar for bees and wasps. An oral antihistamine may help itch and an oral pain reliever may help soreness. A pharmacist can recommend treatments and help check them against your health conditions and other medicines. A sting in the mouth or throat or near the eyes, or symptoms that get worse or are not getting better, call for medical advice rather than home care alone.[1][9][10]

Why does bee sting swelling get worse after 48 hours?

A large local reaction is swelling that extends from the sting site and stays contiguous with it; the US practice parameter describes it as usually an IgE-mediated late-phase reaction. The swelling usually increases 6 to 12 hours after the sting and can keep increasing for 24 to 48 hours. A German review reports that large local reactions occur in up to 25% of the population. A large patch is not automatically anaphylaxis, and the typical course is not a guarantee that swelling on day two is harmless.[1][3]

The reaction can be painful, swollen, red, and itchy. Swelling that spreads directly from a puncture on the hand toward the wrist is not the same pattern as separate hives on the chest or face, which are a sign of a systemic reaction. Where the symptoms are matters more than the color of the patch.[1][7]

Ask what is changing besides the size. Signs of infection such as pus or fluid, rapid or significant worsening, feeling generally unwell, or severe pain out of proportion to the wound need reassessment. Neither a red patch nor a red streak along the skin establishes infection on its own: the US parameter notes that swelling, and even lymphangitic streaks, can come from mast cell mediator release rather than infection.[1][9][10]

Large local reaction: what does “large” mean?

The US practice parameter describes a large local reaction as swelling to more than 10 cm across, contiguous with the sting, that increases in size for 24 to 48 hours. This is a descriptive category, not a home diagnostic test. A smaller sting in the mouth or throat can be more urgent than a much larger patch on the calf, because local swelling there can compromise the airway. Do not wait to reach a size cutoff before seeking help for a risky location or systemic symptoms.[1][9]

How long does a wasp sting last?

A small local reaction causes pain, swelling, and redness that usually last from a few hours to a few days. Larger reactions take longer: the US practice parameter describes large local swelling that subsides over 3 to 10 days.[1]

The useful question is whether the overall course fits a recovering local reaction. A patch that remains visible but is less sore and no longer expanding is different from new severe pain, pus or fluid, or rapidly spreading swelling. If symptoms are getting worse, are not getting better, or you feel unwell, arrange assessment rather than waiting for a particular day on a timeline.[9][10]

NICE advises that skin redness and itching after bites or stings are common and may last for up to 10 days. That is UK guidance for a broader set of bites and stings, not a promise that every US bee sting should be observed at home for that long. Symptoms that get worse or are not getting better deserve review.[9][10]

Take a clear photograph and note when the sting occurred, when swelling began, and whether the area is still changing. This can help a clinician interpret the trajectory, especially if a video visit is being considered for a stable problem. Do not let tracking photographs delay care for shortness of breath, wheeze, noisy breathing, faintness, repeated vomiting with other allergic symptoms, or rapid deterioration.[4][9]

Bee sting allergic reaction: local swelling or anaphylaxis?

“Allergic reaction” is used loosely in everyday speech. Clinically, a large local reaction and anaphylaxis are different problems. A large local reaction remains around and contiguous with the sting. A systemic reaction produces symptoms not contiguous with it, such as generalized hives, swelling at other sites, breathing symptoms, faintness, or gastrointestinal symptoms. The severity of a systemic reaction can change, so it needs prompt assessment.[1][4]

Skin-only systemic reaction: not the same as a local patch

Hives on the trunk after a sting to the ankle are a systemic skin reaction even if the person is breathing normally. The US parameter counts anaphylaxis as hypotension or involvement of at least 2 organ systems, so not every skin-only reaction meets that definition, but it should not be dismissed as a big local reaction. Seek urgent medical guidance, follow an existing allergy action plan, and escalate immediately if airway, breathing, circulation, or other concerning symptoms develop.[1][4]

Anaphylaxis: do not wait for a rash

Anaphylaxis can involve difficulty breathing, wheezing, throat or voice changes, collapse, or reduced circulation. Severe crampy abdominal pain or repetitive vomiting can also be part of anaphylaxis, especially after a non-food trigger such as a sting. Anaphylaxis may occur without typical skin features. Waiting for hives before taking breathing or fainting symptoms seriously is unsafe.[2][4]

Use your prescribed epinephrine promptly when your action plan indicates anaphylaxis, and call 911. Tell the dispatcher that anaphylaxis is suspected and explain what medicine was given. If you do not have epinephrine, call 911 immediately. Do not substitute a borrowed oral medicine, a shower, a steroid cream, or a plan to watch the reaction overnight for emergency care.[1][2][4][12]

Earlier stings without a serious reaction do not guarantee the next one will be mild: the US parameter notes that 50% of fatal sting reactions occur with the first reaction. A prior large local reaction carries a 4% to 10% risk of a systemic reaction to a future sting, and it generally does not mean you need allergy shots. The acute response is based on current symptoms, while long-term risk assessment considers the prior reaction, exposure pattern, medical history, and appropriate specialist testing.[1]

When should you worry about a bee or wasp sting?

The first decision is the care setting, not which cream to buy. Trouble breathing, throat or tongue swelling, a new hoarse voice, collapse, or marked faintness after a sting is an emergency. Generalized hives with breathing, circulation, or severe gastrointestinal symptoms also calls for emergency action. A rapidly evolving reaction should not be managed through asynchronous messages.[1][4]

Mouth, throat, eye, and multiple-sting exposures

A sting inside the mouth or throat is not ordinary home-care territory because local swelling there can compromise the airway. A sting near the eye needs prompt medical advice. Multiple stings can make a systemic reaction more likely. Unusual delayed reactions, mostly toxic, have been reported, including kidney failure with muscle breakdown, and very large numbers of honeybee stings can cause fatal envenomation. A swarm exposure or illness after multiple stings warrants urgent medical evaluation even without a known allergy.[1][6][9][10]

People with a previous systemic sting reaction have about a 50% chance of a systemic reaction to a future sting, so they should follow their established emergency plan and seek help early if symptoms occur. A refill appointment is not a replacement for evaluation after a new reaction. NICE advises considering referral or specialist advice for a severely immunocompromised person with signs of infection, rather than a reflex prescription based on appearance.[1][9][11]

Bee sting vs. wasp sting: does the insect change treatment?

Bees, wasps, hornets, and yellow jackets are all stinging insects, but their behavior and the presence of a retained stinger can differ. Honeybees usually leave a barbed stinger and attached venom sac behind, can sting only once, and, except for Africanized honeybees, are usually nonaggressive away from their hives. Yellow jackets are very aggressive and sting with minimal provocation, and ground-nesting yellow jackets can also leave stingers. These distinctions help with first aid, but the current symptoms determine whether the situation is local, systemic, or an emergency.[1][6]

A retained stinger is a clue, not a perfect insect identification test. People often did not see the insect closely, and a hurried identification may be wrong. You do not need to catch the insect, touch a nest, or risk another sting to make a safe first-aid decision; the US parameter advises having nests near the home removed by trained professionals. This guide is specifically about bee and wasp-type stings; CDC also lists fire ants among stinging insects. Tick bites, mosquito bites, spider bites, and marine stings raise different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable venom exposures. For a suspected tick bite, see the tick-bite treatment guide; do not use bee-stinger advice to remove an attached tick.[8]

For someone with a previous systemic reaction, the identity of the insect becomes more relevant during allergy assessment. Decisions about venom immunotherapy are linked to the clinical history rather than a positive allergy test alone: venom IgE is present in more than 20% of healthy adults, and only 5% to 15% of them will have a systemic reaction to a later sting. For that reason, venom testing cannot be used to screen people who have never had a systemic sting reaction.[1]

How do you remove a bee stinger?

Look for a visible stinger at the puncture after moving to safety. A quick sideways scrape with a fingernail, or the edge of a card if one is already in hand, is a reasonable first step. Wash the skin afterward. If nothing is visible, do not cut, squeeze, or dig at the area trying to find a hidden stinger.[8][10]

CDC and NHS first-aid instructions favor scraping and advise against tweezers or squeezing the stinger. A 1996 experiment, however, found that wheal size increased with delayed removal, even within a few seconds, and did not differ between stings scraped or pinched off after 2 seconds. A 2020 systematic review found two eligible studies, rated the certainty of the evidence very low, and concluded that a retained honeybee stinger should be removed as quickly as possible, with no apparent disadvantage to pinching and pulling.[5][6][8][10]

The practical message is not to leave a visible stinger in place while searching for a credit card. Use a prompt, simple method that you can carry out safely. The evidence is small and concerns venom delivery or local wheal size; the review found no data on whether rapid removal changes the risk of anaphylaxis. The review includes the 1996 experiment, so the two are not independent replications.[5][6]

If you already pulled the stinger out, the available studies do not suggest that prompt pinching was worse than scraping. Focus on current symptoms, wash the area, and continue appropriate first aid. Any systemic symptoms still require the same emergency response regardless of the removal method. A sting near the eye or in the mouth or throat needs medical advice rather than repeated attempts at home removal.[5][6][9][10]

Stinger removal is a small part of care and is not a substitute for epinephrine, the first-line treatment for anaphylaxis. Failing to see a stinger does not make a sting safe: anaphylaxis from wasp stings is not rare, and in central Europe wasps are the leading insect cause of anaphylaxis.[2][3][4]

Which bee sting home remedies help, and which should you avoid?

Cold compresses, washing, and elevation

Basic care is intentionally simple. Wash with soap and water, use a cold pack, and raise the area when practical. The NHS advises an ice pack wrapped in a cloth, or a clean cloth soaked in cold water, for up to 20 minutes if the area is swollen. This is comfort care for pain and swelling.[1][8][10]

Itch and pain relief

An oral antihistamine can be considered for troublesome itch, and an oral pain reliever can help pain. A hydrocortisone skin cream is another commonly used option for local itching and swelling. Follow the product label and pharmacist advice rather than combining several creams or borrowing a strong prescription steroid. These treatments have different purposes and are not interchangeable with an epinephrine auto-injector.[1][4][7][10]

There is an evidence limit here. NICE notes uncertainty about the effectiveness of oral antihistamines for bites and stings, and the US practice parameter assigns its symptomatic-care advice for large local reactions a grade D evidence rating. Symptom care is reasonable, but it should not be advertised as a proven way to shorten every large local reaction or prevent future severe allergy.[1][9]

Baking soda, vinegar, suction, and other “venom removal” claims

Do not rely on baking-soda paste, vinegar, toothpaste, meat tenderizer, or a suction gadget as an emergency or definitive sting treatment. This guide does not endorse the internet rule that bees need an alkali and wasps need an acid: the US parameter, CDC and NHS advice reviewed here focuses instead on removal of a retained stinger, washing, cold, symptom treatment, and triage.[1][8][10]

Avoid scratching and repeated manipulation. If a remedy burns, causes a new rash, or worsens irritation, stop using it and seek appropriate advice. A home mixture can complicate the skin appearance without addressing whether the original reaction is local or systemic. A reaction that is getting worse despite home care deserves reassessment rather than another unverified product.[9][10]

Medicine precautions and when a prescription needs assessment

A medicine that is sold without a prescription can still be the wrong choice for a particular adult. A pharmacist can recommend treatments; tell them about pregnancy, kidney or liver disease, prior medication reactions, other prescribed medicines, and anything already taken for this sting. This guide gives no universal dose because the product and individual safety review matter.[10]

Some antihistamines can make you sleepy, and WAO guidance notes that sedation may be counterproductive in anaphylaxis. Read warnings about driving, alcohol, and other sedating medicines, and do not assume that more sedation means better treatment of the sting. If you are suddenly faint, confused, or unusually drowsy during a developing reaction, do not simply attribute it to an antihistamine and go to sleep: the NHS lists feeling very confused, drowsy or dizzy as a reason to call for emergency help.[4][10]

A topical cream is for the skin, not the eye or inside the mouth. Stings in the mouth or throat or near the eyes need medical advice, and signs of infection such as pus or fluid need a pharmacist or clinician, so ask before treating those areas with a steroid cream. Applying a medicine around a dangerous sting location must not delay the indicated examination.[10]

Oral corticosteroids are sometimes considered by a clinician for a severe large local reaction: the US parameter says a short course may be useful in severe cases, but that definitive proof of efficacy through controlled studies is lacking. They are not a routine requirement for every swollen sting. WAO guidance notes increasing evidence that steroids may be of no benefit in the acute treatment of anaphylaxis, so they are no reason to stay home with emergency symptoms.[1][4]

Do not use leftover antibiotics, an old prednisone pack, or someone else’s auto-injector plan as a self-directed treatment course. If you already have an epinephrine prescription, know the instructions for your particular device and follow the written emergency action plan. Devices differ: the EpiPen label says to hold the device in place for 3 seconds, while the Auvi-Q label says to push until you hear a click and hiss and hold for 2 seconds. Technique should be reviewed with a clinician before an emergency, not improvised from a general article.[1][2][12][13]

Do bee or wasp stings need antibiotics?

Usually not. Rapid redness, warmth, itching, and swelling after a sting are commonly caused by mast cell mediators rather than bacterial infection. NICE says not to offer an antibiotic when there are no symptoms or signs of infection, and the US sting practice parameter says antibiotics are not indicated unless there is clear evidence of secondary infection. Antibiotics do not treat venom-induced inflammation or anaphylaxis.[1][9]

Even a red streak can be caused by noninfectious inflammation after a sting: the US parameter says these reactions do not represent cellulitis even though lymphangitic streaks may be present. A sting is not an indication to take whatever antibiotic remains in a cabinet. For the separate skin-infection problem, see the cellulitis guide.[1]

Severe pain out of proportion to what the skin looks like, rapidly worsening illness, or significant infection in a severely immunocompromised person needs urgent in-person evaluation. These findings should not be routed through a routine same-day prescription pathway. The risk decision comes before the question of which antibiotic might be used.[9]

What should you do if a sting causes anaphylaxis?

Use your prescribed epinephrine immediately according to your device instructions and written action plan, and call 911. Epinephrine injected into the muscle of the outer thigh is the first-line treatment in WAO and US anaphylaxis guidance, and the 2023 US parameter says to give it at the first sign or symptom of suspected anaphylaxis. For people who weigh 30 kg or more, the EpiPen and Auvi-Q labels both list a 0.3 mg auto-injector. Do not wait to see whether an antihistamine, inhaler, steroid tablet, or cold compress works: WAO notes that inhaled bronchodilators are not an alternative to epinephrine and that antihistamines can delay more urgent measures.[2][4][12][13]

Tell the emergency dispatcher about breathing difficulty, throat or tongue swelling, faintness, the time of the sting, and any epinephrine given. If someone is with you, ask them to remain nearby and assist. Do not drive yourself. The EpiPen label says to get emergency medical help right away after using it, and the US sting parameter advises transport to an emergency department, because people with anaphylaxis need observation and further treatment may be needed. The 2023 US parameter says immediate EMS activation may not be required after a prompt, complete and durable response to epinephrine; that is a plan to agree with your clinician in advance, not a judgment to make during a new sting reaction.[1][2][4][12]

Do not stand up suddenly or walk around while faint or unwell. WAO guidance says most people should lie on their back during anaphylaxis; sitting up may help if breathing is difficult, a pregnant person should lie semi-reclined on the left side, and an unconscious person should be placed in the recovery position. Follow dispatcher instructions. A person who is unresponsive or not breathing normally needs dispatcher-guided resuscitation, not an oral medicine or something to drink.[4]

If symptoms persist or recur before help arrives, follow the action plan and dispatcher instructions about another epinephrine dose. The EpiPen label says a second auto-injector can be used if symptoms continue or come back, the Auvi-Q label allows a second dose starting 5 minutes after the first, and both labels say more than two sequential doses should be given under direct medical supervision. This page does not replace the instructions for your own prescribed device. Emergency professionals determine additional treatment and the observation period.[4][12][13]

After recovery, arrange an allergy evaluation to clarify the trigger, review epinephrine technique and the written plan, and consider whether venom immunotherapy is appropriate. For adults with severe sting anaphylaxis, the 2023 US parameter says evaluation for mastocytosis, including a bone marrow biopsy, should be considered. A severe reaction is not finished medically just because the skin looks normal again. New-anaphylaxis investigation and a stable refill of an existing prescription are different kinds of care.[1][2][4][11]

What does the evidence say about sting treatment?

The US stinging-insect practice parameter is titled the 2016 update and was published in a 2017 journal issue. It is not a new 2026 guideline. It supports symptom treatment for large local reactions, epinephrine education and allergist referral after systemic reactions, and selective use of venom testing and immunotherapy. The 2023 US anaphylaxis practice parameter updates emergency anaphylaxis care, including mastocytosis evaluation after severe sting anaphylaxis. A 2012 German review, read here as an abstract only, also separates large local reactions from anaphylaxis.[1][2][3]

The strongest practical consensus is about recognizing anaphylaxis and prioritizing epinephrine, not about which cream makes a hand stop swelling fastest. WAO guidance explains the limited role of antihistamines in anaphylaxis and the growing doubt about routine corticosteroids. Local symptom recommendations rest on low-grade evidence and limited direct studies rather than large, modern comparative trials.[1][2][4][9]

The stinger-removal literature illustrates why recommendations need context. CDC and NHS favor scraping; the experimental study and later systematic review emphasize speed, with no evidence that prompt pinching is worse. The systematic review contains the earlier experiment, so citing both does not create two independent trials. We present the difference openly rather than converting one technique into a false safety rule.[5][6][8][10]

The clinical review from Germany states that the acute symptoms of an insect sting are treated symptomatically; its full text was not readable for this review, so no regimen from it is reproduced here. An individual clinician decides whether an adult’s functional impairment, comorbidities, and examination justify a short course of oral steroids. The absence of a dose table is deliberate: a swelling guide should not become an unsupervised steroid protocol.[1][3]

The 2016 UK nursing review, read here as an abstract only, lists cold packs, antihistamines, analgesia, and topical corticosteroids for local reactions and calls venom immunotherapy the treatment of choice for reducing future anaphylactic reactions in people who have had signs of respiratory obstruction or low blood pressure. It is not adopted here as a set of US rules.[7]

How do you prevent another sting, and who needs an allergist?

Avoid disturbing nests and be cautious when eating or drinking outdoors. Avoid walking outside barefoot or in sandals. CDC advises remaining calm and still if a single stinging insect is flying around, because swatting may cause it to sting. If several insects attack at once, run to get away and go indoors; CDC advises against jumping into water because some insects hover above it.[1][8]

If a nest needs removal, the US parameter advises having it removed by trained professionals rather than handling it yourself. After a systemic reaction, preparedness matters: the parameter advises carrying an epinephrine auto-injector and considering a medical identification bracelet or necklace, and both auto-injector labels advise having two devices available. Check that your devices are in date and make sure the people around you know what to do.[1][12][13]

Local reactions alone: testing and allergy shots are usually not routine

The US parameter says venom immunotherapy is generally not required for people who have had only large local reactions, but might be considered for those with frequent unavoidable exposure. An allergy test can be positive in someone who has no clinically significant systemic sting allergy: venom IgE is found in more than 20% of healthy adults, so testing is interpreted together with the actual reaction history.[1]

Systemic reactions: assessment after emergency care

After a systemic reaction, an allergist can assess the history, determine which testing is appropriate, and discuss future risk and preventive options. The US parameter recommends venom immunotherapy for people who have had an anaphylactic reaction to a sting and have venom-specific IgE. For children and adults whose only systemic symptoms were in the skin, it says immunotherapy is generally not required but may be considered in special circumstances, as a shared decision. Treatment should be given only by experienced staff who can provide emergency care for anaphylaxis, and a positive test alone, without a history of systemic reaction, is not a reason to start it.[1][7]

Keep a record of the reaction: approximate insect appearance if known, sting location, timing, symptoms away from the site, medicines used, and emergency-care records. Do not deliberately expose yourself to another sting to “test” the allergy. Future risk and whether to carry epinephrine after a skin-only systemic reaction require individualized medical advice.[1]

What are your next steps: local care, in-person assessment, or a stable refill?

For a stable adult with only local symptoms, start with appropriate first aid and consider pharmacist or clinician advice for itch and pain. Seek an examination if swelling substantially affects function, symptoms are not improving, infection is suspected, or you are unsure what caused the reaction. A video conversation can review the history, but it cannot substitute for emergency treatment, eye assessment, or a necessary physical examination.[9][10]

Use in-person urgent care or an emergency setting when the location, severity, or uncertainty warrants it. Mouth or throat stings, concerning eye involvement, rapidly worsening symptoms, systemic illness, and multiple-sting exposures are not problems to postpone while seeking a virtual prescription. For suspected anaphylaxis, use the emergency pathway immediately rather than selecting a clinic appointment.[1][9][10]

TeleDirectMD’s directly listed epinephrine auto-injector refill service is a separate, narrow option for adults with an established severe-allergy history and a prior prescription who are stable and otherwise appropriate for refill review. It is not presented here as a dedicated new-sting treatment service. A refill evaluation may review device status and the existing action plan; eligibility is determined individually.[11]

Active anaphylaxis, a recent severe reaction without completed emergency evaluation, a new suspected severe allergy, venom testing, and venom immunotherapy are outside that refill pathway. People with a new systemic reaction need the appropriate emergency and specialist workup. For preparedness and device education, see the epinephrine auto-injector guide.[1][4][11]

This is an adult educational guide, not a pediatric dosing reference, a promise of a prescription, or a replacement for an individualized emergency plan. The practical choice is between safe local symptom care when the pattern fits and the appropriate in-person or emergency assessment when it does not. Neither the size of a patch nor a reassuring photograph should overrule whole-body symptoms.[1][4][9]
