Migraines: Acute Treatment, Prevention Options & CGRP Antibodies Explained

Acute therapy, preventive options, CGRP antibodies, and what the latest AHS/AAN guidelines mean for your care, explained by Dr. Bhavsar.

Written by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: Mar 2026 · Updated: Jul 2026
15 min read

What are the best treatments for migraines, including prevention?

Migraine is the leading cause of disability in people under 50, according to a review of Global Burden of Disease data (Steiner et al., J Headache Pain 2018). Acetaminophen and oral NSAIDs are first-line for mild to moderate attacks, triptans are an effective first-line treatment for moderate to severe attacks, and medication works best when taken early in the attack (Am Fam Physician 2018). Using triptans on 10 or more days a month (ICHD-3 8.2.2), or non-opioid pain relievers on 15 or more days a month (ICHD-3 8.2.3), for more than 3 months can cause medication overuse headache. Preventive therapy should be considered with four or more headaches a month (Am Fam Physician 2019), and the 2024 American Headache Society position statement says CGRP-targeting therapies should be considered first-line for prevention without prior failure of other drug classes (Charles et al., Headache 2024). A sudden thunderclap headache needs emergency evaluation because its most common secondary cause is subarachnoid hemorrhage (Chen and Fuh, Curr Opin Neurol 2021). A licensed clinician, through telehealth or in-person care, can review the symptoms and decide on appropriate therapy.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated July 14, 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • Triptans are an effective first-line treatment for moderate to severe attacks, and acetaminophen or an oral NSAID is first-line for mild to moderate attacks. Take medication early in the attack.[1]
  • Consider preventive therapy if you have four or more headaches a month, at least eight headache days a month, or attacks that stay disabling despite good acute treatment.[2]
  • The American Headache Society's 2024 position statement says CGRP-targeting therapies should be considered a first-line option for migraine prevention, without first failing other preventive drug classes.[3]
  • Using triptans on 10 or more days a month, or non-opioid pain relievers such as acetaminophen and NSAIDs on 15 or more days a month, for more than three months can cause medication overuse headache, a treatable but often missed diagnosis.[4][5]
  • A sudden, severe (thunderclap) headache needs emergency evaluation, because the most common secondary cause is bleeding around the brain (subarachnoid hemorrhage).[6]

Migraine is a major cause of disability worldwide. In the Global Burden of Disease 2016 study, migraine accounted for 5.6% of all years lived with disability, behind only low back pain, and it is the leading cause of disability in people under 50.[7] Yet in clinical experience, most patients seen for the first time have either been undertreated for years, relying on over-the-counter medications that do not adequately control their attacks, or overtreated, taking acute medications so often that the medications themselves are now driving daily headaches.

The good news is that migraine management has changed substantially. Migraine-specific preventive therapies that target calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) are now available, and the American Headache Society (AHS) found that their efficacy and tolerability are equal to or greater than those of the older first-line preventives. Its 2024 position statement update moved these therapies earlier: they should be considered a first-line option without a requirement for prior failure of other preventive drug classes.[3] Acute treatment options have also expanded with the gepants.

This guide covers what you need to know: how migraine is diagnosed, what the current treatment options look like, when preventive therapy makes sense, and how to avoid the common pitfalls that trap patients in a cycle of worsening headache.

What Migraine Actually Is

Migraine is not simply a "bad headache." It is a neurological disorder with defined diagnostic criteria. Untreated or unsuccessfully treated attacks last 4 to 72 hours. The pain has at least two of these features: one-sided location, pulsating quality, moderate or severe intensity, and worsening with (or avoidance of) routine physical activity. During the headache there is nausea or vomiting, or sensitivity to both light (photophobia) and sound (phonophobia).[8]

The underlying biology centers on calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), the most abundant neuropeptide in the human trigeminal ganglia. CGRP plays a central role in the blood vessel widening and the peripheral and central sensitization of a migraine attack.[9] Triptans act on 5-HT1B/1D receptors on intracranial blood vessels and trigeminal sensory nerves, which constricts cranial vessels and inhibits the release of pro-inflammatory neuropeptides.[10] Gepants and CGRP monoclonal antibodies target CGRP or its receptor directly.[9]

Migraine With Aura vs. Without Aura

Some people with migraine have aura: fully reversible visual, sensory, speech or language, motor, brainstem or retinal symptoms. At least one aura symptom spreads gradually over 5 minutes or more, and each aura symptom lasts 5 to 60 minutes. Visual aura is the most common type, occurring in over 90% of people with migraine with aura, at least in some attacks.[11]

Migraine with aura matters for contraception. The FDA label for one widely used combined estrogen-progestin pill (Yaz) groups women with a high risk of arterial or venous thrombotic disease as unable to use it, and that group includes women who have headaches with focal neurological symptoms, or migraine with or without aura if over age 35.[12] Tell whoever prescribes your contraception about your aura history. Certain aura types, such as hemiplegic migraine and migraine with brainstem aura, are also reasons to consider preventive therapy.[2]

Episodic vs. Chronic Migraine

Episodic migraine means fewer than 15 headache days per month.[9] Chronic migraine means headache on 15 or more days per month for more than three months, with migraine features on at least 8 of those days per month. The most common cause of symptoms that look like chronic migraine is medication overuse.[13] This progression is not inevitable: a recent review concluded that trial data support earlier use of CGRP therapies to help avoid chronification.[9]

Acute Treatment: Stopping an Attack

The goal of acute treatment is straightforward: stop the attack as quickly and completely as possible, and keep it from coming back within 24 hours. The critical principle is timing. Medication should be taken early in the attack.[1] In a Cochrane overview of sumatriptan, taking medication early, when pain was mild, was more effective than waiting until the pain was moderate or severe.[14]

Triptans: The First-Line Standard

Triptans are serotonin (5-HT1B/1D) receptor agonists[10] and are an effective first-line treatment for moderate to severe migraine.[1] Several triptans are available in the U.S.; they differ in dose forms, dosing limits and half-life.

Sumatriptan has the largest evidence base of the group: a Cochrane overview pooled data on 18 dose and route combinations in 52,236 participants. The subcutaneous 6 mg injection was the most effective route, reducing pain from moderate or severe to none at two hours in 59% of people versus about 15% with placebo; oral 50 mg gave complete relief in 28% versus 11% with placebo.[14] The injection works fastest but has more adverse events and a higher cost, so oral tablets are the practical choice for most patients.[14]

Triptan Contraindications

The sumatriptan (Imitrex) label lists these contraindications: ischemic coronary artery disease or coronary artery vasospasm; Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome or related arrhythmias; a history of stroke or transient ischemic attack; hemiplegic or basilar migraine; peripheral vascular disease; ischemic bowel disease; uncontrolled hypertension; use of an ergotamine-containing medication or another 5-HT1 agonist (such as another triptan) within 24 hours; use of an MAO-A inhibitor now or within 2 weeks; and severe hepatic impairment.[10] Pregnancy is not on that list, but if you are pregnant or planning pregnancy, review your options with your physician. If you have cardiovascular risk factors, discuss them before starting a triptan.

NSAIDs and Acetaminophen

Strong evidence supports acetaminophen and oral NSAIDs as first-line treatments for mild to moderate migraine attacks. Typical doses studied include naproxen 500 mg (825 mg may be slightly more effective), ibuprofen 400 mg, and aspirin 1,000 mg. In a trial comparing sumatriptan plus naproxen (Treximet) with each medication alone, the combination was superior for partial pain relief at two hours.[1]

Acetaminophen is an option for patients who cannot take NSAIDs or triptans. The recommended dose is 1,000 mg, though acetaminophen was less effective than NSAIDs.[1]

Gepants: A New Class of Acute and Preventive Treatment

Gepants are small-molecule CGRP receptor antagonists.[21][22] Unlike the sumatriptan label, the ubrogepant and rimegepant labels do not list coronary artery disease, stroke or uncontrolled hypertension as contraindications, although both labels warn about hypertension and Raynaud's phenomenon.[10][21][22] This makes them an option to discuss when a triptan is not suitable.

  • Ubrogepant (Ubrelvy): 50 mg or 100 mg tablet for acute migraine. A second dose may be taken at least 2 hours after the first if needed, up to 200 mg in 24 hours. It must not be combined with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors. Its approval trials measured pain freedom and freedom from the most bothersome symptom at 2 hours.[21]
  • Rimegepant (Nurtec ODT): 75 mg orally disintegrating tablet, labeled both for acute treatment (75 mg as needed, maximum 75 mg in 24 hours) and for preventing episodic migraine (75 mg every other day).[22] The 2024 AHS position statement includes rimegepant and atogepant among the CGRP-targeting preventive therapies it supports as first-line.[3]

Anti-Nausea Medications

Nausea is a major barrier to effective acute treatment: if you cannot keep a tablet down, no oral medication will work. Antiemetics are often used for migraine. Metoclopramide and prochlorperazine are the most extensively studied, and both have been proven superior to placebo.[1] The 2025 AHS guideline update for emergency department care found IV prochlorperazine highly likely to be effective based on multiple high-quality (class I) studies and made it a Level A ("must offer") recommendation for eligible adults.[23]

Preventive Therapy: Who Needs It and What Works

Preventive therapy is underused. A family medicine review notes that many patients would benefit from prophylactic therapy, but only 3% to 13% obtain it.[2] That gap represents a large number of people experiencing preventable disability.

When to Consider Prevention

The American Academy of Family Physicians review recommends considering preventive therapy when any of the following apply:[2]

  • Four or more headaches a month, or at least eight headache days a month
  • Debilitating attacks despite appropriate acute treatment
  • Difficulty tolerating, or a contraindication to, acute therapy
  • Medication overuse headache
  • Certain migraine subtypes, such as hemiplegic migraine or migraine with brainstem aura
  • Patient preference
Setting Expectations for Prevention

Successful prevention is defined as a 50% reduction in the number of headache attacks or days. Improvement may appear in six to eight weeks, but full effect can take up to six months.[2] Keep a headache diary during this period so you have objective data.

CGRP Monoclonal Antibodies: The New Standard

The first CGRP monoclonal antibody, erenumab, was approved in the U.S. in 2018.[27] All earlier first-line migraine preventives were developed for other conditions and adopted later for migraine; the CGRP-targeting therapies were developed specifically for migraine.[3]

Three self-injected antibodies are labeled for the preventive treatment of migraine in adults:

  • Erenumab (Aimovig): 70 mg once monthly; some patients may benefit from 140 mg once monthly.[27] The open-label APPRAISE trial (2024) found that 56% of patients on erenumab cut their monthly migraine days by more than half, versus 16% on non-specific oral preventives.[9]
  • Fremanezumab (Ajovy): 225 mg monthly, or 675 mg every 3 months given as three consecutive 225 mg injections.[28] In a 2026 network meta-analysis, fremanezumab ranked highest for both efficacy and safety.[29]
  • Galcanezumab (Emgality): a 240 mg loading dose (two 120 mg injections), then 120 mg monthly. In its two episodic migraine trials, monthly migraine headache days fell by 4.7 and 4.3 days on 120 mg, versus 2.8 and 2.3 days on placebo.[30]

A fourth option, eptinezumab (Vyepti), is given as a 100 mg intravenous infusion every 3 months; some patients may benefit from 300 mg.[31]

2024 AHS Position Statement: CGRP as First-Line Prevention

The 2024 American Headache Society position statement update concluded that CGRP-targeting therapies should be considered a first-line approach for migraine prevention, along with the older first-line treatments, without a requirement for prior failure of other classes of preventive treatment.[3] This is an important shift, particularly for patients who have contraindications to older medications.

The side effect profile of CGRP antibodies is generally favorable. On the erenumab label, the most common side effects are injection site reactions and constipation, and the label warns about constipation with serious complications, hypertension and Raynaud's phenomenon.[27] Injection site reactions are the most common side effect on the fremanezumab and galcanezumab labels.[28][30] In a three-year open-label extension of erenumab, the safety profile was similar to earlier studies.[9] Insurance coverage can be a barrier: most plans use stepped care, requiring trials of other preventives before covering CGRP antibodies.[9]

Medication Overuse Headache: The Hidden Driver

Medication overuse is the most common cause of symptoms that look like chronic migraine.[13] Here is the paradox: the medications you are taking to relieve your headache are making you have more headaches.

The ICHD-3 diagnostic criteria for MOH require:[32]

  • Headache occurring on 15 or more days per month in someone with a pre-existing headache disorder
  • Regular overuse for more than three months of one or more drugs taken for acute or symptomatic treatment of headache
  • The pattern is not better explained by another diagnosis

The usage threshold varies by medication class. Triptans,[4] opioids[33] and combination analgesics[34] count as overuse at 10 or more days per month for more than three months. Non-opioid pain relievers such as acetaminophen and NSAIDs count at 15 or more days per month.[5] ICHD-3 notes that these day counts are based on expert opinion rather than formal evidence.[32]

What I see most often in practice: a patient with episodic migraine starts using their acute medication more frequently as headaches intensify. The medication works, so they use it again. Over months, they shift from 4 headache days per month to 15 or more, with a dull background headache present most days. The acute medication still provides brief relief, reinforcing the behavior. By the time they see a physician, they often believe their migraine has simply "gotten worse," and they are partly right, but the medication is a significant driver.

Recognizing Medication Overuse Headache

Signs that you may be in medication overuse: daily or near-daily headache that is different from your typical migraine; acute medication works initially but the headache returns within hours; using acute medication on 10 or more days a month; feeling like you need to take something "just in case." If this sounds familiar, a physician evaluation is needed before simply stopping medications on your own, because withdrawal needs a supervised plan.

Treatment centers on a supervised plan to stop or cut back the overused medication. After withdrawal, migraine either reverts to an episodic pattern or remains chronic,[13] and medication overuse headache is itself a reason to consider preventive therapy.[2] A 2024 meta-analysis summarized in a 2025 review found that CGRP antibodies reduced medication overuse compared with placebo when the overused drugs were triptans or multiple drugs, though not simple analgesics.[9]

Menstrual Migraine

Menstrual migraine refers to attacks that cluster predictably around menstruation. The ICHD-3 research criteria for pure menstrual migraine use day 1 plus or minus 2 days, that is, from 2 days before to 3 days after the first day of bleeding.[35] Menstrually associated migraine is often prolonged and difficult to manage with conventional therapies.[36]

Two broad management strategies apply. The first is optimized acute treatment: using the most effective acute medication at the earliest sign of the attack. A long-acting triptan such as frovatriptan, with a half-life of about 26 hours, is one option.[19]

The second strategy is mini-prophylaxis: short-term preventive treatment around the expected period. In a randomized trial of 546 women, frovatriptan 2.5 mg once or twice daily for 6 days, starting 2 days before the expected menstrual migraine, lowered the share of women with a menstrual migraine from 67% on placebo to 52% (once daily) and 41% (twice daily).[36] The 2012 AAN/AHS guideline rated frovatriptan effective for preventing menstrual migraine (Level A).[20] This is an off-label use: the frovatriptan label states that it is not indicated for the prevention of migraine attacks.[19]

For women on hormonal contraception, the approach depends on aura and age. As noted above, a combined estrogen-progestin pill label lists headaches with focal neurological symptoms, and migraine with or without aura after age 35, among its contraindications.[12] Your physician can review non-estrogen options with you.

Triggers, the Headache Diary, and What Actually Causes Your Attacks

Every patient seen in clinical practice with migraine has a trigger they believe they need to eliminate. Chocolate, red wine, aged cheese. The reality is more complicated, and more useful.

Triggers do not cause migraine attacks directly. They lower the threshold in a brain that is already primed for an attack. Most attacks require multiple factors converging: hormonal changes plus poor sleep plus skipping a meal, for example. This means avoiding a single trigger often does not prevent attacks, and being overly restrictive about triggers can worsen quality of life without meaningful benefit.

Commonly reported, modifiable triggers include:

  • Sleep disruption: both insufficient sleep and sleeping too much are triggers. Regular sleep timing matters for many migraine patients.
  • Psychological stress: not only stress itself, but also the "let-down" after a stressful period (weekend migraine is a classic example).
  • Hormonal changes: especially around menstruation.
  • Skipped meals and dehydration: both can lower the migraine threshold in susceptible individuals.
  • Caffeine: both excess intake and withdrawal can trigger attacks. Consistency matters; the goal is a stable, moderate intake, not elimination.
  • Sensory stimuli: bright or flickering light, strong odors, and loud sustained noise can trigger attacks in some patients.

A headache diary, recording date, duration, severity, associated symptoms, possible triggers, and medications used, is the single most useful tool for identifying your personal pattern. It also provides the objective data your physician needs to assess preventive therapy response. Diary apps such as Migraine Buddy and N1 Headache are available, and many patients find them easier than paper.

Red Flags: When a Headache Is Not Migraine

Every physician who treats headache has at least one patient in mind who presented with "their usual migraine" and turned out to have a subarachnoid hemorrhage. The lesson is not to panic about every headache, but to apply systematic screening for warning signs.

The SNNOOP10 list summarizes red flags for secondary (dangerous) headache. Its core items include:[37]

  • S: Systemic symptoms, including fever, and a history of cancer (neoplasm).
  • N: Neurologic deficit, including decreased consciousness.
  • O: Onset that is sudden or abrupt. A thunderclap headache needs urgent investigation because the most common secondary cause is subarachnoid hemorrhage.[6] This is the most critical red flag.
  • O: Older age at onset: new headache starting after age 65.
  • P: Pattern change, or recent onset of a new headache.

The list's other warning features include positional headache; headache brought on by sneezing, coughing or exercise; papilledema; progressive or atypical headache; pregnancy or the puerperium (the weeks after delivery); a painful eye with autonomic features; headache that starts after head trauma; immune system disorders such as HIV; and painkiller overuse or a new drug at headache onset.[37]

Go to the Emergency Department If You Have:
  • The "worst headache of your life" or sudden, severe headache reaching max intensity in seconds
  • Headache with fever, stiff neck, or rash
  • Headache with new weakness, numbness, vision loss, or speech changes
  • Headache after head trauma
  • Headache in pregnancy or in the weeks after delivery
  • Headache lasting more than 72 hours (status migrainosus)

Status Migrainosus

Status migrainosus is a debilitating migraine attack lasting more than 72 hours. Breaks of up to 12 hours due to medication or sleep still count toward that duration.[38] By the time patients seek help, they have often already used their usual acute medications without adequate relief.

Management escalates beyond typical outpatient options. For adults treated in the emergency department for a migraine attack, the 2025 AHS guideline update says IV prochlorperazine and greater occipital nerve block must be offered (Level A); IV ketorolac, IV metoclopramide and subcutaneous sumatriptan should be offered (Level B); and IV dexamethasone and IV valproate may be offered (Level C). It made no recommendation for normal saline (Level U).[23] IV dexamethasone can also be considered to help decrease headache recurrence.[1]

Do not try to manage status migrainosus at home. If your migraine has lasted more than two days and is not improving, seek care.

Managing Migraine Through Telehealth

Much of migraine care is well suited to telehealth. The diagnosis of migraine is clinical, made from the history rather than from imaging or lab tests, and the ongoing management of a known diagnosis requires physician review, not physical examination, in most cases.

What we do effectively via telehealth at TeleDirectMD:

  • Initial evaluation of headache history to diagnose episodic or chronic migraine
  • Prescribing and refilling acute medications (triptans, gepants, anti-nausea agents)
  • Initiating and monitoring preventive therapy, including CGRP monoclonal antibodies when appropriate
  • Reviewing headache diary data to assess treatment response and adjust plans
  • Identifying medication overuse headache and developing a supervised withdrawal plan
  • Discussing hormonal considerations for women with migraine, including contraception guidance

In-person or specialist evaluation remains important for new or changing headache patterns that warrant imaging, for suspected hemiplegic or basilar migraine, for patients who have failed multiple preventive therapies, and when neurological examination is required. Frequently Asked Questions

For mild to moderate attacks, acetaminophen or an oral NSAID such as naproxen or ibuprofen is a first-line option. For moderate to severe attacks, triptans such as sumatriptan are an effective first-line treatment. The key principle is to take medication early in the attack. Gepants (ubrogepant, rimegepant) are another acute option, and their labels do not list the heart and stroke contraindications that the sumatriptan label does.

Consider preventive therapy if you have four or more headaches a month or at least eight headache days a month, attacks that stay disabling despite good acute treatment, trouble tolerating acute medicines, or medication overuse headache. The 2024 American Headache Society position statement says CGRP-targeting therapies should be considered a first-line option for prevention, alongside older first-line drugs such as propranolol and topiramate, without first failing other drug classes.

Medication overuse headache (MOH) is headache on 15 or more days a month in someone with a pre-existing headache disorder, together with regular overuse of acute headache medicine for more than three months. Triptans, opioids and combination analgesics count as overuse at 10 or more days a month; non-opioid pain relievers such as acetaminophen or NSAIDs count at 15 or more days a month. Treatment is a supervised plan to stop or cut back the overused medication, often with preventive therapy.

Migraine with aura involves fully reversible neurological symptoms, most often visual, that come before or with the headache. Each aura symptom lasts 5 to 60 minutes, and at least one spreads gradually over 5 minutes or more. Aura matters for contraception: the label for one common combined estrogen-progestin pill lists headaches with focal neurological symptoms as a contraindication, so tell whoever prescribes your contraception about your aura. Some aura types, such as hemiplegic migraine and migraine with brainstem aura, are also reasons to consider preventive therapy.

CGRP (calcitonin gene-related peptide) monoclonal antibodies block CGRP or its receptor to prevent migraine. Erenumab (Aimovig) and galcanezumab (Emgality) are monthly self-injections, fremanezumab (Ajovy) is given monthly or every three months, and eptinezumab (Vyepti) is an IV infusion every three months. The 2024 AHS position statement supports CGRP-targeting therapies as a first-line preventive option. In galcanezumab's two episodic migraine trials, monthly migraine headache days fell by 4.7 and 4.3 days on treatment versus 2.8 and 2.3 days on placebo. Injection site reactions are the most common side effect.

Common migraine triggers include disrupted sleep (both too little and too much), psychological stress, hormonal changes (particularly around menstruation), bright or flickering lights, strong odors, skipping meals, dehydration, alcohol (especially red wine), and changes in caffeine intake. Triggers vary significantly between individuals. A headache diary helps identify your personal pattern rather than relying on generic trigger lists.

Menstrual migraine refers to attacks that occur predictably around menstruation; the ICHD-3 research criteria for pure menstrual migraine use the window from 2 days before to 3 days after the first day of bleeding. These attacks are often prolonged and difficult to manage with conventional therapies. Management includes standard acute therapy and, when attacks are predictable, short-term perimenstrual frovatriptan, which the 2012 AAN/AHS guideline rated effective for this use (Level A). That use is off-label.

Red flags for a secondary headache include systemic symptoms such as fever, a new neurologic deficit, sudden or thunderclap onset, new headache starting after age 65, and a change in your usual headache pattern. A sudden, severe (thunderclap) headache needs emergency evaluation because the most common secondary cause is subarachnoid hemorrhage. Any new or meaningfully changing headache pattern warrants prompt medical assessment.

Status migrainosus is a debilitating migraine attack lasting more than 72 hours; breaks of up to 12 hours due to medication or sleep still count. It typically needs treatment beyond standard outpatient options, such as IV medications in the emergency department. Seek urgent evaluation rather than trying to manage it at home.

Telehealth works well for much of migraine care: initial evaluation, prescribing and refilling acute and preventive medications, reviewing headache diaries, and adjusting treatment plans. In-person evaluation is needed for new or changing headache patterns that require imaging, for neurological symptoms, or when your physician needs to perform a physical exam.
