Does D-Mannose Work for UTIs?

A large double-blind trial (Hayward 2024, JAMA Internal Medicine, N=598) found D-mannose no better than placebo for preventing recurrent UTIs. Here's what the evidence actually supports, and what works instead.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD Last reviewed August 30, 2026 14 min read

Does D-mannose actually work for preventing UTIs?

In a large double-blind trial (Hayward 2024, N=598), 51.0% of women taking D-mannose and 55.7% taking placebo had another clinically suspected UTI, a difference that was not statistically significant, and the 2025 AUA/CUA/SUFU guideline amendment tells clinicians to inform patients that D-mannose alone "may not be effective" for UTI prevention. Better-supported options depend on the situation. For perimenopausal and postmenopausal women with recurrent UTIs, the guideline says clinicians should recommend vaginal estrogen (Moderate Recommendation), and a 2020 meta-analysis found vaginal estrogen lowered recurrent UTIs versus placebo (relative risk 0.42). Cranberry products reduced symptomatic, culture-verified UTIs in women with recurrent UTIs (relative risk 0.74) in the 2023 Cochrane review. In the ALTAR trial (BMJ 2022), methenamine hippurate had 1.38 UTIs per person-year versus 0.89 with daily antibiotic prophylaxis; the difference stayed within the trial's non-inferiority margin, so methenamine was judged non-inferior. Women with recurrent UTIs who drink less than 1.5 L a day may be offered more water. Two newer oral antibiotics, pivmecillinam (Pivya, 2024) and gepotidacin (Blujepa, 2025), are approved for uncomplicated UTIs. Any of these can be discussed with a licensed clinician, accessible via telehealth.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated August 30, 2026.

🗂 Key Takeaways

  • A large double-blind trial (Hayward 2024, N=598) found no meaningful reduction in UTI recurrence versus placebo in women with recurrent UTIs treated in UK primary care.[1]
  • The 2025 AUA/CUA/SUFU guideline amendment tells clinicians to inform patients that D-mannose alone "may not be effective" for UTI prevention. It also says clinicians should offer cranberry and may offer methenamine hippurate.[2]
  • For perimenopausal and postmenopausal women, the guideline says clinicians should recommend vaginal estrogen, and a meta-analysis found it lowered recurrent UTIs versus placebo (relative risk 0.42).[2][17] In November 2025 the FDA began removing boxed-warning statements on cardiovascular disease, breast cancer and probable dementia from menopausal hormone therapy labels.[3]
  • Cranberry products reduced symptomatic, culture-verified UTIs by about 26% in women with recurrent UTIs (RR 0.74) in a 2023 Cochrane review of 50 trials.[5] The magnitude is modest, but the evidence base is real.
  • Methenamine hippurate was non-inferior to daily low-dose antibiotics in the ALTAR trial, although UTI rates were higher with methenamine (1.38 vs 0.89 episodes per person-year).[6]
  • Two newer oral antibiotics for uncomplicated UTIs, pivmecillinam (Pivya, 2024) and gepotidacin (Blujepa, 2025), add treatment options.[7][8]
  • Brand-name UTI supplement blends (Uqora, Utiva, AZO Cranberry) have no product-specific trial results among this guide's sources; a registered Uqora study was terminated without posted results.[22]

What Is D-Mannose, and Why Was Everyone Taking It?

D-mannose is a simple sugar, a monosaccharide isomer of glucose. It is absorbed in the upper gastrointestinal tract and excreted in the urine.[1] The theory behind its use in UTIs is that E. coli, the main cause of UTIs, attaches to the lining of the urinary tract, and D-mannose in urine may block that attachment so the bacteria are flushed out with urination.[9][19] It may do this by binding to a site on the tip of the bacteria's fimbriae, and it has shown benefit in animal models.[1]

The mechanism made D-mannose a popular UTI supplement. Then came a large placebo-controlled trial.

What Changed in 2024 to 2026

Three developments reshaped the practical answer to "what should I take to prevent UTIs":

  1. 2024: JAMA Internal Medicine published the Hayward trial, a double-blind, placebo-controlled D-mannose trial in 598 women in UK primary care. It found no benefit versus placebo.[1]
  2. 2025: The American Urological Association, Canadian Urological Association, and Society of Urodynamics, Female Pelvic Medicine & Urogenital Reconstruction amended their joint recurrent-UTI guideline (approved by the AUA Board of Directors in August 2025). The amended guideline says clinicians should offer cranberry, may offer methenamine hippurate, and should inform patients that D-mannose alone may not be effective.[2]
  3. November 2025 onward: The FDA initiated removal of boxed-warning statements about cardiovascular disease, breast cancer and probable dementia from menopausal hormone therapy labeling, and in February 2026 announced approved labeling changes that include topical vaginal estrogen products.[3] The American Urogynecologic Society supported removing the boxed warning from topical low-dose vaginal estrogen.[4]

In parallel, two new oral antibiotics were approved for uncomplicated UTIs: pivmecillinam (Pivya) in 2024 and gepotidacin (Blujepa) in 2025.[7][8] Both add options for treating uncomplicated UTIs in women.

The Hayward Trial: What It Actually Showed

Before 2024, the main positive evidence for D-mannose came from Kranjčec 2014, a trial in 308 women that compared D-mannose 2 g daily with nitrofurantoin 50 mg daily and with no prophylaxis. Recurrence rates were 14.6% for D-mannose, 20.4% for nitrofurantoin, and 60.8% for no prophylaxis.[10] That trial was open-label and had no placebo arm; the Hayward authors noted that the lack of a placebo comparison could have affected how often women presented with symptoms.[1]

The Hayward trial addressed those problems.[1] It enrolled 598 women with recurrent UTIs from 99 UK primary care centers, randomized them to 2 g of D-mannose powder daily or a matched placebo powder for six months, and asked a pragmatic question: did they contact ambulatory care for another clinically suspected UTI?

The results: 51.0% of the D-mannose group had another clinically suspected UTI, versus 55.7% of the placebo group. The relative risk was 0.92 (95% CI 0.80 to 1.05), and the authors report that the confidence interval excluded the minimum treatment effect their patient advisory panel had considered important, which was at least a 50% reduction.[1] The authors found no difference in symptom burden, time to the next UTI, number of UTIs, antibiotic use, or hospitalizations.[1]

The authors' conclusion, verbatim: "Daily D-mannose should not be recommended to prevent future episodes of clinically suspected UTI in women with rUTI in primary care."[1]

This is not the last word. The authors note that their results may not apply to women seen in specialist (secondary) care, that underdosing is possible although their dose matched two earlier trials that found benefit, and that D-mannose started during an active infection might behave differently.[1] Pooled analyses also disagree: a 2024 network meta-analysis of nonantibiotic options reported a lower UTI risk with D-mannose than with placebo (RR 0.34),[24] while a 2025 meta-analysis of 6 randomized trials found no significant reduction compared with control (RR 0.57, 95% CI 0.29 to 1.15).[23] A 2022 Cochrane review, which searched up to February 2022, found little to no evidence to support or refute D-mannose for preventing or treating UTIs.[19] But for the specific, high-volume question "should I take D-mannose to prevent my next UTI," the large placebo-controlled trial found no meaningful benefit.

The 2025 AUA Guideline Stance

In 2025 the AUA, CUA, and SUFU amended their recurrent-UTI guideline, which dates from 2019 and was confirmed in 2022; the AUA Board of Directors approved the amendment in August 2025.[2] Its statements on non-antibiotic prophylaxis:

  • Statement 13, cranberry: Clinicians should offer cranberry as an option for prophylaxis for women with recurrent UTIs (Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade B).[2]
  • Statement 14, D-mannose: Clinicians should inform patients with recurrent UTIs that D-mannose alone for prophylaxis "may not be effective in UTI prevention" (Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade B).[2]
  • Statement 15, methenamine hippurate: Clinicians may offer methenamine hippurate for prophylaxis for women with recurrent UTIs (Conditional Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade C).[2]
  • Statement 16, water intake: When women with recurrent UTIs drink less than 1.5 L/day, clinicians may offer increased water intake for prophylaxis (Conditional Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade C).[2]

Statement 20 says that in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women with recurrent UTIs, clinicians should recommend vaginal estrogen therapy to reduce the risk of future UTIs if there is no contraindication (Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade B).[2]

D-Mannose Dosage: There Is No Established Dose

There is no established D-mannose dose, because no dose has been shown to prevent UTIs in a placebo-controlled trial. The two trials that shaped the evidence both used 2 g daily: the Hayward trial gave 2 g of D-mannose powder daily for six months, and the earlier Kranjčec 2014 trial gave 2 g of D-mannose powder in 200 ml of water daily for six months.[1][10] The Hayward authors list possible underdosing as a limitation, while noting that their dose matched two earlier trials that found benefit.[1] Because the larger, placebo-controlled trial found no benefit over placebo, there is no dose that can be recommended as effective.

If you do take D-mannose, 2 g daily is the amount that has actually been tested. In the 2022 Cochrane review, adverse events in D-mannose trials were few and poorly reported, none were serious, and they were mostly diarrhea and vaginal burning.[19] Pregnant women were excluded from the Hayward trial,[1] so check with a clinician before using it in pregnancy, and also if you have diabetes.

Cranberry: The Modest but Real Evidence

Cranberry has a long, mixed history as a UTI remedy. In 2020 the FDA announced a qualified health claim for certain cranberry products, concluding there is "limited and inconsistent credible scientific evidence" for cranberry juice beverages and a reduced risk of recurrent UTI in healthy women.[11] The 2023 Cochrane review of 50 randomized trials in 8,857 people found cranberry products reduced the risk of symptomatic, culture-verified UTIs by about 30% overall (RR 0.70, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.84) and by about 26% in women with recurrent UTIs (RR 0.74, 95% CI 0.55 to 0.99).[5]

The benefit is real but selective. Cranberry probably helped women with recurrent UTIs, children, and people susceptible to UTIs after a medical intervention. The evidence did not support its use in the elderly, people with bladder emptying problems, or pregnant women.[5]

The proposed active ingredients are proanthocyanidins (PACs), which inhibit the adherence of p-fimbriated E. coli to the cells lining the bladder. The Cochrane review could not show a difference in UTI risk between low, moderate and high PAC doses, and it is unclear whether juice and tablets differ.[5] The FDA's qualified claim wording refers to one 8 oz serving of cranberry juice beverage a day, or 500 mg of cranberry dietary supplement a day.[11]

Methenamine Hippurate: An Old Drug Making a Comeback

Methenamine hippurate is a prescription drug labeled for prophylactic or suppressive treatment of frequently recurring urinary tract infections. In acid urine, its methenamine component is hydrolyzed to formaldehyde.[20]

The 2022 ALTAR trial in BMJ randomized 240 women with recurrent UTIs at eight UK centers to methenamine hippurate or daily low-dose antibiotic prophylaxis for 12 months.[6] The methenamine dose was 1 g twice daily.[16] During treatment, antibiotic-treated UTIs occurred at 1.38 episodes per person-year with methenamine hippurate versus 0.89 with daily antibiotics. The absolute difference of 0.49 (90% CI 0.15 to 0.84) stayed within the trial's prespecified non-inferiority margin of one UTI per person-year, so methenamine was judged non-inferior; UTI rates were higher, not lower, on methenamine.[6] Resistance findings were mixed: during treatment, antibiotic resistance in perineal swab isolates was more common with daily antibiotics (72% vs 56%), but after treatment ended, multidrug resistance was more common in the methenamine arm (20% vs 5%).[16]

The tradeoffs: it is labeled for prevention of frequently recurring UTIs. It is contraindicated in renal insufficiency, severe hepatic insufficiency, or severe dehydration, it should not be given to patients taking sulfonamides, and the label says care should be taken to keep the urine acidic.[20] The AUA says clinicians may offer it for prophylaxis,[2] so it is a reasonable option to discuss for women who want to avoid daily antibiotic prophylaxis and are not candidates for vaginal estrogen.

Vaginal Estrogen: The Highest-Impact Option Postmenopause

If you are perimenopausal or postmenopausal and having recurrent UTIs, the AUA guideline says clinicians should recommend vaginal estrogen to reduce the risk of future UTIs if there is no contraindication.[2] In a meta-analysis, vaginal estrogen also lowered vaginal pH compared with placebo.[17]

In a 2020 meta-analysis of randomized trials, five studies of vaginal estrogen (1,936 patients) showed a significant reduction in recurrent UTIs versus placebo (relative risk 0.42, 95% CI 0.30 to 0.59), while oral estrogen showed no significant difference.[17] Individual trials are often small: one 2021 randomized trial of vaginal estrogen cream or ring in 35 postmenopausal women found fewer UTIs within 6 months with vaginal estrogen than with placebo (11 of 18 vs 16 of 17 women in the intention-to-treat analysis).[12] The 2025 AUA/SUFU/AUGS guideline on genitourinary syndrome of menopause states that local low-dose vaginal estrogen has the most robust evidence base among treatments for that condition.[13]

Safety concerns kept many women and clinicians away from it. The American Urogynecologic Society says studies of topical low-dose vaginal estrogen have not found significantly increased risks of cardiovascular disease, cancer, thromboembolism, or dementia.[4] The FDA initiated removal of the boxed-warning statements on cardiovascular disease, breast cancer and probable dementia from menopausal hormone therapy labeling in November 2025, and its February 2026 announcement lists topical vaginal estrogen among the product types with approved labeling changes.[3] Labels dated before these changes, such as a generic estradiol vaginal insert label revised in September 2025, still carry the older boxed warning.[21]

For menopausal vaginal atrophy, the label schedule for 10 mcg estradiol vaginal inserts is 1 insert daily for 2 weeks, then 1 insert twice weekly. Contraindications on that label include undiagnosed abnormal genital bleeding, breast cancer or a history of it, estrogen-dependent neoplasia, and active or past blood clots.[21] Vaginal estrogen can be prescribed after a synchronous telehealth visit when it is appropriate.

Probiotics and Lactobacillus: An Emerging Story

Depletion of vaginal lactobacilli is associated with UTI risk, which suggests that restoring them may help.[14] The best-known trial is the 2011 Stapleton phase 2 trial of a Lactobacillus crispatus intravaginal suppository (Lactin-V) in 100 young premenopausal women with recurrent UTI, given daily for 5 days and then weekly for 10 weeks. Recurrent UTI occurred in 15% of women on Lactin-V versus 27% on placebo (RR 0.5, 95% CI 0.2 to 1.2, so the confidence interval included no effect).[14]

That was a vaginal product, so its results do not tell us whether oral probiotic capsules work. If you want to try a probiotic, choose a strain with published human trial data and set expectations accordingly.

The New Antibiotics: Pivya and Blujepa

Two oral antibiotics were approved for uncomplicated UTIs in 2024 and 2025.[7][8]

Pivmecillinam (Pivya) is a penicillin-class antibacterial approved in 2024 for female patients 18 and older with uncomplicated UTIs caused by susceptible E. coli, Proteus mirabilis, and Staphylococcus saprophyticus. The labeled dose is one 185 mg tablet by mouth 3 times a day for 3 to 7 days as clinically indicated. The most common side effects in clinical trials were nausea and diarrhea.[7]

Gepotidacin (Blujepa) was approved in 2025 for female adult and pediatric patients 12 years and older weighing at least 40 kg with uncomplicated UTIs caused by susceptible bacteria including E. coli. It is a triazaacenaphthylene bacterial type II topoisomerase inhibitor. The labeled dose is 1,500 mg (two 750 mg tablets) by mouth twice daily, about 12 hours apart, for 5 days. In two trials it was non-inferior to nitrofurantoin; composite response rates were 51.8% vs 47.0% in one trial and 58.9% vs 44.0% in the other. The most common side effects include diarrhea, nausea, abdominal pain, and flatulence.[8]

The AUA guideline's first-line agents for symptomatic UTIs in women are nitrofurantoin, TMP-SMX, and fosfomycin, chosen according to the local antibiogram.[2] Pivmecillinam and gepotidacin are options to discuss when first-line agents are not suitable, for example because of culture results or past reactions.

Uqora, Utiva, AZO, and Other Brand Blends

Consumer UTI supplement brands package familiar ingredients, such as D-mannose or cranberry, into branded products.

  • Uqora: A study of Uqora products, run with Uqora as a collaborator and registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04024046), was designed to compare Uqora products with placebo in women with recurrent UTIs. It was terminated in 2020 and has no posted results.[22]
  • Utiva and AZO cranberry products: These are cranberry products. The Cochrane review supports cranberry products in general for women with recurrent UTIs but found no clear difference between PAC doses,[5] and this guide's sources include no trial of these specific brands.

Under FDA rules, only a drug can legally claim to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent a disease. A dietary supplement label with a structure/function claim must carry a disclaimer that FDA has not evaluated the claim and that the product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease.[15]

What Does Not Work

Patients frequently ask about the remedies below. None of the trials, reviews or guidelines cited in this guide supports them for preventing or treating UTIs:

  • Vitamin C at supplement doses. Often suggested as a way to acidify urine.
  • Apple cider vinegar. Popular online, with no supporting trial in this guide's sources.
  • Baking soda. An old folk remedy for burning; it is not a treatment for infection.
  • Colloidal silver. No supporting trial in this guide's sources.
  • Uva ursi (bearberry). No supporting trial for UTI prevention in this guide's sources.
  • Garlic supplements. Popular in wellness content, with no supporting trial in this guide's sources.
  • Wiping direction. The classic "front to back" advice is not harmful, but no trial in this guide's sources tested it for preventing UTIs.

Red Flags: When to Contact a Clinician

Supplement questions are usually not urgent. UTI symptoms sometimes are. Seek prompt evaluation for any of the following:

  • Fever above 100.4°F (38°C), flank or back pain, nausea, or vomiting with urinary symptoms. These suggest pyelonephritis.
  • Blood in the urine that is more than a trace, especially in men, women over 40, or anyone with tobacco exposure.
  • Symptoms during pregnancy at any trimester.
  • Symptoms that persist or worsen after 48 hours of an antibiotic that should be effective.
  • New symptoms in a man of any age (UTI in healthy adult men is uncommon and usually requires further evaluation).
  • New symptoms after urinary tract instrumentation, catheterization, or stone passage.
  • Recurrent UTIs that are not responding to standard preventive measures.

Symptomatic UTIs in otherwise healthy adult women can often be diagnosed and treated through a telehealth visit. Complicated cases, pediatric patients, and pyelonephritis usually need in-person evaluation.
