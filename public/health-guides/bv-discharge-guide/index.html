BV Discharge

A physician's evidence-based guide to what bacterial vaginosis discharge looks like, how it differs from other causes, and why a swab beats a visual guess.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: September 8, 2026
13 min read

What does bacterial vaginosis discharge look like?

Bacterial vaginosis classically causes a thin, milklike discharge that smoothly coats the vaginal walls, usually white or gray, often with a strong fish-like odor, especially after sex. That discharge is one of the four Amsel criteria that CDC describes for diagnosis, along with a vaginal pH above 4.5, clue cells on microscopy and a fishy odor on the whiff test; at least three are needed. The texture is a useful contrast with yeast, which tends to cause thick, curdy discharge, and with trichomoniasis, whose discharge can be diffuse, malodorous or yellow-green. A critical caveat: most BV has no symptoms at all, with 84 percent of women with BV in a national survey reporting none, so discharge alone is not a reliable rule-in and its absence is not a rule-out. Because a medical history alone is not enough to tell the causes of vaginal symptoms apart, a swab test rather than a visual guess is the reliable way to identify which one you have.

Key Takeaways

  • BV discharge is classically thin and milklike, smoothly coats the vaginal walls, and is often white or gray, in contrast to the thick, curdy discharge typical of yeast.[1][2]
  • A strong fish-like odor, especially after sex, is a typical BV symptom, and a fishy odor on the whiff test is one of the four Amsel diagnostic criteria.[1][2]
  • Most BV produces no symptoms at all: 84 percent of women with BV in a national survey reported no symptoms, so the absence of discharge does not rule BV out.[5]
  • Several conditions produce abnormal discharge, including BV, yeast, trichomoniasis, and cervicitis, and a medical history alone is not enough to tell them apart, so testing is more reliable than a visual guess.[1]
  • Diagnosis rests on the Amsel criteria (at least 3 of 4 findings), a Nugent Gram stain score of 7 to 10, or a molecular test, not on the appearance of discharge alone.[1][4]
  • Douching does not help discharge: CDC says it might increase the risk of BV relapse and that no data support it for treatment or symptom relief, and in a national survey BV was more common with more frequent douching.[1][5]
  • BV is treated with antibiotics prescribed by a healthcare provider: metronidazole or clindamycin in CDC's recommended regimens, with secnidazole, tinidazole and other clindamycin forms as alternatives.[1][2]

Discharge is the symptom people associate most with bacterial vaginosis, and it is also the most misunderstood. This guide explains exactly what BV discharge looks like, how it differs from the other causes of abnormal discharge, why so many women with BV have no discharge at all, and how clinicians actually use discharge, along with three other findings, to make the diagnosis.

What BV Discharge Actually Looks Like

The classic BV discharge has a recognizable profile. CDC describes it as homogeneous and thin, with a milklike consistency, meaning it looks the same throughout rather than lumpy, and it smoothly coats the vaginal walls rather than collecting in one spot.[1] CDC's patient page describes it as a thin white or gray discharge.[2] The discharge is a sign of the underlying shift: in BV, the normal hydrogen peroxide and lactic acid producing Lactobacillus species are replaced by high concentrations of anaerobic bacteria, including Gardnerella vaginalis and Prevotella and Mobiluncus species.[1] Alongside that discharge, a strong fish-like odor is common, especially after sex.[2]

It is worth naming what it usually is not. Thick, curdy discharge is typical of yeast, and discharge that is diffuse, malodorous, or yellow-green can come from trichomoniasis.[1] A foul odor can also come from a forgotten tampon or other foreign body, and foul-smelling discharge is one of the possible symptoms of pelvic inflammatory disease.[7][8] But these descriptions only get you so far. CDC notes that a medical history alone is insufficient for accurate diagnosis of vaginitis and can lead to the wrong medicine, so the human eye is not a reliable diagnostic instrument here.[1]

Trichomoniasis is the important third cause in this table. It is a sexually transmitted infection caused by Trichomonas vaginalis. Most people who have it (70 to 85 percent) have minimal or no genital symptoms, but women sometimes have vaginal discharge that can be diffuse, malodorous, or yellow-green, with or without vulvar irritation, findings that push the differential well beyond the BV versus yeast question.[1] The table wording follows CDC's descriptions, and CDC stresses that none of the typical yeast symptoms is specific and that pH testing is not highly specific either.[1][2]

Discharge by Cause: Reading the Differences

Because the table above shows real overlap, a better way to think about abnormal discharge is by what drives each one. BV discharge reflects a shift in the vaginal microbiome: lactobacilli are replaced by high concentrations of anaerobic bacteria, and a polymicrobial biofilm forms on vaginal cells.[1] In one molecular study, women without BV had 1 to 6 bacterial species per sample, mostly lactobacilli, while women with BV had 9 to 17.[6] Yeast infection is usually caused by the fungus Candida albicans.[1] Trichomoniasis is an infection with a single organism that is readily passed between sex partners.[1]

The practical consequence is that treatment differs. BV is treated with an antibiotic such as metronidazole or clindamycin, uncomplicated yeast infection with an antifungal azole, and trichomoniasis with metronidazole (CDC's recommended regimen for women is 500 mg twice daily for 7 days, with a single 2 g dose of tinidazole as an alternative), which is why identifying the cause before treating is the whole game.[1] Without rapid, simple tests, yeast infection is both overdiagnosed and underdiagnosed.[10] It is also a genuinely common problem: in a national U.S. survey, 29.2 percent of women aged 14 to 49 had BV on a Gram stain, and CDC estimates that 75 percent of women will have at least one yeast infection, so this differential is everyday medicine rather than an edge case.[5][1] For the full BV versus yeast comparison, see our BV vs yeast guide.

When There Is No Discharge At All

Here is the single most counterintuitive fact about BV: most people who have it do not notice discharge. In the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2001 to 2004, only 15.7 percent of women with BV on a Gram stain of a self-collected swab reported vaginal symptoms, which means 84 percent did not report any.[5]

This cuts in both directions. If you do not have discharge, you can still have BV. And BV matters beyond symptoms: women with BV are at increased risk of acquiring sexually transmitted infections such as HIV and of complications of pregnancy.[1][5] CDC recommends treatment for women with symptoms and for all pregnant women with symptoms; for BV found without symptoms during pregnancy, studies have yielded mixed results, a decision your clinician can help weigh.[1]

Beyond BV, Yeast, and Trichomoniasis

The big three, BV, yeast, and trichomoniasis, are the infections most often behind vaginal symptoms, but they are not the whole list, and two other causes are common enough to matter.[1] One is cervicitis, inflammation of the cervix. When testing finds a cause, it is most often chlamydia or gonorrhea; cervicitis is often silent, but some women notice an abnormal vaginal discharge and bleeding between periods, especially after sex. It needs its own testing for chlamydia and gonorrhea and an assessment for pelvic inflammatory disease rather than a vaginitis medicine.[1] The other is atrophic vaginitis, caused by a decrease in estrogen, which normally happens after menopause: the vaginal tissues become thinner, and symptoms can include dryness, slight discharge, burning, and light bleeding after intercourse.[12]

There are rarer inflammatory causes as well, such as desquamative inflammatory vaginitis, an uncommon form of chronic purulent vaginitis whose main symptoms include a purulent (pus-like) discharge, irritation, and pain with sex; it is a diagnosis of exclusion.[11] The point of naming them is not to alarm but to be honest about the limits of visual matching: abnormal discharge spans a real differential. In a clinician's office, pH, a KOH test, and microscopy of the discharge can often identify the cause, and vaginal swabs are an optimal specimen for chlamydia and gonorrhea testing, so a single evaluation can address most of it.[1]

How Discharge Fits Into Diagnosis

Discharge is one part of the diagnosis, not the whole thing. The Amsel criteria, proposed in a 1983 study of 397 university students,[3] require at least three of four findings: a homogeneous, thin discharge that smoothly coats the vaginal walls; a vaginal pH above 4.5; clue cells on microscopy, which are vaginal cells studded with adherent bacteria; and a fishy odor before or after adding 10% potassium hydroxide (KOH) to a sample, the whiff test.[1] Three of the four are objective (pH, clue cells, whiff test), which is why the appearance of discharge alone never confirms the diagnosis. Compared with the Nugent score, the Amsel criteria have a sensitivity of 37 to 70 percent and a specificity of 94 to 99 percent.[1]

The laboratory alternative is the Nugent score, in which a Gram stain is scored from 0 to 10 as a weighted combination of lactobacilli, Gardnerella or Bacteroides morphotypes, and curved gram-variable rods, with a score of 7 or higher indicating BV.[4] CDC calls the Gram stain the reference standard laboratory method and reads a score of 7 to 10 as BV.[1] Both approaches beat examining discharge by eye, and molecular tests (NAATs) add another option: they detect specific bacterial DNA with high sensitivity and specificity, though CDC says they should be used only in women with symptoms.[1]

How Discharge Is Actually Tested

Knowing what happens at the test takes some of the mystery out of it. A sample of the discharge is taken with a swab, and several things are done with it. In the clinician's office, the pH of the vaginal secretions is measured with pH paper, a saline wet mount is examined under a microscope for clue cells and motile trichomonads, and a potassium hydroxide preparation is used both for the whiff test and to look for yeast hyphae or blastospores.[1] CDC cautions that pH testing is not highly specific, and that microscopy picks up only about half of trichomoniasis and yeast infections compared with molecular testing or culture.[1] In the laboratory, a Nugent Gram stain grades the bacterial morphotypes, some molecular BV panels also report results for Candida species and Trichomonas vaginalis, and chlamydia and gonorrhea are tested by NAAT, for which vaginal swabs are an optimal specimen.[1][4]

Two practical notes. BV molecular tests can be performed on either clinician- or self-collected vaginal specimens, and for chlamydia testing, patient-collected vaginal swabs are equivalent in sensitivity and specificity to clinician-collected ones.[1] And tell the clinician about douching, vaginal products, or any self-treatment, because CDC advises that these be asked about as part of the evaluation.[1] None of this hurts, and it is the step that turns a description of discharge into a diagnosis.

Abnormal Discharge vs Normal Discharge

A meaningful share of what brings people in for "abnormal discharge" is actually normal. Glands in the cervix and the walls of the vagina normally produce clear mucus, which may turn white or yellow when exposed to the air, and the amount varies during the menstrual cycle with changes in hormone levels. Ovulation, pregnancy, and sexual excitement can all increase the amount of normal discharge.[7]

The line to watch is change. A discharge that has been your normal for years and is not accompanied by odor, itch, or irritation is very likely fine. A sudden change in the amount, color, odor, or consistency of discharge, or new itching, redness, or swelling, is the signal to evaluate rather than explain away.[7] Crucially, douching to manage either normal or abnormal discharge is counterproductive: it removes the healthy bacteria that line the vagina, and CDC notes that it might increase the risk of BV relapse.[7][1]

Pregnancy changes the baseline too. It is one of the factors that can increase the amount of normal discharge, which is easy to mistake for a problem.[7] The same rule applies: a change, an itch, or a new odor during pregnancy warrants a call to the clinician rather than an assumption that it is normal, because CDC recommends BV treatment for all pregnant women with symptoms, given its links to premature rupture of membranes and preterm birth.[1] Any vaginal bleeding during pregnancy should be treated as a potential emergency.[13]

Discharge Red Flags: When to Be Seen Promptly

Certain features should never be waited out. Seek prompt evaluation for discharge that is greenish or gray with a foul odor, discharge with fever or pelvic or lower abdominal pain, pain during sex, bleeding between periods, or any discharge during pregnancy.[7][8][1] These can signal pelvic inflammatory disease, an infection of the uterus, ovaries, and other reproductive organs most often caused by gonorrhea or chlamydia, or other infections that need treatment without delay.[8] CDC notes that women with PID often have subtle or nonspecific symptoms, that delay in diagnosis and treatment probably contributes to lasting damage, and that severe illness, nausea and vomiting, or a temperature above 38.5 C (101 F) are among the reasons for hospital care.[1] Any vaginal bleeding during pregnancy should be treated as a potential emergency.[13] Likewise, if a completed course of treatment did not resolve the discharge, return for evaluation, because the diagnosis may have been wrong; CDC notes that retreatment with the same regimen is an acceptable approach after a first recurrence, but recurring symptoms should be evaluated first.[1]

Preventing Discharge From Returning

Because the discharge follows the infection, the same steps that lower recurrence lower the chance the discharge comes back. Take all of the medicine prescribed, even if symptoms go away.[2] Avoid douching, which CDC says might increase the risk of relapse.[1] During treatment, either avoid sex or use condoms consistently and correctly, as CDC advises, and not douching and using condoms the right way every time are among the steps that may help lower the risk of BV.[1][2] None of these are a guarantee, and CDC notes that how BV spreads and how best to prevent it are not completely understood.[2]

If the discharge returns despite a completed course, return for evaluation. For women with multiple recurrences after a recommended regimen, CDC describes suppressive options such as 0.75% metronidazole gel twice weekly for more than 3 months, which has been reported to reduce recurrences, although the benefit does not persist when it is stopped.[1] How to get rid of BV lays out the recurrence options in full.

Treatment for BV Discharge

Once confirmed, BV is treated with one of CDC's recommended regimens: metronidazole 500 mg by mouth twice daily for 7 days, metronidazole 0.75% gel, one full applicator (5 g) vaginally once daily for 5 days, or clindamycin 2% cream, one full applicator (5 g) vaginally at bedtime for 7 days. Alternatives include a single 2 g dose of secnidazole oral granules, oral tinidazole, and oral or vaginal clindamycin, and CDC lists secnidazole as an alternative because of its higher cost and lack of long-term outcome data.[1] Clindamycin cream is oil based and might weaken latex condoms and diaphragms for 5 days after use.[1] Take all of the medicine prescribed, even if the discharge and odor go away before the course ends.[2]

Recurrence is common: in one Australian study of women treated with oral metronidazole 400 mg twice daily for 7 days, 58 percent had BV again within 12 months, and a past history of BV and a regular sex partner were linked to recurrence.[9] So if discharge returns, the conversation shifts from one more course to a recurrence plan. How to get rid of BV covers that in depth.

What to expect while it clears. The discharge and odor may improve before the course ends, which is exactly when people are tempted to stop, and CDC's advice is to take all of the medicine even if symptoms go away.[2] Some women then notice a yeast infection. In the product labels, about 10 percent of women treated with metronidazole vaginal gel developed Candida vaginitis during or right after therapy, vaginal yeast (moniliasis) was reported in 10.4 percent on the 7-day clindamycin cream regimen, and vulvovaginal candidiasis in 9.6 percent after secnidazole; it is a recognized side effect of treatment rather than a failure of it, and it may need an antifungal.[14][15][16] It is discussed in the BV vs yeast guide.
