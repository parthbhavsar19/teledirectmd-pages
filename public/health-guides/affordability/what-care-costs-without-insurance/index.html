What Care Costs Without Insurance

What a doctor visit and urgent care cost without insurance, and whether paying cash ever beats running it through your plan.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: October 3, 2026
5 min read

Without insurance, a routine doctor visit in the United States typically costs roughly $100 to $300, and an urgent care visit roughly $125 to $300. These are wide ranges because the price depends on where you go, what you need, and whether the clinic quotes you a cash price or a hospital list price. The single most important thing to know is that the price is not fixed, and it can be looked up before you walk in the door.

Key Takeaways

  • A primary care visit without insurance usually costs $40 to $300, averaging around $171 (Zocdoc[1]).
  • An urgent care visit typically costs $125 to $300, averaging about $180 (GoodRx[2]).
  • The emergency room is the expensive default, with an average uninsured visit around $2,715 in 2025 (Zocdoc[1]).
  • Cash and self-pay prices are sometimes lower than insurance-negotiated rates and sometimes higher, so you have to look up the actual number.
  • You can learn the price in advance with free tools built on claims data, or by asking for a flat cash price.

Typical costs, by setting

These numbers are consumer estimates from widely used cost references, and they are approximate because prices vary by region and facility type. Use them to set expectations, not as quotes.

Type of visit Typical self-pay range Notes
Primary care office visit $40 to $300 Average around $171; new patients often higher
Urgent care visit $125 to $300 Average around $180
Telehealth visit often lower Flat cash prices are common
Retail clinic often lower Limited services, flat fees
Emergency room often $1,000 to $2,700+ Average $2,715 in 2025

The urgent care range of $125 to $300 with a $180 average comes from GoodRx[2], and the primary care range of $40 to $300 with a $171 average from Zocdoc[1]. The emergency room average of $2,715 is also from Zocdoc[1].

The nonprofit FAIR Health[3] gives a clean frame for how the settings compare. For the same routine concern, it illustrates an urgent care visit at roughly $300 versus an emergency room visit at roughly $1,000, plus a higher ER copay. The ER is the expensive default for problems that are not emergencies, and urgent care is usually the cheaper path for the same complaint.

Why the price varies so much

There are three different prices attached to almost any medical service, and which one you are quoted changes the bill.

Hospitals are required by the federal Hospital Price Transparency rules[4] to publish three things: their gross charges, which is the full list price; the discounted cash price they charge people who pay themselves; and the payer-specific negotiated rates agreed with each insurer.

The gross charge is a ceiling almost no insured person pays, because insurers negotiate it down. The discounted cash price is what a self-pay patient can often get. The negotiated rates are what insurance companies actually pay. Which of these you are quoted is the single biggest driver of the price you see.

Cash price versus insurance: the honest picture

A natural assumption is that cash always costs more than going through insurance, or that insurance always saves you money. The evidence is genuinely mixed, and that is the point.

An analysis of hospitals' disclosed prices published by STAT News[5] found that for services you can shop for, the cash price was lower than the highest insurer-negotiated price 87% of the time, lower than most major insurers' negotiated prices 55% of the time, and lower than even the lowest negotiated price 43% of the time. A later Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School study[6] reached the same general conclusion.

But the opposite also happens. Reporting on the new price data found that among a sample of 1,550 hospitals, 21% billed cash and uninsured patients at their highest rate[7]. In short, some institutions discount for cash and some do not.

The practical lesson is not that cash is cheaper or more expensive. It is that the price depends on the specific provider, and you can find it out before you go.

How to find the actual price before you go

You do not have to arrive and wait for the bill. Three concrete moves get you a number in advance.

First, look it up. The free FAIR Health consumer tool[8] shows typical in-network and out-of-network costs for a service in your area, and the free Healthprices.org[9] tool from the Health Care Cost Institute shows average negotiated prices by metro area. Both are built on claims data, not marketing estimates.

Second, ask for the cash or self-pay price directly. Many urgent care centers, clinics, and telehealth services publish a flat cash price for common visit types. A flat, upfront price means no surprise coinsurance and no later bill.

Third, for hospital care, check the hospital's own price transparency file, which the rules above require them to publish, or ask their billing office for a good-faith estimate before a non-emergency procedure.

Lower-cost options that exist today

If you do not have insurance, several pathways cost less than the default:

  • Federally Qualified Health Centers are required by law to offer sliding-scale fees based on income, and some visits cost little or nothing.
  • Retail clinics and telehealth often have lower, flatter prices for straightforward problems such as a sore throat, a urinary tract infection, or a sinus infection.
  • Urgent care is nearly always cheaper than the emergency room for the same non-emergency concern (FAIR Health[3]).
  • Community and free clinics serve uninsured patients at low or no cost in many areas.

A genuine emergency is different. If a condition is life threatening, cost should not steer you away from an emergency room, which is required to stabilize you regardless of your ability to pay.

What insurance still does that cash cannot

Nothing here means insurance is pointless. Insurance caps a catastrophic year at an out-of-pocket maximum, covers hospitalizations, surgery, and chronic medication, and protects against costs that are impossible to pay out of pocket. The cash-price discussion applies mainly to routine, shoppable care, where self-pay can sometimes beat a high-deductible plan. For anything serious, coverage is what actually protects you.

Frequently asked questions

How much does urgent care cost without insurance? Typically $125 to $300, with an average around $180, according to GoodRx[2]. The final price depends on the clinic, your location, and any tests or treatment you need.

How much does a doctor visit cost without insurance? A primary care visit usually runs $40 to $300, averaging $171 across major U.S. cities according to Zocdoc[1].

Is urgent care cheaper than the emergency room without insurance? Usually. For the same non-emergency concern, FAIR Health[3] illustrates urgent care at roughly $300 versus an emergency room at roughly $1,000, and an ER visit often carries two separate bills.

Is it cheaper to pay cash than to use insurance? Sometimes, but not always. STAT News[5] found cash prices beat the highest insurer-negotiated rate 87% of the time for shoppable services, while the Wall Street Journal analysis[7] found 21% of hospitals billed cash patients at their highest rate. The answer depends on the provider.

How do I find out the price before I go? Use the free FAIR Health[8] and Healthprices.org[9] tools, ask the clinic for its self-pay price directly, or check a hospital's price transparency file.

What if I cannot afford the cash price? Ask about a sliding-scale fee at a Federally Qualified Health Center, a community clinic, or the provider's financial assistance program. Many will reduce the price based on income.

Is a telehealth visit cheaper without insurance? Often yes. Telehealth practices frequently publish a flat cash price for common visit types, which can be lower than an in-person office visit in the same area.

Does paying cash affect my credit? Paying cash is a normal transaction and does not by itself affect credit. It is unpaid bills that can eventually reach collections and credit reports.
