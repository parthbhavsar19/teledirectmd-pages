Genital Herpes (HSV-2) Treatment Guide

Antiviral regimens, suppressive therapy, transmission reduction, and pregnancy management.

Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed Feb 2026
Based on CDC STI Treatment Guidelines, 2021

How do you treat genital herpes (HSV-2)?

Genital herpes (HSV-2) affects an estimated 11.9% of people aged 14 to 49 in the United States. The 2021 CDC STI Treatment Guidelines list valacyclovir 1 g twice daily, acyclovir 400 mg three times daily, or famciclovir 250 mg three times daily, each for 7 to 10 days, for a first episode. Daily suppressive therapy reduces recurrences by 70% to 80% in people with frequent outbreaks, and in a randomized trial of 1,484 discordant couples, daily valacyclovir 500 mg roughly halved HSV-2 acquisition (hazard ratio 0.52). Episodic therapy (for example, valacyclovir 500 mg twice daily for 3 days) works best when started within 1 day of symptoms. Most genital herpes is transmitted by people who are unaware of their infection or have no symptoms at the time; condoms and antivirals reduce but do not eliminate risk. In pregnancy, CDC recommends suppressive therapy starting at 36 weeks for women with recurrent genital herpes, and cesarean delivery when recurrent lesions are present at the onset of labor. Most uncomplicated cases are appropriate for telehealth evaluation; complicated or red-flag presentations should be seen in-person.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated July 14, 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • HSV-2 causes most cases of recurrent genital herpes, and an estimated 11.9% of people aged 14 to 49 in the United States are infected.[1]
  • First-episode treatment: valacyclovir 1 g twice daily, acyclovir 400 mg three times daily, or famciclovir 250 mg three times daily, each for 7 to 10 days.[1]
  • Daily suppressive therapy reduces recurrences by 70% to 80% in people with frequent outbreaks,[1] and daily valacyclovir 500 mg roughly halved HSV-2 acquisition in discordant couples (hazard ratio 0.52).[4]
  • Most genital herpes is transmitted by people who are unaware of their infection or have no symptoms at the time. Condoms and daily antivirals reduce the risk, but neither eliminates it.[2]
  • In pregnancy, CDC recommends suppressive therapy starting at 36 weeks' gestation for women with recurrent genital herpes, and cesarean delivery when recurrent lesions are present at the onset of labor.[1][2]

Genital herpes is one of the most common sexually transmitted infections in the United States, yet it remains one of the most misunderstood. The stigma around a diagnosis often overshadows the clinical reality: this is a manageable chronic infection with effective antiviral medications, evidence-based transmission prevention strategies, and, for many people, recurrences that become less frequent over time.[2]

In practice, patients newly diagnosed with HSV-2 often fear the worst: constant outbreaks, ruined relationships, dangerous pregnancy complications. Most of those fears don't match the evidence. What matters clinically is understanding your treatment options, knowing when to use them, and having a clear picture of how to protect partners.

This guide covers the full clinical picture: CDC treatment regimens, the difference between suppressive and episodic therapy, how seroprevalence and serology testing work, asymptomatic shedding data, transmission risk reduction, pregnancy management, and where telehealth fits into long-term HSV-2 care.

How Common Is HSV-2? Understanding Seroprevalence

An estimated 11.9% of people aged 14 to 49 in the United States are infected with HSV-2, or about 12%.[1] Prevalence is not evenly distributed. In national survey data from 2015 to 2016, age-adjusted HSV-2 prevalence among people aged 14 to 49 was 15.9% in females and 8.2% in males.[9] In one cohort study, men with genital HSV-2 had about 20% more recurrences than women, which the authors suggest may contribute to the higher rate of transmission from men to women.[11]

Globally, the WHO estimates that around 846 million people aged 15 to 49 are living with genital herpes infections, more than 1 in 5 in that age group.[8] WHO's 2020 estimates include 520 million people with genital HSV-2 and 376 million with genital HSV-1, about 50 million of whom also have HSV-2.[8] Genital infections caused by HSV-1 have been increasing, a shift that a 2025 review attributes mainly to falling HSV-1 seroprevalence, less childhood exposure to the virus, and changes in sexual practices.[7]

One fact worth understanding: the majority of people infected with HSV-2 have never had the condition diagnosed. Many have mild or unrecognized infections but shed virus intermittently, so most genital herpes infections are transmitted by people who are unaware they are infected or who have no symptoms when transmission occurs.[2] This silent reservoir is the primary driver of ongoing transmission.

HSV-1 vs. HSV-2 Genital Herpes: What's the Difference?

Both HSV-1 and HSV-2 can infect the genital tract, but they behave very differently after the first episode. Recurrences and subclinical shedding are much more frequent with genital HSV-2 than with genital HSV-1.[2] In a cohort of people with a symptomatic first episode of genital HSV-2, 89% had at least one recurrence during follow-up, the median recurrence rate was 0.34 per month, and 38% had at least 6 recurrences in the first year.[11] In a study of 377 adults with symptomatic genital HSV-2, virus was detected on 33.6% of days in people less than 1 year from their first episode and on 16.7% of days in those 10 or more years out; shedding without symptoms occurred on 26.2% and 9.3% of days, respectively.[6]

Genital HSV-1 follows a different trajectory. It can be acquired through receptive oral sex with a partner who has oral herpes.[5] After the first episode, recurrences are less frequent than with genital HSV-2, and genital shedding decreases rapidly during the first year.[2] In a University of Washington cohort of 82 people with a first episode of genital HSV-1, genital shedding was detected on 12.1% of days at 2 months and on 7.1% of days at 11 months.[10]

In clinical practice, patients with genital HSV-1 are often relieved to hear that their infection is less likely to cause frequent recurrences long-term. The flip side is that the transmission prevention data for suppressive therapy come from HSV-2 discordant couples. For genital HSV-1, no data are available on suppressive therapy for preventing transmission.[2]

Diagnosis and Type-Specific Serology: When to Test

Active genital lesions are best diagnosed with a swab from the lesion tested for HSV, and the virus should be typed as HSV-1 or HSV-2. Swabs are taken from the base of the lesion, and testing early in an episode is much more likely to find the virus.[5] Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAAT, such as PCR) are the most sensitive; the sensitivity of viral culture is low, especially for recurrent lesions, and drops quickly as lesions heal.[2] A diagnosis based on appearance alone is unreliable: genital ulcers can also be caused by syphilis, chancroid and other infections, as well as by noninfectious causes such as trauma.[2]

Type-specific serologic testing (HSV-2 IgG) detects antibodies that develop during the first weeks after infection. False-negative results are more likely early, so after a suspected recent exposure CDC advises repeat testing 12 weeks later.[2] The indications for serology are narrower than many patients expect. CDC does not recommend HSV-2 serologic screening of the general population, and the USPSTF recommends against routine serologic screening in people without symptoms (a D recommendation), concluding with moderate certainty that the harms outweigh the benefits.[2][13]

When Type-Specific HSV-2 IgG Testing Is Appropriate
  • Your partner has genital herpes and you want to know your own status[2]
  • Recurrent or atypical genital symptoms or lesions with a negative HSV PCR or culture result[2]
  • A clinical diagnosis of genital herpes that was never confirmed by laboratory testing[2]
  • Pregnancy, for a woman with no history of genital herpes whose sex partner has HSV infection[2]
  • Persons with HIV, as part of the initial evaluation, particularly with a history of genital symptoms[2]

One practical note: the most commonly used test, the HerpeSelect HSV-2 enzyme immunoassay, is often falsely positive at low index values (1.1 to 3.0), and one study reported a specificity of only 39.8% for index values of 1.1 to 2.9. For low index values (below 3.0), CDC recommends a confirmatory test with a second method (Biokit or Western blot) before the result is interpreted.[2]

If you have lesions or suspect a recent STI exposure that involves multiple pathogens at once, broader screening is appropriate. Direct-to-consumer at-home tests now exist for some infections. For example, the Visby at-home PCR test covers chlamydia, gonorrhea, and trichomoniasis, but not herpes. Suspected herpes lesions still need clinical assessment, either in-person or via telehealth with appropriate follow-up.

First-Episode Treatment

The first clinical episode of genital herpes tends to be the most severe. Newly acquired genital herpes can cause a prolonged illness with severe genital ulcers and neurologic involvement.[2] A first episode can also bring systemic symptoms and swollen lymph nodes, how severe it is may depend on previous HSV-1 or HSV-2 infection, and complications such as urinary retention or meningism can require hospital care.[5] CDC states that all patients with a first episode should receive antiviral therapy,[2] and the European guideline advises starting it at the earliest opportunity, on clinical suspicion alone, rather than waiting for lab confirmation.[5]

Drug Regimen Notes
Valacyclovir 1 g orally twice daily for 7 to 10 days[1] Better absorbed than acyclovir, which allows less frequent dosing[2]
Acyclovir 400 mg orally three times daily for 7 to 10 days[1] Acyclovir 200 mg five times daily is also effective but is not recommended because of how often it must be taken[1]
Famciclovir 250 mg orally three times daily for 7 to 10 days[1] Also has high oral bioavailability[2]

Treatment can be extended if healing is incomplete after 10 days of therapy.[1] For severe disease or complications that need hospitalization (such as disseminated infection, pneumonitis, or hepatitis) or nervous system complications (meningitis or encephalitis), CDC recommends IV acyclovir 5 to 10 mg/kg every 8 hours until clinical improvement, followed by oral therapy to complete more than 10 days of total treatment, with a longer course for nervous system complications.[1][2]

Suppressive Therapy vs. Episodic Therapy

After the first episode, patients have two long-term antiviral strategies: suppressive (daily) therapy or episodic (outbreak-triggered) therapy. The right choice depends on outbreak frequency, relationship context, and patient preference. Neither strategy is wrong. CDC notes that people with mild or infrequent outbreaks can also benefit from antiviral therapy, so the options should be discussed.[2]

Suppressive Therapy

Suppressive therapy means taking an antiviral every day, regardless of symptoms. It reduces recurrences by 70% to 80% in people who have frequent recurrences, people taking it often report no symptomatic outbreaks, and it also works for people with less frequent recurrences.[2] Daily suppression also reduces shedding: in the valacyclovir transmission trial, HSV DNA was detected on 2.9% of days with valacyclovir versus 10.8% of days with placebo.[4]

CDC Recommended Suppressive Regimens (HSV-2)
  • Acyclovir 400 mg orally twice daily[1]
  • Valacyclovir 500 mg orally once daily: might be less effective than other valacyclovir or acyclovir regimens for people with 10 or more episodes a year[1]
  • Valacyclovir 1 g orally once daily: the label's standard suppressive dose, with 500 mg once daily as an alternative for people with 9 or fewer recurrences a year[1][14]
  • Famciclovir 250 mg orally twice daily: CDC notes famciclovir appears somewhat less effective for suppressing viral shedding[1][2]

CDC advises discussing once a year whether to continue suppressive therapy, because recurrences become less frequent over time for many people. Neither stopping treatment nor laboratory monitoring is necessary, because adverse events and antiviral resistance with long-term use are uncommon.[2] Stopping for a while allows recurrence frequency to be reassessed, although many patients find little change in frequency or severity after stopping, and it is safe and reasonable to restart.[5] There is no fixed outbreak threshold for daily therapy: most early trials enrolled people with about six or more recurrences a year, but patients across the range of disease benefit, and the decision balances recurrence frequency, the impact on the person, and the need to manage transmission risk.[5]

Episodic Therapy

Episodic therapy means initiating antivirals at the first sign of a recurrence. It is most effective when started within 1 day of lesion onset or during the prodrome that precedes some outbreaks,[1] and the valacyclovir label states that benefit has not been established when treatment starts more than 24 hours after symptoms begin.[14] Episodic treatment shortens outbreaks by a median of 1 to 2 days.[5] It treats outbreaks as they happen; daily suppressive therapy, not episodic treatment, is the approach CDC describes as also lowering the risk of passing HSV-2 to partners.[2]

For episodic therapy to work, patients need to recognize their prodrome, such as pain or burning at the site before a lesion appears.[2] I routinely provide a prescription to keep on hand so patients can start treatment immediately without waiting for an appointment. CDC likewise advises giving patients a supply of the drug or a prescription, with instructions to start treatment immediately when symptoms begin.[1] Waiting a day or more for a prescription can cost the benefit, because benefit has not been established when treatment starts more than 24 hours after symptoms begin.[14]

Transmission Risk and Prevention

HSV-2 is transmitted during sexual activity.[8] Condoms help, but in a large pooled analysis their protection against HSV-2 was not as large as seen with other STIs, although they still offered moderate protection for both men and women.[3] Condoms and antivirals can be used together: CDC encourages discordant couples to consider suppressive antiviral therapy in addition to consistent condom use and avoiding sex during recurrences.[2]

Asymptomatic Shedding: The Underappreciated Risk

Most genital herpes infections are transmitted by people who are unaware they are infected or who have no symptoms when transmission occurs.[2] Transmission risk appears greatest during outbreaks or prodrome, but transmission can also happen without lesions because of subclinical viral shedding.[5] This is why people with no history of outbreaks can transmit the virus to partners, and why avoiding sex only during outbreaks is an incomplete prevention strategy.

Shedding is most frequent in the first 12 months after acquiring HSV-2.[2] In one study, virus was detected on 33.6% of days in people within a year of their first episode and on 16.7% of days in people 10 or more years out, still a substantial proportion.[6] This biology is the clinical rationale for suppressive therapy even in patients with infrequent symptomatic outbreaks.

These numbers tell an honest story: antivirals and condoms provide meaningful but incomplete protection, and neither alone eliminates risk. Condoms used consistently and correctly reduce, but do not eliminate, the risk of genital herpes transmission.[2] Abstaining from sex with uninfected partners when lesions or prodromal symptoms are present is a necessary addition to antiviral and barrier prevention, not a substitute for them.[2]

Partner Disclosure and Counseling

CDC advises that people with genital herpes inform current sex partners about the diagnosis and inform future partners before starting a sexual relationship.[2]

A recommendation for patients: the disclosure conversation often feels harder than it turns out to be. It helps to arrive with accurate information about transmission risk and protective strategies, and to frame the discussion around management and prevention rather than the diagnosis as an isolated fact. The European guideline advises that disclosure advice be practical and tailored to each person's situation, and that clinicians be frank about subclinical shedding and the limited impact of condoms and antivirals.[5]

Key Points for Partner Counseling
  • Asymptomatic partners of people with symptomatic genital herpes should be asked about any history of genital symptoms and offered type-specific HSV-2 serology to learn their own status.[2]
  • For seronegative partners, discuss suppressive antiviral therapy in the infected partner, consistent condom use, and avoiding sex during outbreaks as the standard prevention framework.[2]
  • CDC does not recommend post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) or pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) with antivirals for uninfected partners: no data support a recommendation or show that they would prevent acquisition, and they should not be offered as a prevention strategy.[2]
  • All persons with genital herpes should be tested for HIV, because HSV-2 genital herpes increases the risk of acquiring HIV twofold to threefold.[2]

Quality of life and relationship impact matter clinically. High distress at diagnosis is common, often persists with recurrences, and may be reduced by antivirals; some people develop symptoms of depression or withdraw from intimate relationships out of fear of disclosure.[5] When patients are anxious, depressed, or experiencing relationship distress from their diagnosis, addressing this directly, including mental health referral when appropriate, is part of good herpes care.

Pregnancy Management

Herpes can be very serious for a young baby,[15] but the risk of passing it on depends heavily on timing. The risk of transmission to the newborn is high (30% to 50%) when a mother acquires genital herpes near the time of delivery, and low (under 1%) for women with a prenatal history of recurrent herpes or who acquire genital herpes during the first half of pregnancy.[2] Understanding this distinction drives management decisions.

Suppressive Therapy in Pregnancy

CDC recommends suppressive antiviral therapy starting at 36 weeks' gestation for women with recurrent genital herpes.[1] Suppression from 36 weeks reduces cesarean deliveries by reducing recurrences at term, although it might not prevent transmission to the newborn in every case.[2] The European guideline says it should be offered to all women with a history of genital herpes.[5] A Cochrane review of seven randomized trials (1,249 participants) found that antiviral prophylaxis reduced:[12]

  • Recurrence of genital herpes at delivery: relative risk 0.28
  • Cesarean delivery for genital herpes: relative risk 0.30
  • HSV detected at delivery: relative risk 0.14

The review could not estimate the effect on neonatal herpes itself, and it advises discussing the risks, benefits, and alternatives of prophylaxis with each woman.[12]

CDC Suppressive Regimens in Pregnancy (starting at 36 weeks)
  • Acyclovir 400 mg orally three times daily: acyclovir is believed to be safe for use during all trimesters[1]
  • Valacyclovir 500 mg orally twice daily: prenatal data are more limited than for acyclovir, but animal data suggest low risk[1][2]

Delivery Mode

Women with recurrent genital herpes lesions at the onset of labor should have a cesarean delivery to reduce the risk of neonatal HSV infection, although cesarean delivery does not eliminate that risk.[2] The USPSTF notes that cesarean delivery can reduce the risk of transmission when genital lesions or prodromal symptoms are present.[13] Women without symptoms or signs of genital herpes or its prodrome can deliver vaginally.[2]

For women who acquire a first episode of genital herpes in the third trimester, the European guideline advises that cesarean delivery be considered, particularly when symptoms start within 6 weeks of delivery, because the risk of viral shedding in labor is very high.[5] CDC advises that women who acquire HSV in the second half of pregnancy be managed in consultation with maternal-fetal medicine and infectious disease specialists.[2]

Pregnancy-Specific Additional Considerations

  • First episode earlier in pregnancy: A first episode in the first or second trimester is managed according to the woman's clinical condition, often with oral or IV acyclovir, and vaginal delivery is anticipated if delivery does not follow soon after.[5] Pregnant women can also develop HSV hepatitis, with fever and markedly elevated liver tests but sometimes no genital or skin lesions, which needs IV acyclovir.[2]
  • Breastfeeding: CDC states that acyclovir is believed to be safe during breastfeeding.[1] The valacyclovir label notes that acyclovir, its active metabolite, is present in human milk.[14]
  • Routine serial testing: Not indicated. Sequential PCR testing in late pregnancy to predict viral shedding at delivery is not recommended.[5]
  • Routine HSV-2 serology screening of all pregnant women: Not recommended by the CDC or the USPSTF.[2][13] Targeted testing can help in specific situations, such as a woman with no history of genital herpes whose partner has HSV.[2]

HSV-2 in HIV-Positive Patients

Lesions caused by HSV are common among people with HIV and might be severe, painful, and atypical, and HSV shedding is increased.[2] The two infections interact: HSV-2 genital herpes increases the risk of acquiring HIV twofold to threefold,[2] and herpes infections activate HIV replication and may facilitate onward HIV transmission to sexual partners.[5] The CDC recommends that all persons with genital herpes be tested for HIV.[2]

First-episode treatment is the same as for people without HIV, although courses might need to be extended for lesions to heal.[2] For daily suppressive therapy in people with HIV, CDC lists acyclovir 400 to 800 mg two to three times daily, famciclovir 500 mg twice daily, or valacyclovir 500 mg twice daily. For episodic treatment, CDC lists acyclovir 400 mg three times daily, famciclovir 500 mg twice daily, or valacyclovir 1 g twice daily, each for 5 to 10 days.[1] The risk of genital ulcer disease rises during the first 6 months after starting antiretroviral therapy, especially with a CD4 count below 200; suppressive therapy reduces that risk and can be continued for 6 months after ART starts.[2]

One critical caveat: in people with HIV and HSV, suppressive antiviral therapy does not reduce the risk of HIV transmission or HSV-2 transmission to partners, and it does not delay HIV disease progression in people taking ART.[2] It also has no impact on the risk of acquiring HIV.[5] This misconception occasionally surfaces in clinical discussions and needs to be corrected.

The Role of Telehealth in Herpes Management

Telehealth fits naturally into HSV-2 care because most of what matters happens between outbreaks, not during them. A physician can start suppressive or episodic therapy based on history and prior lab results, counsel on lowering transmission risk, and manage ongoing prescriptions by video. Herpes in pregnancy needs obstetric care for delivery planning.

The initial diagnosis is the one area where telehealth has limits. If you have active lesions that have never been virologically confirmed, you ideally need an in-person swab (PCR or culture from the lesion base) to confirm the diagnosis and determine virus type. Once that is established, all subsequent management can be handled effectively via secure video visit, including antiviral prescribing, suppressive therapy adjustments and partner counseling.

For established patients, telehealth removes a meaningful barrier to care. Many people with herpes avoid discussing it with providers because of the stigma associated with in-person STI visits. A private video consultation makes it easier to have direct conversations about disclosure, partner testing, pregnancy planning, and antiviral strategy, conversations that are essential to good clinical outcomes but often don't happen when the barrier to access is high.

When to Seek Urgent or Emergency Care

Seek Prompt Medical Attention If You Experience:
  • Severe headache, stiff neck, or sensitivity to light, especially with fever, during an outbreak: these suggest HSV meningitis, which requires IV acyclovir[2]
  • Urinary retention or inability to urinate: can occur with a severe first episode of genital herpes and may require hospital care[5]
  • Extensive ulcers, or lesions that persist or recur despite oral antivirals, particularly in immunocompromised patients: acyclovir resistance should be suspected, with specialist care[2]
  • Eye pain, redness, light sensitivity, or changes in vision in the context of herpes: herpes eye infection can affect sight and needs urgent assessment; a red eye that hurts to look at light needs emergency care[16]
  • A newborn who seems unwell when the mother has genital herpes (fever, poor feeding, unusual sleepiness or floppiness, or sores on the skin, eyes, or mouth), particularly in the first weeks of life: neonatal herpes is a medical emergency, so get emergency care right away; babies with neonatal herpes need prompt IV acyclovir[2][15]
  • Symptoms suggesting disseminated herpes in immunocompromised patients, such as hepatitis, pneumonitis, or widespread skin involvement: requires hospital care and IV acyclovir[2][5]
  • A first genital herpes outbreak during pregnancy, especially in the second half: contact your obstetric team promptly, because management involves maternal-fetal medicine and infectious disease specialists[2]
  • Active genital lesions in a pregnant woman at the onset of labor: requires obstetric evaluation for cesarean delivery[2]

Most recurrent outbreaks in healthy, immunocompetent adults do not require emergency care. They are self-limiting and generally cause minor symptoms,[5] and episodic oral antivirals started at symptom onset can shorten them.[1][5] The scenarios above represent the exceptions: situations where the infection has moved beyond a localized mucocutaneous process and requires more intensive evaluation or treatment.
