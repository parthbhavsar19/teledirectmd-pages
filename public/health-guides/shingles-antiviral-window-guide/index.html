Antivirals for Shingles: Valacyclovir, Acyclovir, Famciclovir and the 72-Hour Window

Which antivirals treat shingles, their labeled doses, why the first 72 hours matter, when treatment still makes sense after 72 hours, and urgent warning signs.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MDLast reviewed November 23, 202618 min read

Which antivirals treat shingles, and how soon should you start?

CDC names three preferred antiviral drugs for shingles: acyclovir, valacyclovir and famciclovir. Treatment is most effective within 72 hours of symptom onset, and the FDA-labeled valacyclovir regimen is 1 gram three times daily for 7 days, started at the earliest sign of shingles. Do not wait for the clock if new blisters are forming or the rash affects the eye, face, ear, immune system, or nervous system. The valacyclovir trial and a clinical review of shingles care explain why timing and location both matter.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated November 23, 2026.

Key facts

  • Three prescription antivirals treat shingles: valacyclovir, famciclovir and acyclovir. [18][28]
  • Labeled adult doses: valacyclovir 1 g three times daily for 7 days, famciclovir 500 mg every 8 hours for 7 days, acyclovir 800 mg five times daily for 7 to 10 days. [19][20][21]
  • Start early: CDC says treatment is most effective within 72 hours of symptom onset, and the labels report the best results within 48 hours of rash onset. [18][19][21]
  • After 72 hours, guidelines still suggest treatment while new blisters keep forming in higher-risk cases, for eye or ear shingles, and for anyone with a weakened immune system. [25]
  • Head to head, valacyclovir and famciclovir performed about the same, and valacyclovir cleared pain faster than acyclovir in adults 50 and older. [1][23]
  • All three need a lower dose when kidney function is reduced, and older adults are more likely to have kidney and nervous system side effects. [19][20][21]

For overall shingles symptoms, Shingrix vaccination and longer-term nerve pain care, see the complete shingles guide.

Why does the 72-hour window matter for shingles antivirals?

Oral antiviral trials for shingles commonly enrolled participants within 72 hours after rash onset. [2][3][4] New lesions typically appear over 3 to 5 days, and antivirals reduce new lesion formation and viral shedding. [7] CDC states that treatment is most effective within 72 hours of symptom onset, and the valacyclovir and acyclovir labels note the best results when treatment starts within 48 hours of rash onset. [18][19][21] In the famciclovir placebo trial, the effect was also greater when therapy began within 48 hours of rash onset and in people 50 or older. [20] The practical message is simple: do not wait to seek care when shingles is suspected.

The 72-hour mark is a clinical trial evidence window, not a reason to ignore high-risk cases that present later. [2][3][7]

What are the doses of valacyclovir, famciclovir and acyclovir for shingles?

All three are oral prescription antivirals with FDA labeling for shingles. [19][20][21] Valacyclovir is converted in the body to acyclovir, and famciclovir is converted to penciclovir, which is why valacyclovir and famciclovir can be taken less often than acyclovir. [19][20][27] The doses below are for adults with normal kidney function; kidney adjustments are in the kidney dosing table further down. [19][20][21] Valacyclovir can be taken with or without food. [19]

Which antiviral is best for shingles?

No single antiviral has proven clearly better on every outcome. In head-to-head trials, valacyclovir sped pain resolution compared with acyclovir, while valacyclovir and famciclovir performed about the same. [1][23] In the valacyclovir label's summary of that comparison, among people who had pain after the rash healed, the median duration was 40 days with 7 days of valacyclovir and 59 days with 7 days of acyclovir. [19]

ComparisonWho was studiedWhat the trial found

Valacyclovir vs famciclovir597 immunocompetent adults 50 and older, treated within 72 hoursNo statistically significant difference in pain resolution, rash healing or PHN. [23]
Famciclovir vs acyclovir545 adults with uncomplicated shinglesCrusting and loss of acute pain were comparable; no significant difference in time to loss of PHN. [20]

Guideline bodies read this evidence slightly differently. A 2026 American Family Physician review rates early antiviral use as strength-of-recommendation A and states that valacyclovir and famciclovir achieve superior pain control and reduce the risk of PHN. [26] The European dermatology guideline says that limited evidence suggests better results with valacyclovir and famciclovir than with oral acyclovir, but that this was not consistently reproduced, and it suggests choosing together with the patient based on dosing frequency, cost, other conditions and drug interactions. [25] NIH guidelines for people with HIV prefer valacyclovir or famciclovir because of their better absorption and simpler dosing. [27]

In practice, the choice often comes down to dosing frequency (three times a day instead of five), kidney function and cost. [19][20][21][25]

What do the antiviral studies show?

Antivirals speed the healing of lesions, reduce new lesions and viral shedding, and decrease the severity of acute pain. [18] In the trials, famciclovir sped lesion healing compared with placebo, valacyclovir sped pain resolution compared with acyclovir, and extending acyclovir from 7 to 21 days added only slight benefit. [1][2][3] Adding prednisolone to acyclovir also gave only slight benefit, and neither the longer course nor the steroid reduced PHN. [3] In the famciclovir placebo trial, people who developed PHN had it for a median of 63 days with famciclovir 500 mg versus 119 days with placebo. [20]

Whether antivirals prevent PHN is less certain. A Cochrane review of six randomized trials with 1,211 participants found high-quality evidence that oral acyclovir does not significantly reduce how often PHN develops, and too little evidence to judge other antivirals. [24] A network meta-analysis notes that early antiviral use has been found to reduce pain, and a meta-analysis of cohort studies linked starting treatment 3 or more days after onset with PHN. [5][6] The trials do not promise a result for any one person. Age, rash location, immune status, pain severity and new lesion formation all matter. [7]

Is it too late to start antivirals for shingles after 72 hours?

Not always. The labels themselves are cautious: valacyclovir and famciclovir state that their effectiveness when started more than 72 hours after rash onset has not been established, and the acyclovir label says there are no data on treatment started after 72 hours. [19][20][21] Because trials did not test later starts, advice after 72 hours rests on expert consensus. [25] Many experts recommend starting treatment even after 3 days if new blisters are still appearing or complications are present, and antiviral therapy is recommended for all immunocompromised patients. [7]

The European guideline suggests starting an antiviral after 72 hours as long as new blisters appear in people at risk of a complicated course or with complications, in people with signs of spread to the skin, organs or nervous system, in eye or ear shingles, and in all immunocompromised people. [25] NIH guidelines for people with HIV advise treating as soon as possible and within 1 week of rash onset, or any time before the lesions have fully crusted. [27] In a trial of shingles around the eye, oral acyclovir started within 7 days of rash onset reduced eye complications. [9]

Who is most likely to benefit from antivirals?

The European guideline recommends antiviral treatment for these groups: [25]

  • Adults 50 and older, with shingles anywhere on the body
  • Shingles on the head or neck
  • Moderate to severe pain, bleeding or dying-skin lesions, more than one nerve segment involved, scattered blisters outside the band, or involvement of mucous membranes
  • A weakened immune system
  • Severe underlying skin disease such as atopic dermatitis

Who needs a lower antiviral dose for shingles?

The active drug from each of these medicines is cleared by the kidneys, so each label lowers the dose when kidney function is reduced. [19][20][21] Acute kidney failure has been reported in older adults with or without reduced kidney function, in people with kidney disease given more than the recommended dose, in people taking other kidney-toxic drugs, and in people who are not well hydrated. [19] The labels advise keeping well hydrated while taking these drugs. [19][21] The European guideline suggests checking creatinine at the start of treatment in anyone with known or suspected kidney problems. [25]

Older adults deserve extra care. The valacyclovir label notes that older patients are more likely to have reduced kidney function needing a lower dose, and more likely to have kidney or nervous system side effects. [19] The famciclovir label recommends no age-based change unless kidney function is reduced. [20]

What are the side effects of shingles antivirals?

In two valacyclovir shingles trials, nausea was reported by 15% of people taking valacyclovir versus 8% on placebo, and headache by 14% versus 12%. [19] In the valacyclovir versus famciclovir trial, safety was similar, with headache and nausea the most common adverse events. [23] Valacyclovir should be stopped if nervous system side effects occur. [19] The valacyclovir label lists no known clinically significant drug or food interactions, while the acyclovir label urges caution with other drugs that can harm the kidneys. [19][21]

Can you take shingles antivirals during pregnancy, while breastfeeding or with a weakened immune system?

Pregnancy

Decades of clinical data with valacyclovir and acyclovir in pregnancy have not identified a drug-associated risk of major birth defects, and famciclovir pharmacovigilance reports have not identified one either. [19][20] The European guideline suggests against starting an antiviral in pregnancy when there is no risk of complications, and suggests acyclovir as the preferred choice when the benefits to the mother outweigh possible risks. [25] Pregnant people with shingles should talk with their own clinician before starting any antiviral.

Breastfeeding

Acyclovir passes into breast milk after valacyclovir, at an estimated infant dose of about 0.6 mg/kg/day when the mother takes 500 mg twice daily, and the label asks that the benefits of breastfeeding be weighed with the mother's need for the drug. [19]

Weakened immune system

About 30% of people hospitalized with shingles have weakened immune systems. [18] The valacyclovir and famciclovir labels state that efficacy has not been established for shingles in immunocompromised patients. [19][20] NIH guidelines for people with HIV recommend oral valacyclovir or famciclovir, or acyclovir as an alternative, for 7 to 10 days, with intravenous acyclovir when skin lesions are extensive or the organs or brain may be involved. [27] The same guidelines define disseminated shingles as three or more dermatomes, more than 20 blisters outside the main and neighboring dermatomes, or organ or brain involvement. [27] Intravenous acyclovir is labeled for varicella-zoster infections in immunocompromised patients. [21]

Are you contagious while taking antivirals for shingles?

Yes, until the blisters dry and scab over. Active shingles lesions spread the virus through direct contact with blister fluid or by breathing in virus particles from the blisters, until they dry and scab over. [18] People with shingles cannot spread the virus before the blisters appear or after the rash scabs over. [28] You cannot catch shingles from someone with shingles, but a person who never had chickenpox or the chickenpox vaccine can catch chickenpox from you. [28]

Antivirals reduce viral shedding, but CDC ties its precautions to the rash scabbing over, not to the number of doses taken. [18][28] CDC advises covering the rash, not touching or scratching it, washing hands often, and avoiding pregnant people who never had chickenpox or its vaccine, premature or low birth weight infants, and people with weakened immune systems until the rash scabs over. [28]

What does not work, or adds little, for shingles?

  • Antiviral cream in place of pills. In a trial in shingles around the eye, oral acyclovir did better than topical acyclovir for eye complications, pain and skin healing, and the European guideline found too little evidence to recommend any specific topical treatment for acute shingles. [25] NIH guidelines reserve topical eye antivirals for late corneal lesions in people not taking oral antivirals. [27]
  • Longer antiviral courses for routine cases. Treating with acyclovir for 21 days instead of 7 gave only slight benefit and did not reduce PHN. [3]
  • Steroids to prevent nerve pain. Adding prednisolone gave only slight short-term benefit, did not reduce PHN, and caused more adverse events. [3] Acyclovir plus prednisone improved quality-of-life measures in adults over 50, but pain over 6 months did not differ. [4] NIH guidelines do not recommend added steroids for shingles in people with HIV. [27]
  • Numbing creams or capsaicin on the acute rash. The European guideline suggests against local anesthetics or capsaicin for acute shingles. [25]
  • Counting on antivirals alone to prevent PHN. Oral acyclovir does not significantly reduce how often PHN develops. [24]

How long does shingles last with treatment?

The rash usually appears within 2 to 4 days of the first pain or tingling, crusts in 7 to 10 days, and symptoms resolve within 2 to 4 weeks. [26] New lesions typically keep appearing over 3 to 5 days, and the skin itself heals in 2 to 4 weeks. [7][18][22] Pain can last longer than visible skin changes. PHN, pain lasting more than 90 days after symptoms start, affects 10% to 18% of people who have shingles, and estimates vary with age and with how PHN is defined. [7][11][26] The main risk factor for PHN is older age; severe pain or rash at onset also raises the risk. [7][8]

Is shingles near the eye an emergency?

It needs same-day attention. Shingles in the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve can lead to keratitis, uveitis and other eye complications, sometimes with lasting loss of vision. [8][9][10] Lesions on the forehead or upper eyelid together with lesions on the tip of the nose, or any new visual symptoms, call for evaluation by an ophthalmologist. [7] Blisters on the tip of the nose suggest the eye may be involved, and NIH guidelines say everyone with shingles in this nerve branch should have an eye exam. [27] Because eye inflammation can appear later, the NIH guidelines advise follow-up eye exams even after the skin heals. [27] CDC advises diagnosing and treating early to prevent progressive corneal involvement and potential vision loss. [18]

How is shingles pain managed?

For the acute rash, over-the-counter or prescription pain relievers may help, and wet compresses, calamine lotion and warm oatmeal baths may relieve itching. [28] For persistent PHN, the 2026 AAFP review lists gabapentinoids, serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors and tricyclic antidepressants as first-line options, with topical lidocaine or capsaicin as add-on treatment. [26] Trials support gabapentin, pregabalin, selected tricyclic antidepressants and the lidocaine patch for PHN. These drugs have different side effects and must be individualized. [12][13][14][15][16][17]

When are antivirals not enough?

Antivirals do not replace eye care, treatment for bacterial skin infection, neurologic assessment, or hospital evaluation for severe or disseminated disease. Intravenous acyclovir is recommended for immunocompromised people who need hospital care and for severe neurologic complications, and in immunocompromised people shingles can spread into a generalized rash. [7][22] Shingles complications include PHN, hearing or vision loss, encephalitis and pneumonia. [18] Shingles can also inflame brain blood vessels, and the risk of stroke is highest in the weeks to months after an episode. [27]

Which shingles cases can be handled by video, and which need in-person or emergency care?

Shingles is usually distinctive enough to diagnose by appearance once the rash is present, which is why a clear, limited rash can often be assessed by video. Without a rash, a clinical diagnosis may not be possible. [18] All three antivirals are prescription medicines, so a clinician needs to decide whether one is right for you. [19][20][21]

Often suitable for a video visit

  • A one-sided band of blisters on the chest, back, abdomen, arm or leg, away from the face
  • Normal immune function, and kidney function that is normal or known
  • Questions about whether to start or continue an antiviral, side effects, or pain control

Needs in-person care, the same day if possible

  • Rash on the forehead, eyelid or tip of the nose [7][27]
  • Rash in or around the ear, especially with facial weakness or loss of taste [27]
  • A weakened immune system, or a rash spreading beyond one band [25][27]
  • Pain without a rash, where the diagnosis is uncertain [18]
  • Spreading redness, warmth or pus that could mean a bacterial skin infection [22]

Call 911 or go to an emergency department for

  • Vision changes or eye pain with a facial rash [18][27]
  • Confusion, severe headache, seizure, or sudden weakness, numbness or trouble speaking [18][27]
  • Trouble breathing, or a widespread rash with fever, especially with a weakened immune system [18][22][27]
