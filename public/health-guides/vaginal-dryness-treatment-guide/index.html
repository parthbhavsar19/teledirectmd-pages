Vaginal Dryness & GSM: Causes, Vaginal Estrogen & Treatment Options

Genitourinary syndrome of menopause: causes, non-hormonal options, vaginal estrogen, and newer FDA-approved treatments.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: February 2026 · Updated: Jul 2026
15 min read

How do you treat vaginal dryness and genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM)?

Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) affects approximately 27% to 84% of postmenopausal women and is likely underdiagnosed and undertreated, per the 2020 NAMS Position Statement. Nonprescription vaginal moisturizers and lubricants give sufficient relief for most women with mild symptoms. For moderate to severe symptoms, NAMS lists low-dose vaginal estrogen, vaginal DHEA (prasterone), systemic estrogen therapy, and ospemifene as effective treatments; ospemifene is taken as one 60 mg tablet with food once daily per its FDA label. For women with a history of breast cancer, ACOG recommends nonhormonal treatment first and says low-dose vaginal estrogen may be used if that has not helped, after discussing risks and benefits; NAMS notes that safety data in breast cancer are insufficient. NAMS also found too few placebo-controlled trials of vaginal laser to recommend it. A licensed clinician, by telehealth or in person, can review the symptoms and prescribe appropriate therapy.

Key Takeaways

  • Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) is the newer clinical term for what was previously called vulvovaginal atrophy.[10] The 2020 NAMS Position Statement estimates it affects approximately 27% to 84% of postmenopausal women and is likely underdiagnosed and undertreated.[1]
  • Non-hormonal options, vaginal moisturizers and lubricants, are the starting point. NAMS says they give sufficient relief for most women with mild symptoms, and ACOG considers them first-line for women with a history of breast cancer.[1][2]
  • Low-dose vaginal estrogen (cream, insert, or ring), vaginal DHEA (prasterone), and oral ospemifene are all effective treatments for moderate to severe GSM, per NAMS. For breast cancer survivors, safety data are incomplete, so the choice is made with the oncologist.[1][2]
  • Oral ospemifene (Osphena) and vaginal prasterone (Intrarosa) are FDA-approved alternatives to vaginal estrogen, each with its own label restrictions, including for women with a history of breast cancer.[7][8]
  • Vaginal laser therapy is not an established treatment. The FDA has warned about devices marketed for "vaginal rejuvenation," and NAMS found too few placebo-controlled trials to recommend laser.[4][1]
  • Vaginal dryness is not exclusive to menopause. Breastfeeding, antihistamines, antidepressants, Sjögren's disease, and cancer treatment can all trigger similar symptoms.

Many patients are surprised to learn that vaginal dryness after menopause is more than a minor inconvenience. It is a recognized medical condition with a formal name and several FDA-approved treatments. The medical community now calls it genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM), a term chosen to replace the older phrase "vulvovaginal atrophy" and to cover the changes that low estrogen causes in the vulva, vagina, and urinary tract together.[10]

What I see most often in clinical settings is patients who quietly tolerate symptoms for years: painful intercourse, dryness, burning, recurrent urinary tract infections. Many assume it is part of getting older, or they are nervous about hormone therapy. The treatment conversation is more straightforward than most patients expect.

This guide covers what GSM is, why it happens, what the evidence supports for treatment, and how telehealth fits into the picture. It draws on the 2020 NAMS GSM Position Statement, current ACOG guidance, and the FDA prescribing information for each medication.

What Is Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause?

The term GSM came out of a 2013 terminology consensus conference cosponsored by the International Society for the Study of Women's Sexual Health (ISSWSH) and the North American Menopause Society (NAMS). The boards of both societies formally endorsed it in 2014, describing it as a more medically accurate, all-encompassing, and publicly acceptable term than "vulvovaginal atrophy."[10]

GSM describes a collection of symptoms and signs linked to a decrease in estrogen and other sex steroids, involving the labia, clitoris, vaginal opening, vagina, urethra, and bladder.[10] Estrogen helps maintain the thickness, elasticity, lubrication, and acid pH of vaginal tissue. When estrogen drops, the vaginal lining thins, the pH rises, lubrication decreases, and the tissue becomes more fragile and prone to irritation.

The 2020 NAMS Position Statement found that GSM affects approximately 27% to 84% of postmenopausal women.[1] That wide range reflects how differently women experience and report symptoms. In the AGATA study of 913 women attending routine gynecologic examinations, 79.1% were diagnosed with GSM, yet only 30% had ever received a previous diagnosis.[11] That gap is one of the central reasons GSM remains undertreated.

How GSM Differs from Hot Flashes

Hot flashes and night sweats, the vasomotor symptoms of menopause, typically ease over time. GSM behaves differently. In the AGATA study, GSM prevalence was 64.7% one year after menopause and 84.2% six years after menopause, and nearly all women who had been diagnosed earlier still had the condition.[11] This is why GSM is worth treating rather than waiting out.

Symptoms of GSM

Symptoms span three overlapping groups: genital, sexual, and urinary.[10]

  • Genital symptoms: dryness, burning, and irritation
  • Sexual symptoms: lack of lubrication, discomfort or pain with sex (dyspareunia), and impaired sexual function
  • Urinary symptoms: urgency, burning with urination (dysuria), and recurrent urinary tract infections

Not every woman experiences all of these, and the symptoms must be bothersome and not better explained by another diagnosis.[10] Some patients present mainly with sexual symptoms; others come in because of recurrent UTIs. Recognizing both clusters as part of the same process matters for treatment, because treating the low-estrogen state can address both at once.

Causes Beyond Menopause

Menopause is the most common cause of GSM, but estrogen deficiency is not limited to menopause. Several other situations produce a similar hormonal environment and similar symptoms.

Breastfeeding

Lactation suppresses estrogen through elevated prolactin levels, creating a temporary hypoestrogenic state that closely resembles menopause. Breastfeeding women frequently report vaginal dryness and painful intercourse that resolves once they stop nursing and estrogen levels recover. This is entirely normal, and it's worth flagging for postpartum patients who may otherwise worry there is something wrong.

Medications

Antihistamines (diphenhydramine, loratadine, cetirizine) have drying effects on mucous membranes throughout the body, including the vagina. Women who take antihistamines regularly for allergies often notice increased dryness. Certain antidepressants, particularly SSRIs and SNRIs, can reduce vaginal lubrication. Aromatase inhibitors, used in breast cancer treatment, deliberately suppress estrogen production and commonly cause GSM symptoms.

Sjögren's Syndrome

Sjögren's disease is an autoimmune condition that targets moisture-producing glands. Women with Sjögren's disease report vaginal dryness 2 to 3 times more often than women of the same age without the disease.[5] Treatment of vaginal dryness in Sjögren's disease includes vaginal moisturizers, so moisturizers and lubricants remain important parts of care in this group.[5]

Cancer Treatment

Chemotherapy can cause ovarian failure and abrupt menopause in premenopausal women. Pelvic radiation damages vaginal tissue directly. Both can produce GSM symptoms that may be severe. Managing vaginal health proactively during and after cancer treatment is an important but often neglected part of survivorship care.

Premature Ovarian Insufficiency

Women with primary ovarian insufficiency (POI), an early decline in ovarian function, can develop urogenital atrophy and other effects of low estrogen decades earlier than typical. ACOG recommends systemic hormone therapy for women with POI who have no contraindications, both to treat symptoms of low estrogen and to reduce long-term risks such as osteoporosis and cardiovascular disease, continued until about the average age of natural menopause (age 50 to 51).[16] A specialist evaluation is worthwhile.

Non-Hormonal Options: Moisturizers and Lubricants

For mild symptoms, and as a foundation of treatment regardless of what else you add, non-hormonal vaginal products are the starting point. NAMS notes that nonhormone therapies available without a prescription provide sufficient relief for most women with mild symptoms.[1] There's a meaningful distinction between moisturizers and lubricants that many patients don't know, and getting it right makes a difference.

Vaginal Moisturizers

Vaginal moisturizers are used on a regular schedule, not just before sex, to maintain tissue hydration over time. They do not replace estrogen or reverse the tissue changes of GSM, but ACOG cites evidence supporting their effectiveness for temporary relief of vaginal dryness.[2] Small studies have shown relief of vaginal atrophy symptoms with hyaluronic acid products, particularly when used three to five times per week.[2]

Consistency matters. The studies above used moisturizers on a regular schedule, so treat a moisturizer like a skincare routine rather than an occasional fix.

Lubricants for Sexual Activity

Lubricants are used during sexual activity to reduce friction in the moment. They do not provide the sustained tissue hydration that a moisturizer offers, but they are important for comfort during intercourse. A 2025 review in Canadian Family Physician describes three main types: oil-, silicone-, and water-based products.[6]

Type Pros Cons Notes
Water-based Condom-compatible, easy to clean, widely available May dry out quickly; some formulas contain irritating additives Recommended, along with silicone-based products, for people who use condoms or who have recurrent vaginal infections or irritation. Choose products free of harmful ingredients and within recommended osmolality and pH ranges.[6]
Silicone-based Long-lasting, water-resistant, smooth Not compatible with silicone sex toys; harder to wash off Also recommended for people who use condoms or who have recurrent vaginal infections or irritation.[6]
Oil-based Moisturizing, natural options available (coconut oil, vitamin E) Not among the types recommended for condom users Not the recommended choice if you use condoms or are prone to vaginal infections or irritation.[6]

Osmolality deserves more attention than it typically gets. It measures the concentration of dissolved particles in a product, and lubricants vary widely. The Canadian Family Physician review recommends silicone- or water-based lubricants that are free of harmful ingredients and within recommended osmolality and pH ranges, especially for people who use condoms or have recurrent vaginal infections or irritation.[6] If a product does not list these values, ask a pharmacist or choose one that does.

Low-Dose Vaginal Estrogen: What You Need to Know

Low-dose vaginal estrogen is the treatment I turn to most often for patients with moderate to severe symptoms. The 2020 NAMS Position Statement lists low-dose vaginal estrogens, along with vaginal DHEA, systemic estrogen therapy, and ospemifene, as effective treatments for moderate to severe GSM.[1]

The word "estrogen" understandably gives some patients pause. The key point is that low-dose vaginal estrogen is applied locally and is not the same as systemic hormone therapy. The Estring label, for example, notes that estrogen exposure with the ring is generally lower than with systemic estrogens used for hot flashes.[15] Labels still differ by product: the Vagifem prescribing information carries a boxed warning on endometrial cancer, cardiovascular disorders, probable dementia, and breast cancer, and lists breast cancer or a history of breast cancer as a contraindication.[14] Your prescriber should review the label for the specific product you use.

Do You Need Progesterone With Vaginal Estrogen?

Usually not, though the evidence has limits. NAMS states that when low-dose vaginal estrogen, vaginal DHEA, or ospemifene is used, a progestogen is not indicated, but endometrial safety has not been studied in clinical trials beyond 1 year.[1] The Vagifem label is more cautious and advises prescribers to consider adding a progestogen for women with a uterus to reduce the risk of endometrial cancer.[14] Persistent or recurring abnormal genital bleeding after menopause should always be evaluated.[7]

How Long Until It Works?

Improvement builds gradually because the vaginal lining rebuilds over time. In the prasterone trials, for example, the main outcomes were assessed after 12 weeks of daily use.[8] GSM reflects an ongoing low-estrogen state, so talk with your physician before stopping a treatment that is working.

Vaginal Estrogen and Breast Cancer Survivors

This is the conversation I have most carefully in clinical practice. Women who have had breast cancer often develop GSM, sometimes because chemotherapy or endocrine therapy lowers estrogen, and many are told they cannot use estrogen of any kind. The evidence is more nuanced than that, and it is also incomplete.

ACOG's Clinical Consensus on urogenital symptoms after estrogen-dependent breast cancer recommends nonhormonal treatment first. If nonhormonal treatments have not adequately helped, low-dose vaginal estrogen may be used, including in women taking tamoxifen, after a discussion of risks and benefits.[2] ACOG adds that available evidence suggests low-dose vaginal estrogen is safe in individuals with a history of hormone receptor–positive breast cancer who are at low risk of recurrence.[2] NAMS is more cautious, stating that there are insufficient data to confirm the safety of vaginal estrogen, DHEA, or ospemifene in women with breast cancer.[1] FDA labeling matters here too: the Vagifem label lists a history of breast cancer as a contraindication.[14]

Observational data are mostly reassuring. In a U.S. claims database study of 42,113 women with GSM after breast cancer, the risk of recurrence within 5 years was comparable between those who used vaginal estrogen and those who did not.[3] A Danish national cohort of 8,461 postmenopausal women treated for early-stage estrogen receptor–positive breast cancer found no overall increase in recurrence or mortality with vaginal estrogen.[9]

The more complicated group is women on aromatase inhibitors (AIs), such as anastrozole, letrozole, and exemestane. In the Danish cohort, women using vaginal estrogen while on adjuvant AIs had a higher risk of recurrence (adjusted relative risk 1.39), though not higher mortality.[9] ACOG says that for women taking AIs, low-dose vaginal estrogen can be used after shared decision-making between the patient, gynecologist, and oncologist.[2]

For Breast Cancer Survivors on Aromatase Inhibitors

Prasterone/DHEA and ospemifene (covered below) are sometimes discussed as alternatives, but neither is a simple answer. The Osphena label says it has not been adequately studied in women with breast cancer and should not be used in women with known or suspected breast cancer or a history of breast cancer.[7] ACOG notes there is no indication that ospemifene increases recurrence risk, but long-term safety data are limited.[2] The Intrarosa label states it has not been studied in women with a history of breast cancer.[8] Any hormonal option here should be decided together with your oncologist.

Ospemifene (Osphena): The Oral Option

Ospemifene (brand name Osphena) is an oral estrogen agonist/antagonist. It is FDA-approved for moderate to severe painful intercourse and for moderate to severe vaginal dryness, both symptoms of vulvar and vaginal atrophy due to menopause. The labeled dose is one 60 mg tablet taken by mouth with food once daily.[7]

According to its label, ospemifene binds to estrogen receptors and has tissue-selective effects: it activates estrogen pathways in some tissues and blocks them in others. In the endometrium (uterine lining), it has estrogen-like effects.[7]

Ospemifene can be a reasonable fit for women who:

  • Prefer an oral medication over vaginal application
  • Have difficulty with vaginal insertion due to severe atrophy or other conditions
  • Prefer a pill that is not an estrogen product

Ospemifene carries a boxed warning for endometrial cancer and cardiovascular disorders, including stroke and deep vein thrombosis.[7] It should not be used with undiagnosed abnormal genital bleeding, estrogen-dependent cancer, active or past blood clots (deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism), active or past stroke or heart attack, or pregnancy.[7] Its label also advises using it for the shortest duration consistent with treatment goals and risks, so it requires a thorough medication and history review before prescribing.[7]

Prasterone / DHEA Vaginal Insert (Intrarosa)

Prasterone (brand name Intrarosa) is a vaginal insert containing dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA). It is FDA-approved for moderate to severe painful intercourse (dyspareunia), a symptom of vulvar and vaginal atrophy, due to menopause, and its label lists 2016 as its initial U.S. approval.[8] The dose is one 6.5 mg insert once daily at bedtime.[8][2]

Prasterone itself is an inactive steroid that the body converts into active androgens and/or estrogens, and its label notes that the exact mechanism in vulvar and vaginal atrophy is not fully established.[8] In the label's pharmacokinetic data, blood levels of testosterone and estradiol were slightly higher with the insert than with placebo.[8] This is a different approach from vaginal estrogen (which delivers estrogen directly) or ospemifene (which works through the bloodstream).

In the trials behind its approval, the main outcomes were painful intercourse, vaginal cell changes, and vaginal pH, measured from baseline to week 12.[8] It should not be used with undiagnosed abnormal genital bleeding, and the label states it has not been studied in women with a history of breast cancer.[8] NAMS notes that long-term studies on the endometrial safety of vaginal DHEA are lacking.[1]

Vaginal Laser Therapy: What the Evidence Shows

Lasers and other energy-based devices have been marketed for "vaginal rejuvenation" and for symptoms related to menopause. You may have seen these advertised at medical spas or gynecology offices. The evidence behind them does not support their routine use.

FDA Warning on "Vaginal Rejuvenation" Devices

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration warned that manufacturers were marketing "vaginal rejuvenation" devices with claims that they treat conditions and symptoms related to menopause, urinary incontinence, or sexual function, even though the safety and effectiveness of these devices for "vaginal rejuvenation" had not been evaluated or confirmed by the FDA. The agency reported finding numerous cases of vaginal burns, scarring, pain during sexual intercourse, and recurring or chronic pain.[4]

NAMS concluded that there are insufficient placebo-controlled trials of energy-based therapies, including laser, to draw conclusions on efficacy and safety or to make treatment recommendations.[1] ACOG notes that laser therapy is neither FDA-approved nor FDA-cleared for treating symptoms related to menopause and that additional research is warranted before recommending it.[2]

If you are being offered vaginal laser therapy for GSM, ask specifically what evidence the provider is citing and whether FDA-approved alternatives have been tried first. That is a reasonable thing to ask.

Impact on Sexual Function and Urinary Health

GSM affects more than physical tissue. It can affect relationships, self-image, and quality of life in ways that patients often carry quietly. In the REVIVE survey of 3,046 U.S. postmenopausal women with vulvar and vaginal atrophy symptoms, symptoms affected enjoyment of sex in 59%, and 24%, 23%, and 23% reported interference with sleep, general enjoyment of life, and temperament, respectively.[12]

Painful intercourse (dyspareunia) deserves direct clinical attention. It is not a normal consequence of aging that women should simply accept. When intercourse is painful, many women avoid sexual activity altogether, which can strain relationships. Adequate lubrication and treatment of the underlying tissue changes can make a real difference.

Urinary Symptoms

The urethra and bladder are part of GSM, and urinary symptoms such as urgency, burning with urination, and recurrent urinary tract infections are included in its definition.[10] For recurrent UTIs, a Cochrane review found that vaginal estrogen reduced the number of postmenopausal women with UTIs compared with placebo in two small trials, though results varied with the type of estrogen and treatment duration; oral estrogen did not reduce UTIs.[13] This is one reason to treat GSM even when urinary symptoms are the main complaint.

If you leak urine with coughing, sneezing, or exercise (stress urinary incontinence), mention it at your visit, because it has its own evaluation and treatment options.

Telehealth for GSM: What Can Be Managed Remotely

Low-dose vaginal estrogen, ospemifene, and prasterone can all be prescribed through a telehealth visit when clinically appropriate. The evaluation centers on a thorough history: symptoms, medical history, current medications, and screening for label contraindications such as undiagnosed genital bleeding, a history of blood clots, or a relevant cancer history.[14][7]

Telehealth is a good fit for many GSM conversations. Many patients are reluctant to raise these symptoms in a busy office visit, and the privacy of a video consultation often makes it easier to talk openly. A pelvic exam can document objective signs, though, so for patients with prior gynecologic problems, unusual symptoms, or uncertain findings, in-person evaluation remains important.

If you have undiagnosed vaginal bleeding, a history of estrogen-dependent cancer, or active blood clots, an in-person evaluation should come first; product labels call for unexplained postmenopausal bleeding to be evaluated before treatment.[8] Telehealth works well for the many patients who have a clear symptom history, no major contraindications, and are ready to start treatment.

Choosing the Right Treatment: A Clinical Framework

No single treatment is right for every patient. The right starting point depends on symptom severity, personal preference, medical history, and whether estrogen-containing products are appropriate. Here is how I approach the decision:

Clinical Situation Recommended Starting Point Notes
Mild dryness or discomfort with intercourse only Vaginal moisturizer + silicone- or water-based lubricant for intercourse Use consistently before deciding it is not enough. Nonprescription options give sufficient relief for most women with mild symptoms.[1]
Moderate to severe dryness, painful intercourse, or urinary symptoms Low-dose vaginal estrogen (insert, ring, or low-dose cream) One of several effective options for moderate to severe GSM, along with vaginal DHEA and ospemifene.[1] Use moisturizer and lubricant as adjuncts.
Prefers oral medication over vaginal application Ospemifene (Osphena) 60 mg once daily with food Requires medication and history review. Not for women with active or past blood clots, stroke or heart attack, or estrogen-dependent cancer.[7]
Breast cancer survivor, responds to non-hormonal treatment Continue vaginal moisturizer + lubricant ACOG considers nonhormonal methods first-line for survivors.[2]
Breast cancer survivor, symptoms persist despite non-hormonal treatment, not on AI Low-dose vaginal estrogen, after oncologist discussion ACOG says it may be used when nonhormonal treatments have not helped, including with tamoxifen.[2]
Breast cancer survivor on aromatase inhibitor Shared decision with your oncologist before any hormonal option ACOG allows low-dose vaginal estrogen after shared decision-making; one cohort found higher recurrence with vaginal estrogen plus AIs.[2][9] The Osphena label says not to use it after breast cancer, and Intrarosa has not been studied in this group.[7][8]
Vaginal dryness due to breastfeeding Lubricants and moisturizers; discuss any vaginal estrogen with your physician Symptoms typically resolve after weaning. Estrogens are present in human milk and can reduce milk production, so weigh any estrogen product with your physician.[14]
