Key Takeaways

  • Adults in the United States average two to three colds a year,[1] while CDC's preliminary estimate for the 2024–2025 flu season is about 45,000 U.S. flu deaths.[3] These are different illnesses that call for different approaches.
  • The hallmark difference: flu symptoms usually come on suddenly,[18] while cold symptoms are usually milder and more likely to include a runny or stuffy nose.[2]
  • Flu antivirals (oseltamivir, baloxavir) work best when started early, especially within 48 hours of symptom onset, but CDC recommends treatment as soon as possible for anyone hospitalized, severely ill, or at higher risk of complications, even if they come in later.[9]
  • For cold symptoms, honey (for children over 1 year) has the clearest evidence for cough;[8] zinc lozenges may shorten colds, though reviews disagree on how much;[6][7] and regular vitamin C slightly shortens colds, while starting it after symptoms begin shows no consistent benefit.[23]
  • Seek emergency care for difficulty breathing, persistent chest pain or pressure, confusion, seizures, or a fever or cough that improves and then returns or worsens.[18]

Here's a statistic that reframes how most people think about these two illnesses: for the 2024–2025 flu season, CDC's preliminary estimates are about 51 million flu illnesses, 710,000 hospitalizations, and 45,000 deaths in the United States alone.[3] Over the course of each year, the average American adult also gets two to three common colds, nuisances that rarely make the news but are one of the most frequent reasons for missed school and work.[1]

Yet in clinical practice, clinical experience shows that most patients use "cold" and "flu" interchangeably. This isn't a trivial distinction. Confusing a cold for the flu can mean missing a critical 48-hour window during which antiviral medications are most effective. And confusing the flu for a cold can mean dangerously underestimating an illness that, for high-risk patients, can progress to pneumonia, organ failure, and death within days.

This guide exists to give you the same information I'd give a family member: how to tell these two illnesses apart, when you actually need treatment versus when you can safely ride it out, and which of the many over-the-counter remedies have real evidence behind them (and which don't). Everything here reflects the latest clinical guidelines through early 2026, including the IDSA influenza treatment guidelines and the CDC's updated respiratory virus recommendations.

Cold vs. Flu: How to Tell the Difference

Colds and flu are both contagious respiratory illnesses, but they are caused by different viruses, and their symptoms overlap enough that it can be hard to tell them apart on symptoms alone. In general, flu is worse than a cold, and its symptoms are more intense and begin more abruptly. Knowing the difference matters because the treatment approach diverges significantly.[2]

The clinical shorthand I teach patients: if your symptoms came on like flipping a light switch, one hour you were fine, the next you're in bed with a high fever and aching from head to toe, that's flu until proven otherwise. If your symptoms crept in gradually, starting with a scratchy throat and progressing to congestion and sneezing over a couple of days, that's almost certainly a cold.

When precision matters, for high-risk patients, during flu season, or when treatment decisions hinge on the diagnosis, special tests can tell whether a person has flu.[2] The IDSA guideline recommends rapid molecular assays over rapid antigen tests (RIDTs) for outpatients, because molecular assays detect influenza more reliably.[4]

What Causes Each: Different Viruses, Similar Spread

The Common Cold

More than 200 respiratory viruses can cause colds. Rhinoviruses are the most frequent cause of colds in the United States. Other causes include common human coronaviruses (not SARS-CoV-2), parainfluenza viruses, adenoviruses, enteroviruses, and human metapneumovirus.[1] This viral diversity is precisely why there's no cold vaccine and no cure, you'd need to target hundreds of viruses simultaneously.

Influenza

The flu, by contrast, is caused by influenza viruses, primarily types A and B. While this is a much narrower group than cold viruses, influenza mutates constantly. Antigenic drift (small mutations) occurs every season, which is why you need a new flu vaccine each year. Antigenic shift (major mutations) happens less frequently but can produce pandemic strains, as occurred in 2009 with H1N1.

How They Spread

Both cold and flu viruses spread through the same mechanisms:

  • Respiratory droplets: Experts think flu viruses spread mainly by droplets made when people with flu cough, sneeze, or talk.[20] Putting physical distance between yourself and others helps lower the risk.
  • Surface contact: Less often, a person can get flu by touching a surface or object that has the virus on it[20] and then touching their eyes, nose, or mouth with unwashed hands.[31]
  • Indoor air: CDC's guidance for people who are sick includes taking steps for cleaner air indoors.[21]

Timing matters for flu in particular. People with flu are most contagious during the first three days of their illness. Flu viruses can be detected in most infected people beginning one day before symptoms develop, and people can remain contagious for five to seven days after becoming sick; young children and people with weakened immune systems may be contagious for longer.[20]

What's Changed: Latest Guidelines on Flu Treatment

Flu treatment has evolved meaningfully in recent years, with updated antiviral options and clearer guidance on who should receive treatment and when. Here's what's most relevant for patients in the 2025–2026 flu season.

The 48-Hour Window, And When to Ignore It

Both the IDSA and CDC recommend starting antiviral treatment as soon as possible. CDC notes that clinical benefit is greatest when treatment starts early, especially within 48 hours of symptom onset,[9] and in the IDSA guideline's evidence summary, treatment started within 6 hours of illness onset reduced symptoms by about 4 days.[4] However, the 48-hour mark is not a hard cutoff. CDC recommends antiviral treatment as soon as possible for anyone with suspected or confirmed flu who is hospitalized, has severe, complicated, or progressive illness, or is at higher risk of complications. Some studies suggest treatment might still benefit hospitalized patients when started up to 4 or 5 days after illness onset,[9] and a pooled analysis of observational studies reported a survival benefit of treatment in hospitalized adults.[4]

CDC Respiratory Virus Guidance: Returning to Normal Activities

CDC's respiratory virus guidance advises staying home and away from others while you have respiratory virus symptoms. You can go back to your normal activities when, for at least 24 hours, your symptoms are getting better overall and you have not had a fever without using fever-reducing medication. After that, take added precautions over the next 5 days, such as wearing a well-fitted mask, keeping physical distance, practicing good hygiene, and taking steps for cleaner air.[21]

Who Is Considered High-Risk for Flu Complications?

CDC recommends antiviral treatment as soon as possible when flu is suspected or confirmed in people at higher risk of complications.[9] CDC's higher-risk groups include:[19]

  • Adults 65 years and older
  • Children younger than 2 years old
  • Pregnant women, including up to 2 weeks after the end of pregnancy
  • People with asthma, chronic lung disease, heart disease, kidney or liver disorders, or endocrine disorders such as diabetes
  • People with a weakened immune system due to disease
  • People with neurologic and neurodevelopmental conditions
  • People with obesity (BMI of 40 or higher)
  • People who live in nursing homes and other long-term care facilities
  • American Indian or Alaska Native persons
  • People younger than 19 on long-term aspirin- or salicylate-containing medications

The Decision Framework: Treat at Home, See a Doctor, or Go to the ER

One of the most common questions I get, especially during peak respiratory season, is some version of "Do I really need to come in for this?" Here's the framework used in practice to help patients decide:

Scenario Recommended Approach Rationale
Mild cold symptoms (stuffy nose, sneezing, mild sore throat) in an otherwise healthy adult Manage at home with rest, fluids, and OTC symptom relief Colds are self-limiting; no specific treatment exists. Symptoms usually peak within 2 to 3 days, and colds usually last less than a week[1]
Suspected flu with mild symptoms in a healthy, non–high-risk adult Consider outpatient evaluation for antiviral treatment if within 48 hours of onset In trials of mostly low-risk outpatients, antivirals shortened fever and symptoms by about 1 to 2 days; the benefit is modest in low-risk patients[4]
Suspected flu in a high-risk patient (see list above) at any point during illness Seek medical care promptly, same day CDC recommends antiviral treatment as soon as possible for higher-risk patients, and starting later can still be beneficial for them[5][9]
Cold symptoms lasting well beyond the usual week without improvement, or symptoms that improve then worsen See a doctor May indicate bacterial sinusitis, secondary bacterial infection, or a non-cold diagnosis
Difficulty breathing, persistent chest pain or pressure, confusion, seizures, or not urinating[18] Emergency department immediately These are signs of severe influenza, pneumonia, or potential sepsis, all time-sensitive emergencies

The key principle the emphasis is on: for colds, the question is almost always "how can I feel better while this runs its course?" For flu, the question is "do I need antiviral treatment, and if so, how quickly can I get it?" The urgency is fundamentally different.

What Your Doctor Is Thinking: Behind the Clinical Reasoning

When you present with respiratory symptoms during flu season, I'm running through a mental algorithm in real time. Understanding this process may help explain why we ask the questions we do, and why the answers matter.

The Key Questions and Why They Matter

  • "When exactly did your symptoms start?", This is the single most important question for flu treatment. If it's been less than 48 hours, antivirals will be most effective. If it's been longer, I need to assess your risk level to decide whether treatment is still indicated.
  • "Did symptoms come on suddenly or gradually?", Sudden onset with fever and body aches points strongly to influenza. Gradual onset with nasal symptoms points to a cold. This distinction often determines whether I test for flu at all.
  • "Do you have a fever, and how high?", Fever is a common flu symptom,[18] while any fever with a cold is usually low grade in adults.[1] A higher fever raises my suspicion for flu, COVID-19, or a bacterial complication, and it also helps gauge severity.
  • "Do you have any chronic medical conditions?", Asthma, COPD, diabetes, heart disease, immunosuppression, any of these moves you into the high-risk category where the treatment threshold is lower and the urgency is higher.
  • "Could you be pregnant?", Pregnancy, including the 2 weeks after it ends, puts people at higher risk of flu complications,[19] and oral oseltamivir is the preferred treatment during pregnancy.[9]
  • "Have you been vaccinated this season?", Vaccination doesn't rule out flu (effectiveness varies by season), but it informs my pre-test probability. An unvaccinated patient with classic flu symptoms during peak season has a very high likelihood of influenza.

When I Order Testing

I don't test every patient with a runny nose. Flu testing is most valuable when it will change management, specifically, when a positive result would lead me to prescribe antivirals. I'm most likely to test when a high-risk patient presents within the treatment window, when the diagnosis is unclear between flu and another condition, or when results would affect infection control decisions (such as in a household with vulnerable members).

Treatment: What Works, What Doesn't, and What's Overhyped

Flu Antiviral Medications

There are currently four FDA-approved antiviral drugs recommended for influenza treatment:[5]

Medication How It's Taken Key Notes
Oseltamivir (Tamiflu) Oral, 75 mg twice daily × 5 days (adults)[15] The workhorse. Preferred treatment during pregnancy, and CDC does not routinely recommend the other three antivirals for hospitalized patients.[9] In adult trials it shortened time to first symptom relief by about 16.8 hours.[11] Most common side effects: nausea, vomiting, headache.[9]
Baloxavir marboxil (Xofluza) Oral, single weight-based dose (40 mg for 20 to under 80 kg; 80 mg for 80 kg or more)[16] The newest option, one pill, one time. Approved for treatment in ages 5 and older.[16] CDC does not recommend it during pregnancy or breastfeeding, or for hospitalized patients because of limited data,[5] and does not recommend it as monotherapy in severely immunosuppressed people.[9]
Zanamivir (Relenza) Inhaled, 10 mg (two 5-mg inhalations) twice daily × 5 days[9] Not for people with underlying airway disease such as asthma or COPD, because of the risk of bronchospasm. Approved for treatment in ages 7 and older.[9]
Peramivir (Rapivab) IV, single dose[9] Approved for acute uncomplicated flu in ages 6 months and older; CDC does not routinely recommend it for hospitalized patients.[9]

A note on older medications: amantadine and rimantadine are not recommended. They are active only against susceptible influenza A viruses (influenza B is inherently resistant), and high levels of adamantane resistance among circulating influenza A viruses make them unsuitable for treatment.[4]

Cold Symptom Relief: The Evidence Scorecard

There is no cure for the common cold. But that doesn't mean everything is equally (in)effective. Here's what the evidence actually says about the most popular remedies:

Zinc lozenges, possible benefit, debated evidence. The 2024 Cochrane review on zinc for the common cold found that zinc used for treatment may reduce the duration of ongoing colds, but rated that evidence as low certainty and found more non-serious side effects with zinc.[6] In a 2024 article critiquing the review, Hemilä and Chalker argued that the review pooled very different products (lozenges and nasal zinc at very different doses); restricting the analysis to zinc lozenges, they calculated that lozenges shortened colds in adults by 37%, and found no evidence so far that zinc lozenges shorten colds in children.[7] They also stress that formulation matters: lozenges are meant to dissolve slowly in the mouth, and ingredients such as citric acid can bind the zinc so it is not released.[7] The practical takeaway: if you try zinc, use lozenges, not nasal products. Side effects of lozenges are mostly bad taste and mouth irritation.[7] Avoid intranasal zinc: a case series described 17 patients who lost their sense of smell after using intranasal zinc gluconate gel.[30]

Honey, Moderate evidence for cough. A 2018 Cochrane review of children aged 12 months to 18 years found that honey probably relieves cough symptoms better than no treatment, diphenhydramine (Benadryl), and placebo, but may make little or no difference compared to dextromethorphan (an ingredient in many OTC cough remedies).[8] A 2021 systematic review in BMJ Evidence-Based Medicine found honey was superior to usual care for symptoms of upper respiratory tract infections, improving cough frequency and cough severity.[24] Important safety note: honey is not advised for infants up to 12 months old, because bacteria that may be present in honey can cause paralysis.[8]

Vitamin C, Weak evidence for treatment, modest for prevention. The Cochrane review on vitamin C (Hemilä and Chalker, 2013) found that regular supplementation of at least 200 mg a day shortened colds by 8% in adults and 14% in children, but did not reduce how often colds occur in the general community.[23] Starting vitamin C after symptoms begin showed no consistent effect on the duration or severity of colds. The exception is people under heavy physical stress (marathon runners, skiers, and soldiers on subarctic exercises), in whom regular vitamin C roughly halved the risk of catching a cold.[23] Regular moderate supplementation may have a small benefit, but it won't abort an active cold.

OTC decongestants, antihistamines and pain relievers, Symptom relief, not cure. A Cochrane review of nasal decongestants (most trials used pseudoephedrine or oxymetazoline) found that multiple doses may have a small effect on nasal congestion in adults, of uncertain clinical relevance.[26] Oral phenylephrine is a different story: an FDA advisory committee unanimously concluded that current data do not support its recommended oral dose as a nasal decongestant, and FDA has proposed removing oral phenylephrine as an OTC nasal decongestant ingredient because it is not effective.[29] Antihistamines used alone gave a limited short-term benefit (days one and two) on overall symptoms, with no clinically meaningful effect on nasal congestion, runny nose, or sneezing.[27] NSAIDs such as ibuprofen help headache, ear pain, and muscle and joint pain, but did not shorten colds or clearly ease respiratory symptoms.[28] These treat symptoms; none is a cure.

Echinacea, Weak and inconsistent evidence. A Cochrane review found that Echinacea products have not been shown to provide benefits for treating colds, although a weak benefit from some products is possible.[25] I don't recommend it, but I don't discourage patients who feel it helps them.

Antibiotics, No benefit, potential harm. Both colds and flu are viral infections. Antibiotics treat bacteria, not viruses. Prescribing antibiotics for these illnesses does not shorten the illness, does not prevent complications, and exposes patients to unnecessary side effects while driving antibiotic resistance.

Prevention: Evidence-Based Strategies That Actually Work

Influenza Vaccination

Annual flu vaccination is the core of flu prevention. CDC recommends that everyone 6 months and older in the United States, with rare exception, get a flu vaccine every season.[22]

Vaccine effectiveness varies from season to season,[22] and I understand why some patients question whether it's "good enough." Consider the 2024–2025 season, which CDC classified as high severity: vaccine effectiveness against outpatient flu visits ranged from 40% in adults 65 and older to 60% in children 6 months to 4 years, and CDC's preliminary estimates are that vaccination prevented about 10 million illnesses, 5 million medical visits, 180,000 hospitalizations, and 12,000 deaths.[3] Even when the vaccine doesn't prevent infection, studies show it reduces the severity of illness in people who get vaccinated but still get sick.[22]

Hand Hygiene

Frequent handwashing with soap and water, scrubbing for at least 20 seconds, is a simple habit that helps against both cold and flu. If soap and water are not readily available, use a hand sanitizer with at least 60% alcohol.[31] The critical habit is cleaning your hands before touching your face, because germs spread when people touch their eyes, nose, and mouth with unwashed hands.[31]

Respiratory Hygiene and Environmental Measures

  • Cover coughs and sneezes with your elbow or a tissue, not your hands.
  • Stay home when sick. Per CDC's respiratory virus guidance, you can return to normal activities once, for at least 24 hours, your symptoms are getting better overall and you have had no fever without fever-reducing medication. Then take added precautions for the next 5 days.[21]
  • Improve indoor ventilation. Open windows, use HEPA air purifiers, and when possible, gather outdoors rather than in enclosed spaces during peak respiratory season.
  • Clean frequently touched surfaces, doorknobs, light switches, phones, keyboards, especially when someone in the household is sick.

The 48-Hour Window: When to Take Tamiflu

The single most important rule about Tamiflu (oseltamivir) is timing. It is FDA-approved for treatment in people who have had flu symptoms for no more than 48 hours,[15] and CDC notes that clinical benefit is greatest when antiviral treatment starts early, especially within 48 hours of symptom onset.[9]

Earlier is better even inside that window. The IDSA guideline reports that in both adults and children, neuraminidase inhibitor treatment started within 6 hours of illness onset reduced symptoms by about 4 days, while the placebo-controlled trials that enrolled mostly low-risk outpatients within 36 to 48 hours of onset showed fever and symptoms shortened by about 1 to 2 days.[4]

Two important exceptions to the 48-hour rule
  • Hospitalized patients and patients at high risk of complications should still receive antivirals if they present after 48 hours. CDC recommends treatment as soon as possible for anyone hospitalized, severely ill, or at higher risk, and notes that treatment might still benefit hospitalized patients when started up to 4 or 5 days after illness onset.[9]
  • Pregnant patients are in a higher-risk group,[19] and as for other higher-risk patients, the decision to start treatment should not wait for laboratory confirmation of flu.[9]

Does Tamiflu Actually Work? What the Evidence Shows

Yes, but modestly, and the size of the benefit is smaller than many patients expect.

The most rigorous synthesis is the 2014 systematic review by Jefferson and colleagues, published in the BMJ, which analyzed full clinical study reports obtained from the European Medicines Agency and Roche rather than relying only on published trial summaries. In adult treatment trials, oseltamivir shortened the time to first alleviation of flu symptoms by approximately 16.8 hours (95% CI 8.4 to 25.1 hours; P less than 0.001), or about half a day to a day.[11]

The same review found no difference in hospital admissions in adult treatment trials, and no significant reduction in bronchitis, ear infections, sinusitis, or complications classified as serious; a reduction in unverified pneumonia did not hold up in the trials that used a more detailed diagnostic form.[11] That is why CDC reserves its firm treatment recommendation for patients who are hospitalized, severely ill, or at higher risk, while treatment of otherwise healthy outpatients is something to consider when it can start early.[9]

How Long Does Tamiflu Take to Work?

On average, oseltamivir speeds recovery modestly rather than switching the illness off. In adult treatment trials it shortened the time to first alleviation of symptoms by about 16.8 hours,[11] and CDC describes flu antivirals as shortening the time you are sick by about a day.[5] In the CAPSTONE-1 trial, patients who received a single-dose antiviral (baloxavir, in this trial) took a median of about 54 hours to alleviation of symptoms, versus about 80 hours on placebo, so even with active treatment expect two to three days before feeling substantially better.[10]

Cough can linger after other symptoms improve, which is not by itself a sign the medication failed. A fever or cough that improves and then returns or worsens is different: it is an emergency warning sign that needs prompt care.[18]

Tamiflu vs Xofluza (Baloxavir): Head-to-Head

Xofluza (baloxavir marboxil) is the newer option, a single-dose oral antiviral that FDA first approved in October 2018 for people 12 and older[9] and that is now approved to treat uncomplicated flu in people 5 years and older.[16] It has a different mechanism (it inhibits the flu virus's cap-dependent endonuclease)[10] and a very different administration schedule (one dose, not ten).

The 2025 CENTERSTONE trial in the New England Journal of Medicine (1,457 index patients and 2,681 household contacts) found that when baloxavir was given to the sick person, laboratory-confirmed transmission to a household contact by day 5 was 9.5% versus 13.4% with placebo (adjusted odds ratio 0.68). Transmission that caused symptoms was 5.8% versus 7.6%, a difference that was not statistically significant. The trial authors note that studies of neuraminidase inhibitors such as oseltamivir have not shown sufficient evidence that they prevent transmission.[12] Separately, the 2020 BLOCKSTONE trial in NEJM showed that a single baloxavir dose given to household contacts as post-exposure prophylaxis reduced clinical influenza in those contacts from 13.6% to 1.9%.[14]

Xofluza's practical appeal is the one-dose schedule, and in CAPSTONE-1 adverse events were no more common with baloxavir than with placebo.[10] Its main downsides are resistance emergence (variants with reduced susceptibility appeared in 9.7% of baloxavir recipients in CAPSTONE-1,[10] and more often in children 5 to under 12 in label data[16]) and CDC's recommendation against it in pregnancy and while breastfeeding.[5] Efficacy specifically in high-risk outpatients (adolescents and adults with at least one risk factor for complications) was studied in the 2020 CAPSTONE-2 trial in the Lancet Infectious Diseases, which found single-dose baloxavir had superior efficacy to placebo and similar efficacy to oseltamivir for easing flu symptoms; the authors concluded that the results support early therapy for high-risk patients to speed recovery and reduce complications.[13] For most non-pregnant adults, either is a defensible choice; for pregnant patients, oseltamivir is the preferred option per CDC and IDSA guidance.[9][4]

Tamiflu Side Effects and How to Reduce Nausea

Oseltamivir is generally well tolerated. The dominant side effects are gastrointestinal, and these are the ones that most often bother patients.

  • Nausea and vomiting. In adult treatment trials, nausea occurred in about 3.7% and vomiting in about 4.6% more patients on oseltamivir than on placebo.[11] The label notes that tolerability may be enhanced if it is taken with food.[15]
  • Headache. A small increase over placebo in prophylaxis trials (risk difference about 3%).[11]
  • Neuropsychiatric events. The FDA label describes postmarketing reports of delirium and abnormal behavior leading to injury, reported primarily among pediatric patients; CDC notes these were mainly reported in Japanese pediatric patients.[15][9]
  • Skin reactions. Uncommon but reported: serious skin reactions including toxic epidermal necrolysis, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, and erythema multiforme. Stop the medication and seek care if an allergic-type reaction or widespread rash develops.[15]

Practical tips: take each dose with food and sip water throughout the day. If nausea or vomiting keeps you from keeping doses down, contact your clinician.

How to Get a Tamiflu Prescription Online (via Telehealth)

Because the 48-hour window is so tight and most primary care schedules are not, telehealth is often the most practical way to get a Tamiflu prescription during flu season. A video visit with a physician who can review symptoms, check for red flags, and send an e-prescription to your pharmacy can often happen the same day.

The workflow, honestly, is straightforward:

  1. Book a same-day visit. Symptom onset should be within the last 48 hours, ideally less than 24 hours.
  2. Video visit with a physician. The clinician assesses whether flu is likely. Testing can help, but CDC advises that decisions about starting antiviral treatment should not wait for laboratory confirmation.[9]
  3. Screen for red flags. Chest pain, severe shortness of breath, altered mental status, dehydration, or high-risk conditions may push toward in-person evaluation instead of oral treatment at home.
  4. E-prescription. Oseltamivir is e-prescribed to the pharmacy of your choice. It is not a controlled substance.
  5. Start the same day. Every hour matters; do not wait to start if you can pick up the medication that afternoon.

TeleDirectMD can prescribe oseltamivir via same-day video visit in 40+ states plus DC, adults only, non-controlled. Cost is $79 cash or in-network with BCBS, Aetna, and UHC. That is prescription price, not medication price; the medication is dispensed and priced by the pharmacy.

What telehealth cannot do: it cannot evaluate a critically ill patient. If breathing is difficult, oxygen saturation is dropping, or the patient looks sick enough that a physical exam is needed, an in-person visit (urgent care or ED) is the right call.

Red Flags: When to Seek Emergency Care

Seek Immediate Medical Attention If You Experience:
  • Difficulty breathing or shortness of breath, this is the most critical warning sign and should never be dismissed
  • Persistent pain or pressure in the chest or abdomen
  • Persistent dizziness, confusion, or inability to arouse
  • Seizures
  • Not urinating
  • Severe muscle pain
  • Severe weakness or unsteadiness
  • Fever or cough that improve but then return or worsen
  • Worsening of chronic medical conditions[18]
In Children, Additional Warning Signs:
  • Fast breathing or trouble breathing, or ribs pulling in with each breath
  • Bluish lips or face
  • Dehydration (no urine for 8 hours, dry mouth, no tears when crying)
  • Not alert or interacting when awake
  • Severe muscle pain (child refuses to walk)
  • Fever above 104°F that is not controlled by fever-reducing medicine, or any fever in a baby younger than 12 weeks[18]

The flu's most dangerous complications include pneumonia, inflammation of the heart (myocarditis), brain (encephalitis), or muscle tissue, sepsis, and multi-organ failure such as respiratory and kidney failure.[18] A pattern I warn patients about specifically: if you felt like you were getting better, then a fever or cough returns or worsens, that is a CDC emergency warning sign[18] and can signal a complication such as pneumonia. It requires urgent evaluation.

Frequently Asked Questions

The most reliable differentiator is symptom onset and intensity. Flu symptoms usually come on suddenly and can include fever or chills, cough, sore throat, runny or stuffy nose, muscle or body aches, headaches, and fatigue.[18] Cold symptoms are usually milder, and people with colds are more likely to have a runny or stuffy nose. If your symptoms came on like a light switch, especially with fever and muscle aches, flu is more likely. Symptoms alone can be hard to tell apart; special tests can tell whether a person has flu.[2]

Antivirals work best when started early, especially within 48 hours of symptom onset. For otherwise healthy outpatients the benefit is modest: in placebo-controlled trials of mostly low-risk outpatients treated within 36 to 48 hours, treatment shortened fever and symptoms by about 1 to 2 days.[4] For people who are hospitalized, have severe, complicated, or progressive illness, or are at higher risk of complications, CDC recommends treatment as soon as possible even if more than 48 hours have passed, and treatment might still benefit hospitalized patients when started up to 4 or 5 days after illness onset.[9] The short answer: do not let the 48-hour mark stop you from seeking care if you're in a high-risk group or getting sicker.

The only scenario where antibiotics may be appropriate is if you develop a secondary bacterial complication such as bacterial sinusitis, strep throat, an ear infection, or bacterial pneumonia.

Flu vaccine effectiveness can vary from season to season.[22] In the 2024–2025 season, CDC estimated vaccine effectiveness against outpatient flu visits ranged from 40% in adults 65 and older to 60% in children 6 months to 4 years.[3] Even when vaccinated people do get the flu, studies show vaccination reduces the severity of their illness.[22] For the 2024–2025 season, CDC's preliminary estimates are that vaccination prevented about 10 million illnesses, 180,000 hospitalizations, and 12,000 deaths in the United States.[3] CDC recommends a flu vaccine every season for everyone 6 months and older, with rare exception.[22]

Most colds and many flu cases can be managed at home or in an outpatient setting. For flu, CDC's emergency warning signs in adults include difficulty breathing or shortness of breath, persistent pain or pressure in the chest or abdomen, persistent dizziness, confusion or inability to arouse, seizures, not urinating, severe muscle pain, severe weakness or unsteadiness, fever or cough that improve but then return or worsen, and worsening of chronic medical conditions. In children, warning signs also include fast or labored breathing, bluish lips or face, ribs pulling in with each breath, dehydration, not being alert or interacting when awake, fever above 104°F that is not controlled by fever-reducing medicine, and any fever in a baby younger than 12 weeks.[18]

Most people who get flu recover in a few days to less than two weeks.[18] In adult treatment trials, oseltamivir shortened the time to first alleviation of symptoms by an average of 16.8 hours, per the 2014 systematic review by Jefferson and colleagues in the BMJ, so expect roughly a half-day to full-day acceleration.[11] Cough can linger after other symptoms improve.

Oseltamivir speeds recovery modestly rather than switching the illness off. In adult treatment trials it shortened the time to first symptom relief by about 16.8 hours on average,[11] and CDC describes flu antivirals as shortening the time you are sick by about a day.[5]

When checked on November 7, 2026, GoodRx listed generic oseltamivir 75 mg (10 capsules, one adult 5-day course) at $21.98 with its coupon, against a listed retail price of $57.26.[17] For brand Tamiflu or Xofluza, ask your pharmacy for a price; prices vary by pharmacy and change often.

Tamiflu (oseltamivir) is a 5-day, twice-daily course; Xofluza (baloxavir) is a single dose. Both are FDA-approved for uncomplicated flu in people who have had symptoms for no more than 48 hours.[15][16] Oral oseltamivir is the preferred treatment in pregnancy, and CDC does not recommend baloxavir in pregnancy or while breastfeeding.[9][5] In the CAPSTONE-1 trial, time to symptom relief was similar with baloxavir and oseltamivir, and variants with reduced susceptibility to baloxavir emerged in 9.7% of baloxavir recipients.[10]

For adults and adolescents 13 and older with normal kidney function, oseltamivir treatment is 75 mg by mouth twice daily for 5 days.[15][9] The dose is reduced to 30 mg twice daily when creatinine clearance is over 30 to 60 mL/min, to 30 mg once daily when it is over 10 to 30 mL/min, and adjusted further for dialysis.[15] Taking it with food may improve tolerability.[15]
