Impetigo in Children

Note for parents: TeleDirectMD currently sees adults 18 and older only. For a child, contact your pediatrician or an in-person pediatric urgent care.

A physician-reviewed parent guide to symptoms, treatment, MRSA risk, weight-based dosing, and school return.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: September 13, 2026
11 min read

For children 0 to 17 years.

What is impetigo in children, and how is it treated?

Impetigo is a bacterial skin infection caused by group A Streptococcus, Staphylococcus aureus, or both, and it is most common in children 2 through 5 years old ([3]). It typically appears as reddish sores, often around the nose and mouth, that form a honey-colored crust ([4]). More than 90% of bullous impetigo cases affect children younger than 2 ([6]). For limited bullous or nonbullous impetigo, the IDSA guideline recommends topical mupirocin or retapamulin twice daily for 5 days. Oral therapy is recommended for numerous lesions, outbreaks, and ecthyma, as a 7-day course: dicloxacillin or cephalexin for methicillin-susceptible infection, and doxycycline (not recommended under age 8), clindamycin, or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole when MRSA is suspected or confirmed ([1]). CDC says people with impetigo can return to school or daycare once they have started antibiotic treatment and all sores on exposed skin are covered ([3]); some schools and clinicians ask for a longer wait.

Key Takeaways

  • Impetigo is a bacterial skin infection caused by Staphylococcus aureus, Group A Streptococcus, or both, and it is most common between ages 2 and 5.
  • For limited disease, the IDSA guideline recommends topical mupirocin or retapamulin twice daily for 5 days; numerous sores, outbreaks, and deep infection (ecthyma) call for a 7-day course of oral antibiotics.
  • For methicillin-susceptible infection, IDSA recommends cephalexin or dicloxacillin; when MRSA is suspected or confirmed, clindamycin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, or doxycycline (not recommended under age 8).
  • CDC says children can return to school or daycare once they have started antibiotics and sores on exposed skin are covered; school, daycare, and clinician rules vary.
  • The two things not to miss: MRSA, and in a child with eczema, eczema herpeticum; both change management and may need in-person care.

Impetigo is one of those childhood rashes that looks worse than it is. A few honey-colored crusts around a child's nose can alarm any parent, but with the right antibiotic it usually clears within a week. Here is what it actually is, when it needs treatment, and when it is something more serious.

When to Get Emergency Care

Take a child to a pediatric emergency department or urgent in-person evaluation for any of these:

Any age (age-independent red flags)

  • Fever with rapidly spreading redness, warmth, or streaking beyond the sore borders (concern for cellulitis or invasive Group A strep)
  • Widespread fragile blistering with skin sloughing or a "wrinkled paper" appearance (concern for Staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome, SSSS) ([10])
  • Grouped vesicles or "punched-out" erosions in a child with eczema, especially with fever or malaise (concern for eczema herpeticum, a dermatologic emergency)
  • Painful, deep ulcers rather than superficial crusted sores (possible ecthyma)
  • Any child with an underlying immune deficiency
  • Facial swelling that involves the eye or crosses to the other side of the face

Newborns (0 to 28 days)

Any suspected skin infection in a newborn should be evaluated in person, urgently. Bullous impetigo is typically seen in newborns, and SSSS can appear as early as 48 hours after birth ([10]).

Infants under 3 months

Any fever plus a skin infection warrants same-day pediatric evaluation, not telehealth.

Symptoms: What an Impetigo Rash Looks Like

Impetigo is a bacterial skin rash. Parents often search for "impetigo rash" because that is what the visible change is: a rash made of sores that crust over. It has three forms, and each looks different:

Classic (nonbullous) impetigo: 70% of cases

The most common form, accounting for about 70% of cases ([9]). Sores and blisters usually appear around the nose and mouth, and can also appear on the scalp, hairline, arms, or legs. The sores burst and form the characteristic honey-colored crust ([6]). Itching and soreness are usually mild. Lesions can spread to other body areas by touch or shared items.

Bullous impetigo

Larger fluid-filled blisters, often on the trunk of infants and young children ([4]). Bullous impetigo is typically seen in newborns, and the S. aureus toxins involved act only at the infection site, unlike in SSSS ([10]). More than 90% of cases affect children younger than 2 ([6]).

Ecthyma

A deeper, more serious form that penetrates deeper into the skin, causing painful fluid- or pus-filled sores that turn into deep ulcers. It can leave scars ([4]). The IDSA guideline recommends treating ecthyma with an oral antibiotic ([1]).

Causes and How It Spreads

Two bacteria cause almost all impetigo:

  • Staphylococcus aureus (staph), the most common cause, including community-acquired MRSA in some regions
  • Group A Streptococcus (strep, S. pyogenes)

Both organisms can be present together ([3]). The bacteria often enter the skin through a small cut, insect bite, or rash ([4]), but impetigo can also infect skin that is not broken ([6]).

Close contact with another person with impetigo is the most common risk factor ([3]). It spreads easily within families, in crowded settings such as schools and child care facilities, and in sports that involve skin-to-skin contact ([4]).

Age epidemiology

  • Impetigo overall: the most common skin infection in children between ages 2 and 5, and about 10% of skin conditions in children ([6])
  • Bullous impetigo: more than 90% of cases affect children younger than 2 ([6])
  • Older children and adolescents also develop impetigo, particularly athletes in contact sports

Higher-risk groups

Children with scabies ([3]) or eczema ([4]), or other disruptions of the skin barrier; children in crowded living environments; and children in hot, humid climates ([9]). Athletes in sports with skin-to-skin contact are also at higher risk ([4]).

Impetigo vs Eczema: How to Tell Them Apart

Parents often confuse the two because both show up as a rash in young children, and children with eczema (atopic dermatitis) are more likely to develop impetigo ([4]). Disruption of the skin barrier is a recognized impetigo risk factor ([9]).

  • Impetigo is an infection. It appears as sores that open and crust over with the telltale honey-colored scab, usually around the nose and mouth. It is contagious and clears with antibiotics.
  • Eczema (atopic dermatitis) is a chronic inflammatory condition, not an infection. It appears as dry, red, itchy patches, often in the creases of the elbows and knees. It is not contagious and does not respond to antibiotics unless it has become secondarily infected.

The diagnostic question that matters: in a child with eczema, a sudden change from "itchy dry patches" to "oozing, crusting, or spreading sores" suggests impetigo on top of the eczema. That needs evaluation. If it instead looks like clusters of painful blisters, seek urgent care for possible eczema herpeticum, a widespread herpes simplex infection in atopic dermatitis that can cause life-threatening complications ([11]).

When to See a Clinician

See a clinician within 24 hours:

  • Suspected impetigo with a few well-defined sores, no fever, no rapid spread
  • A honey-crusted lesion after a recent scratch or insect bite
  • Confirmation that a rash a school or daycare has flagged is or is not impetigo

Escalate to in-person, same-day evaluation:

  • Any red flag listed under "When to get emergency care"
  • Child under age 5 (young children benefit from in-person evaluation)
  • Widespread sores across multiple body regions
  • Deep, painful, ulcerated lesions
  • Facial lesions near the eye or on the eyelids
  • Recurrent impetigo in the same child
  • Any child with underlying immunosuppression
  • Suspected MRSA (see risk factors below)

What's Changed in the Evidence

  • This guide follows the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) skin and soft tissue infection guideline, 2014 update, which recommends topical mupirocin or retapamulin for limited impetigo and oral therapy for numerous lesions, outbreaks, or ecthyma ([1]).
  • A 2026 American Family Physician review lists topical mupirocin 2% or retapamulin 1% ointment as the recommended initial treatments, with oral antibiotics for outbreaks or for severe, multilesional disease that does not respond to topical therapy within 3 to 5 days ([9]).
  • Ozenoxacin, a topical quinolone first approved by the FDA in 2017, is labeled for impetigo in patients 2 months and older and is active against S. aureus, including methicillin-resistant isolates ([13]). Direct comparisons with currently recommended therapies are lacking ([2]), and the 2026 American Family Physician review reports that it was discontinued in the United States as of September 2025 ([9]).
  • A Cochrane review found that bacteria causing impetigo show growing resistance to commonly used antibiotics worldwide ([8]).

Treatment

Treatment choice depends on how many sores there are, whether the infection is deep (ecthyma), whether there is an outbreak, and whether MRSA is suspected or confirmed ([1]).

Topical antibiotics (limited disease)

For a limited number of lesions, the IDSA 2014 SSTI guideline recommends topical mupirocin or retapamulin twice daily for 5 days (strong recommendation, high-quality evidence) ([1]). The FDA label for mupirocin ointment directs application 3 times daily for up to 10 days, and re-evaluation of patients not showing a clinical response within 3 to 5 days ([14]); follow the prescribing physician's instructions. Ozenoxacin cream is FDA-labeled for impetigo due to S. aureus or S. pyogenes, applied twice daily for five days, in patients 2 months and older ([13]), but it has been reported as discontinued in the United States ([9]).

In a Cochrane review, topical mupirocin was slightly superior to oral erythromycin, and topical mupirocin was at least as effective as oral treatment; it is unclear whether oral antibiotics work better for extensive impetigo ([8]).

Before applying topical medicine: soak the area in warm water or apply a wet compress for a few minutes, then gently remove any scabs so the antibiotic can get into the skin ([5]).

Oral antibiotics (extensive disease, ecthyma, or MRSA)

The IDSA 2014 guideline recommends oral therapy for patients with numerous lesions, for outbreaks affecting several people, and for ecthyma. The oral course is a 7-day regimen with an agent active against S. aureus ([1]).

Specific choice, dose, and duration should always be individualized by the prescribing physician and cross-checked against the child's weight, allergies, and local antibiogram.

Wound care alongside antibiotics

  • Place a nonstick bandage or light gauze over the sores to help prevent spread ([5])
  • Wear gloves when applying antibiotic ointment and wash hands thoroughly afterward ([4])
  • Wash the child's clothes, linens, and towels every day in hot water ([4])
  • Trim the child's fingernails short to prevent damage from scratching ([4])
  • Do not share personal items, such as towels or sports equipment, while the child is contagious ([5])

MRSA risk factors

The IDSA guideline recommends doxycycline, clindamycin, or TMP-SMX when MRSA is suspected or confirmed ([1]). CDC lists these as raising MRSA risk ([12]):

  • Skin-to-skin contact with others, such as in contact sports
  • Attending daycare or school
  • Exposure to crowded and unhygienic places
  • Hospitalizations or nursing home stays

Only a laboratory test of a sample from the infection can confirm MRSA ([12]).

When Can My Child Return to School or Daycare?

CDC says people with impetigo can return to work, school, or daycare once they have started antibiotic treatment, and they should cover all sores on exposed skin ([3]). Other sources advise a longer wait: a 2026 American Family Physician review advises not returning to work or school until 12 to 24 hours after starting antibiotics or until clinical improvement ([9]), Cleveland Clinic advises staying home from school for 48 hours after starting an antibiotic ([6]), and Mayo Clinic advises keeping a child home until the doctor says the child is no longer contagious ([4]).

Schools and daycares set their own rules, so check your specific school or daycare policy. If the sores are not improving on treatment, the child needs re-evaluation; the mupirocin label advises re-evaluating patients not showing a clinical response within 3 to 5 days ([14]).

Complications

Impetigo itself is usually mild. Complications are uncommon but include:

  • Cellulitis: spreading redness, warmth, tenderness, sometimes fever. Requires oral antibiotics and often in-person evaluation
  • Staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome (SSSS): a toxin-mediated illness with superficial skin blistering and peeling, mostly in children younger than 5. Severe, generalized cases need hospitalization and intravenous antibiotics ([10])
  • Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis: a kidney complication of strep infection that very rarely follows impetigo ([3]). The IDSA guideline recommends systemic antibiotics for infections during outbreaks of post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis ([1])
  • Eczema herpeticum: in children with atopic dermatitis, a rash that looks like impetigo may actually be a disseminated herpes simplex infection, which can cause life-threatening complications ([11]) and needs urgent in-person care
  • Scarring: ecthyma sores can leave scars ([4]); the usual crusted sores heal without leaving a scar ([3])

Prevention

  • Cover cuts, scrapes, insect bites, and eczema patches while they heal
  • Wash hands regularly, especially after wound contact
  • Do not share towels, sheets, razors, or sports gear during known outbreaks
  • Wash contact-sport uniforms and equipment after every use
  • Treat scabies promptly; people with scabies are at increased risk for impetigo ([3])
  • Manage eczema well; children with atopic dermatitis are more likely to develop impetigo ([4])

Age-Specific Notes

Newborns (0 to 28 days)

Any suspected skin infection in a newborn requires urgent in-person evaluation. Bullous impetigo is typically seen in newborns ([10]). Telehealth is not appropriate.

Infants (1 to 12 months)

Bullous impetigo is most common in this age group. Any suspected impetigo in an infant should be evaluated in person by a pediatrician.

Toddlers and preschoolers (2 to 4 years)

This is the peak incidence group for classic impetigo. For this age, see a pediatrician in person.

School-age children (5 to 12 years)

Photograph-supported virtual evaluation can be appropriate for uncomplicated cases without red flags, though many families prefer in-person care for a first-time rash. Watch for outbreaks in schools and daycares; MRSA risk is elevated when close contacts have had skin abscesses.

Adolescents (13 to 17 years)

Athletes in sports with skin-to-skin contact are at higher risk of impetigo ([4]) and MRSA ([12]). Ask about team outbreaks and shared equipment.
