Uric Acid Levels

A physician's guide to serum urate: what the number means, what counts as high or normal, and the treatment targets that reduce gout.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed September 8, 2026
17 min read

What is a normal uric acid level, and what is considered high?

Serum urate (uric acid) is usually reported in milligrams per deciliter (mg/dL). Normal ranges vary by laboratory; MedlinePlus lists 3.0 to 7.1 mg/dL for women and 4.0 to 8.6 mg/dL for men. For gout, the number that matters most is the saturation point of about 6.8 mg/dL, above which urate crystals can form. Definitions of hyperuricemia vary; the 2020 ACR guideline uses 6.8 mg/dL or higher. In a long-term study of men, annual gout incidence was 0.1 percent with urate below 7.0 mg/dL and 4.9 percent at 9 mg/dL or above. For people on urate-lowering therapy, the 2020 ACR target is a urate below 6 mg/dL; the guideline does not set a lower target for tophaceous gout. A normal reading during a flare does not rule out gout, and a high reading alone does not diagnose it.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated September 8, 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • Serum urate above about 6.8 mg/dL is above the saturation point for urate crystals, and gout results from chronic elevation above that point. [9][5]
  • Definitions of hyperuricemia vary (the 2020 ACR guideline uses 6.8 mg/dL or higher), and many people with high uric acid never have health problems from it. [1][8]
  • Serum urate can be normal during an acute flare: in one hospital cohort, 39.8 percent of people with gout had a normal level during an attack. [10]
  • Gout risk rises with urate: in one cohort of men, annual gout incidence was 0.1 percent below 7.0 mg/dL and 4.9 percent at 9 mg/dL or higher. [3]
  • The 2020 ACR treatment target on urate-lowering therapy is below 6 mg/dL; the guideline does not set a lower target for tophaceous gout. [1]
  • Some diuretics, alcohol, obesity, kidney failure and a purine-rich diet can raise uric acid; some medicines, such as losartan, can lower it. [7]

What Is Uric Acid (Urate)?

Uric acid is a normal waste product that the body makes when it breaks down chemicals called purines. Purines come from the body's own cells when they die and from certain foods and drinks. Normally, uric acid dissolves in the blood and is filtered out by the kidneys.[8] Lab reports may name the same blood test uric acid, serum urate, or UA.[8]

Urate itself is not the enemy; every person has a level. The problem is when it stays high enough, long enough that it begins to crystallize.[5] The biology of gout is really about the solubility of monosodium urate, not about a toxic chemical level in the ordinary sense.

How Uric Acid Is Measured

Uric acid is measured on a blood sample.[7] Results are usually reported in milligrams per deciliter (mg/dL), and sometimes in millimoles per liter.[4] The test is done to see whether the blood uric acid level is high,[7] and serial measurements guide the dose of urate-lowering treatment.[1]

It is important to know when the level was drawn. In one hospital cohort of 221 people with gout, 39.8 percent had a normal serum urate during an acute attack, which is why a normal reading taken in the middle of an attack can be misleading.[10] The ACR/EULAR gout classification criteria prefer a level measured more than 4 weeks after an episode starts, off urate-lowering treatment, and suggest retesting under those conditions when practical.[4]

Reference Ranges and the 6.8 Threshold

Normal ranges vary by laboratory. The MedlinePlus Medical Encyclopedia lists normal values of 3.0 to 7.1 mg/dL for women and 4.0 to 8.6 mg/dL for men.[7] The physical constant that matters most for gout is the saturation point of urate, about 6.8 mg/dL. Above it, crystals can form in and around joints; when urate is lowered below it, deposited crystals slowly dissolve.[9]

This is why the treatment target for gout sits below the saturation point: crystals dissolve below about 6.8 mg/dL, and with certainty below 6 mg/dL.[9] It is also why "normal" on a lab report is not the same thing as "on target" for someone with gout: the ACR target of below 6 mg/dL[1] is well below the top of the reference ranges above.[7]

What Counts as High (Hyperuricemia)

There is no single definition of hyperuricemia. The 2020 ACR guideline defines asymptomatic hyperuricemia as a serum urate of 6.8 mg/dL or higher with no prior gout flares or tophi.[1] An analysis of U.S. survey data (NHANES 2015 to 2016) used above 7.0 mg/dL in men and above 5.7 mg/dL in women; by that definition, hyperuricemia affected about one in five U.S. adults, while gout affected 3.9 percent.[2] The large gap between the two numbers is the key point: many people have high uric acid without having health problems.[8]

This is why an elevated urate by itself, without gout, is generally watched rather than treated.[3][1] The 1987 Normative Aging Study, which followed 2,046 initially healthy men for 14.9 years, showed the dose-response clearly: annual gout incidence was 0.1 percent below 7.0 mg/dL, 0.5 percent from 7.0 to 8.9, and 4.9 percent at 9 or above, with a 22 percent five-year cumulative incidence in the highest group.[3]

How High Urate Causes Gout

Gout is not simply a high number on a lab sheet. It is a crystal-deposition disease that results from chronic elevation of uric acid above the saturation point for monosodium urate crystal formation. It usually first shows up as severely painful attacks of joint inflammation, and over time joint damage and tophi (crystal deposits under the skin) can develop.[5]

This two-step logic, first sustained hyperuricemia, then crystal formation and inflammation, explains the two treatment strategies: lower urate below the saturation point so deposited crystals slowly dissolve,[9] and treat a flare with anti-inflammatory medicines such as colchicine, NSAIDs, or glucocorticoids.[1]

These bands are interpretive guidance drawn from the sources above, not diagnosis.[1][3][9] A number alone does not diagnose gout. A level drawn during a flare can be normal,[10] and many medicines can interfere with blood test results.[7]

Treatment Targets: Below 6 mg/dL

For people taking urate-lowering therapy, the 2020 ACR guideline strongly recommends a treat-to-target strategy: the dose is adjusted using serial serum urate measurements to achieve and maintain a urate below 6 mg/dL.[1] Breaking from earlier ACR and EULAR guidelines, it does not set a lower threshold for people with more severe disease, such as tophi. It notes that lower levels hasten the resolution of tophi, but that no trial data support a specific lower threshold.[1] MedlinePlus notes that the goal for people treated for gout may be below 6 mg/dL or below 5 mg/dL, depending on clinical factors.[7]

When and How Often to Test

A urate level is part of the evaluation for suspected gout; the blood test helps diagnose gout, usually together with joint-fluid analysis.[8] In clinical practice, joint or tophus aspiration remains an essential part of establishing the diagnosis.[4] Once urate-lowering therapy begins, the guideline calls for serial urate measurements to guide dose titration and later dosing until the target is reached and maintained, with the level checked after each dose change.[1] During a flare, a level can be normal,[10] so for diagnostic purposes it is ideally repeated more than 4 weeks after the episode starts.[4]

These influences explain why a single number is only part of the story.[7][1] Gout risk also reflects genetic predisposition, diet, other health conditions, and medicines that can influence uric acid levels.[5] Among blood pressure medicines, diuretics were associated with higher gout risk and losartan with lower risk in a large UK case-control study,[6] and the 2020 ACR guideline conditionally recommends switching from hydrochlorothiazide and choosing losartan when feasible for people with gout.[1]

When Urate Is Too Low

Low levels of uric acid in the blood are uncommon and usually do not cause health problems.[8] Lower-than-normal levels can be linked to a low-purine diet, some medicines (such as losartan, fenofibrate, and probenecid), and uncommon conditions such as Fanconi syndrome or SIADH.[7] A clinician can decide whether a low level needs any investigation.
