Alaska Wildfire Smoke and Your Lungs

A physician's field guide for people living through smoke season in Fairbanks, the Interior, the Bush, and Southcentral. What Alaska smoke actually does to your body, how to read Alaska DEC monitor data, action steps for asthma and COPD, and when a village or Interior resident needs care beyond the community clinic.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD Last reviewed: August 13, 2026 Reading time: ~14 min

Key Points

  • A 2025 statewide analysis found that the Interior has been the region of Alaska most severely affected by wildfire smoke. Northern Interior Alaska logged over 300 cumulative days of poor air quality from 2003 to 2020, with smoke waves lasting as long as 43 days.[1]
  • The Fairbanks North Star Borough has been in nonattainment for PM2.5 since 2009 and is currently classified by EPA as a Serious nonattainment area, a problem tied to winter wood smoke from home heating and temperature inversions.[3][13][22]
  • A 2024 meta-analysis linked wildfire smoke to premature deaths and respiratory illness, with the strongest links to cardiovascular mortality and to asthma hospitalizations and emergency visits.[4] A 2025 Medicare cohort study found that long-term smoke PM2.5 was linked to heart failure at a notably higher rate per microgram than non-smoke PM2.5.[5]
  • Alaska DEC regulatory-grade monitors are the most accurate readings for smoke decisions. Low-cost community sensors fill gaps in rural areas, but DEC says they are best for relative comparisons and trends rather than exact values, because humidity, temperature, fog and ice fog can affect them.[6][14]
  • Rural Alaska has randomized-trial evidence for telemedicine. A 2022 Lancet Global Health cluster-randomized trial in 15 rural Alaska communities found that telemedicine specialty referral roughly doubled the share of children who received follow-up after a positive school hearing screen (68.5% vs 32.1%). The authors suggest telemedicine might apply to other preventive school-based services.[7]
  • For severe trouble breathing, chest pain, confusion or drowsiness, or signs of a heart attack or stroke, call 911 or get emergency care. Telehealth is not the right first step for these in Alaska any more than it is anywhere else.[18][24] Get to a clinic, hub hospital, or medevac. This is the point where weather delays and geography turn early into essential.

This is a guide for Alaskans. If you want the general wildfire smoke playbook, most of it, including the AQI grid, mask science, HEPA and Corsi-Rosenthal box details, and the recovery timeline, lives in the general Wildfire Smoke and Your Health guide. This one covers what is different in Alaska: the fire regime, the air itself, the geography of care, and the specific steps a resident of Fairbanks, Tok, Bethel, or Nome should take when smoke arrives.

The Alaska Fire Regime Is Not the Lower 48

Alaska's boreal fire regime is dominated by lightning. A synthesis of Alaska fire records found that human-ignited fires fell to about 5% of area burned in the 1990s and 2000s, and that an average of 767,000 hectares burned each year during the 2000s, 50% more than in any previous decade since the 1940s.[2] Smoke reaches far beyond the burn: in years when over 2 million acres burned, 86% to 98% of Alaska census tracts recorded at least one day of moderate smoke.[1] The Alaska Interagency Coordination Center tracks each season's fires in real time.

Two features of the Alaska fire regime matter for your lungs:

  1. Deep burning of organic soils. In black spruce forests, late-season fires and fires on well-drained sites burn deeper into the surface organic layers.[2] Per microgram, smoke PM2.5 has also been linked to more heart failure than other PM2.5: in a national Medicare cohort, the risk per 1 µg/m³ of long-term smoke PM2.5 was notably higher than for non-smoke PM2.5.[5]
  2. Duration and repeat exposure. Northern Interior Alaska had over 300 cumulative days of poor air quality from 2003 to 2020 (about 10% of summer days), with smoke waves lasting as long as 43 days.[1] This is a chronic-exposure pattern, not a single-event acute pattern, and that changes how you plan your medications and your indoor air strategy.

Why Interior Alaska Air Gets So Bad, So Fast

Fairbanks already has a winter air quality problem. DEC names wood smoke from home heating in the winter, coupled with temperature inversions and other natural conditions, as a source of the area's PM2.5 pollution.[22] The Fairbanks North Star Borough has been in nonattainment for PM2.5 since 2009 and is classified as a Serious nonattainment area.[3][13] In summer, wildfires have become the sole driver of air quality exceedances in Alaska.[1] The practical implication: smoke in the Interior can last for weeks, so plan on multi-day indoor time rather than assuming one bad day will pass.

Southeast and Southcentral Alaska have different patterns. Most wildfire activity is concentrated in the Interior, but nearly every community in Alaska has experienced wildfire smoke, because smoke can travel hundreds of miles beyond the burned area. Urban census tracts in Southcentral were among those flagged for high smoke exposure and vulnerability.[1] The same underlying respiratory and cardiovascular risks apply during the days when smoke is present.

Who Is at Highest Risk in Alaska

The Alaska-specific risk map layers on top of the general vulnerable-population list. In Alaska:

  • Elders with chronic disease living in Interior or Bush communities. Long-term wildfire smoke exposure was linked in a 2025 Journal of the American College of Cardiology Medicare cohort study (covering the contiguous U.S., not Alaska) to a per-microgram increase in incident heart failure, with stronger associations in women, Medicaid-eligible people, and residents of lower-income areas.[5] In Alaska, smoke days from 2015 to 2019 were linked to higher odds of asthma-related emergency visits among people over 65 and Alaska Native people, and to heart failure-related visits among Alaska Native people.[21]
  • People with asthma or COPD. CDC lists people with asthma and COPD among those most at risk from smoke.[12] Wildfire smoke is linked to more asthma hospitalizations and emergency visits,[4] and in Anchorage and Fairbanks asthma-related emergency visits rose on smoke days.[21] A 2025 review notes that telehealth technologies have been shown to be effective for asthma management, and that doubling the inhaled corticosteroid dose can reduce severe exacerbations, with doses that depend on age and severity.[8]
  • Pregnant Alaskans, infants, and small children. CDC lists pregnant people and children among those most at risk from wildfire smoke.[12] Children's high metabolic rates, narrow airways, and ongoing physical development make them more susceptible to respiratory symptoms.[8]
  • People whose work or subsistence activity is outdoors. Guides, commercial fishers, construction workers, subsistence hunters and gatherers, and firefighters themselves; CDC lists responders among the groups most at risk.[12] Rescheduling is often not an option in a short Alaska summer, so the emphasis shifts to N95 use, midday retreat to filtered air, and rescue-medication readiness.
  • Village residents without a resident clinician. More than 500 Community Health Aides or Practitioners (CHA/Ps) serve more than 170 Alaska communities as the patient's first contact in the Alaska Tribal Health System, working with a physician or other provider.[9] During smoke events, distance to care becomes a risk of its own; an Alaska study found evidence that rural populations may delay seeking care.[21]

Reading Alaska Air Monitors

The Alaska DEC Air Monitoring and Quality Assurance Program operates and oversees air quality monitoring networks throughout Alaska and issues air advisories when hazardous air conditions occur.[6] Its 2025 network plan lists PM2.5 monitoring sites in Fairbanks, North Pole, Anchorage, Palmer, and Juneau, plus additional wildland fire monitoring sites, and describes a focus on PM2.5 during wildfire seasons.[13] DEC issues air quality advisories for pollution events throughout the state, including wildfires.[15] DEC also runs a community-based network of low-cost sensors placed in rural communities. DEC cautions that these sensors are more sensitive to humidity and temperature, that fog and ice fog can cause artificially high readings, and that they are best for relative comparisons and trends; only regulatory-grade monitors meet full accuracy and quality-assurance requirements.[14] Public maps such as AirNow's Fire and Smoke Map and the PurpleAir map are useful for local awareness, especially in villages without a regulatory monitor, but read them the same way.

The color grid below shows the EPA Air Quality Index categories, which run from Good (0 to 50) to Hazardous (301 and higher); in the Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups range (101 to 150), members of sensitive groups may experience health effects.[16] EPA updated the PM2.5 concentrations behind these categories effective May 6, 2024; for example, Hazardous now begins at 225.5 µg/m³ of PM2.5.[17] The actions in each box are this guide's advice. Sensitive groups (people with asthma, COPD, heart disease or other chronic conditions, pregnant people, children, and older adults) should shift one category to the left of the general public.[4][12] In Interior Alaska during heavy events, that means moving indoors at AQI 101, not 151.

Action Steps During a Smoke Event

The general playbook, monitor AQI, stay indoors, filter the air you can, mask when you must be outside, does apply in Alaska. What follows is the Alaska-specific overlay.

1. Set Up a Clean Room Early in the Season, Not During an Event

Because Interior smoke can linger for weeks, do the room-preparation work in May, not in July. Pick a room you can close off from outside air, use high-efficiency filters (preferably MERV 13 or higher) in your central system if it can use them, and set up a portable air cleaner or filter in that room.[19] In EPA testing, a DIY Corsi-Rosenthal box with four 1-inch MERV 13 filters and a cardboard shroud reached a clean air delivery rate of 401 (plus or minus 31), and EPA found shrouded, multi-filter DIY designs more effective at removing particles than higher-priced commercial units.[10] Change filters often during smoke events, because dirty filters made DIY units almost completely ineffective, and never leave a DIY box fan unit running unattended.[10][19] In a village or Bush setting where shipping is slow and expensive, the DIY version may be the practical option.

2. Refill Rescue and Controller Inhalers Before Smoke Arrives

In many Alaska communities the pharmacy is not next door. If you use albuterol, budesonide-formoterol, tiotropium, or similar respiratory medications, refill in May or June. If you take cardiac medications, do the same. CDC also advises storing a 7 to 10-day supply of prescription medicines in a waterproof, childproof container to take with you if you evacuate.[18] A late refill during a smoke event, complicated by weather-delayed mail, is a common way to run out. If an asthma attack needs more quick-relief medicine than your plan recommends, contact your clinician right away, and if quick-relief medicine is not helping, you are in the red zone.[20][24]

3. Have a Written Asthma or COPD Action Plan

The single most useful piece of paper during a smoke event is a written action plan you and your clinician agreed on in advance, and CDC advises following your asthma action plan during wildfire smoke.[18] A standard plan has a green zone (no coughing, wheezing, chest tightness, or shortness of breath), a yellow zone (one or more of those symptoms), and a red zone (very short of breath, or quick-relief medicines have not helped). CDC's template says to go to the hospital or call 911 right away for danger signs such as trouble walking or talking because of shortness of breath, or lips or fingernails that are blue, pale, or gray.[20] Your clinician sets the yellow-zone step-up for a smoke trigger; a 2025 review notes that doubling the inhaled corticosteroid dose can reduce severe exacerbations and that oral corticosteroids are effective for managing asthma attacks.[8] Specific doses and how long to take them are individual and should be set with a clinician in advance, not improvised during an event. If you are using quick-relief medicine twice a week or more, tell your clinician.[24]

4. Use the Air-Quality Monitor Nearest You, and Adjust for Sensitive Groups

If you are in a sensitive group (asthma, COPD, heart disease, pregnancy, young children, or older adults), take action one AQI category earlier than the general public.[12][16] In Fairbanks and the Interior, that usually means moving indoors and reducing exertion by AQI 101, not 151. In Anchorage or Juneau, the same principle applies.

5. Cold Weather and Smoke Overlap Matters

Cold weather is a common asthma trigger, so smoke and cold can add up.[24] If you have exercise-induced or cold-induced bronchoconstriction, plan for both, and if your plan includes it, take your quick-relief medicine just before physical activity to help prevent symptoms when you must be outside.[24]

If You Live in a Village: The Layered Care Model

Rural Alaska is served by the Alaska Tribal Health System through a layered model. Community Health Aides or Practitioners (CHA/Ps) are the patient's first contact within the network of health professionals in the Alaska Tribal Health System, and they work with a physician or other provider to make choices about treatment, medication, stabilization, and transport. The Community Health Aide program began nearly 60 years ago and today includes more than 500 CHA/Ps serving more than 170 communities.[9]

The 2022 Lancet Global Health cluster-randomized trial in 15 rural Alaska communities in the Bering Strait School District shows what telemedicine referral can add. Children who screened positive for possible hearing loss or ear disease were followed for 9 months: 68.5% in the telemedicine referral communities received follow-up, versus 32.1% with standard primary care referral, and among children who were seen, mean time to follow-up was 41.5 days versus 92.0 days.[7] The authors suggest telemedicine might apply to other preventive school-based services to improve access to specialty care for rural children.

For a smoke event specifically, what this means in practice:

  • Contact your village clinic first. The CHA/P works with a physician or other provider on treatment, medication, stabilization, and transport decisions.[9]
  • The regional hub hospital that serves your area provides physician backup.
  • For patients who are not Alaska Native beneficiaries and who do not have local clinic access, private telehealth is a reasonable first step for step-up asthma or COPD care, rescue-medication prescriptions, and evaluation of new respiratory symptoms during a smoke event. It is not a substitute for in-person or emergency care when the situation warrants that.

When Telehealth Is Enough in Alaska, and When It Is Not

Telehealth handles a large share of what comes up during a smoke event: cough that will not resolve, sinus and eye irritation, asthma symptoms responding partly to a rescue inhaler and needing a step-up, refills for a controller inhaler that ran out, a short prednisone course for a known action plan, or evaluation of a new rash from smoke exposure. It also handles baseline chronic-disease management (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia) that should not be paused during a smoke season, particularly for elders in Interior and Bush communities.

Telehealth is not the right first step, in Alaska or anywhere else, for:

  • Chest pain, chest pressure, or new severe shortness of breath[18][24]
  • Asthma attack not responding to your quick-relief inhaler[20]
  • Blue lips or fingertips, confusion or drowsiness, or fainting[24]
  • Any stroke-like symptoms[18]
  • Pregnancy: decreased fetal movement, contractions, vaginal bleeding, or severe headache
  • Fever above 102°F with rigors or hemodynamic instability

In much of Alaska, the escalation path from telehealth or the village clinic goes: call the regional hub for physician backup, arrange transport (fixed-wing, medevac, or road) as weather allows, and monitor pulse oximetry at home if you have a device. During smoke events, weather itself can delay medevac. That is a reason to call sooner, not later.

Protective Actions Checklist for Alaska Households

Before smoke season (May)
  • Refill rescue and controller inhalers, and any cardiac medications
  • Confirm you have a written asthma or COPD action plan, or schedule a visit to create one
  • Install a MERV-13 filter in central HVAC if you have one, and buy or build at least one HEPA-equivalent or Corsi-Rosenthal air cleaner
  • Lay in a supply of NIOSH Approved N95 respirators in sizes that fit each member of the household[18][19]
  • Identify your primary air-quality monitor: the nearest DEC station, plus AirNow and one local PurpleAir sensor if available
During a smoke event
  • Stay indoors during Unhealthy or worse conditions, and use your clean room during multi-day stretches
  • Sensitive groups (asthma, COPD, heart disease, pregnancy, young children, adults 65 and over) act one AQI category earlier than the general population
  • Follow your action plan. If quick-relief medicine is not helping, you are in the red zone[20]
  • Use telehealth or your village clinic for step-up decisions, refills, and new but non-emergent symptoms
  • Escalate immediately for airway, breathing, circulation, or neurologic changes
Weather delays medevac. Call early.

In many Alaska smoke seasons, the same weather patterns that trap smoke also delay fixed-wing and medevac operations. If you are in a village and someone is deteriorating, do not wait to see whether they improve on the assumption you can always fly out later. Contact the village clinic, the regional hub, or emergency services early enough that flight windows can be planned.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Interior Alaska smoke worse than the AQI number suggests?

Two reasons a single day's number can understate the problem. First, duration: a 2025 statewide study found the Interior has been the part of Alaska most severely affected by wildfire smoke, with smoke waves in the Northern Interior lasting as long as 43 days.[1] Fairbanks also has a winter PM2.5 problem tied to home-heating wood smoke and temperature inversions, and the Fairbanks North Star Borough has been in nonattainment for PM2.5 since 2009.[13][22] Second, toxicity: in a large Medicare cohort, each microgram of long-term smoke PM2.5 was linked to a notably higher heart failure risk than the same amount of non-smoke PM2.5.[5] For the most accurate local reading, use a DEC regulatory-grade monitor when one is near you.[14]

I live in a bush village with no clinic today. What do I do if my asthma acts up in smoke?

First step: use your quick-relief inhaler as your action plan directs, get indoors, and if you have a portable air cleaner run it. Second step: call the Community Health Aide at your clinic, or call the regional hub hospital, or use a telehealth visit for an assessment and, if appropriate, a step-up in your medication. Third step, and this is the one people delay too long: if quick-relief medicine is not helping, you have trouble walking or talking because of shortness of breath, or your lips or fingernails are blue, pale, or gray, call 911 or arrange medevac right away.[20] Weather in smoke season can delay evacuation, so err on the side of calling early.

What air quality monitor should I trust in Alaska?

The Alaska Department of Environmental Conservation operates and oversees the state's air monitoring networks, with PM2.5 monitoring sites in Fairbanks, North Pole, Anchorage, Palmer, and Juneau.[6][13] Only regulatory-grade monitors meet full accuracy and quality-assurance standards. DEC's community network of low-cost sensors, and public maps such as AirNow's Fire and Smoke Map and PurpleAir, are useful in villages without a regulatory monitor, but low-cost sensors are more sensitive to humidity and temperature and are best for trends rather than exact values.[14] During a heavy event, check both, and treat readings from a nearby DEC regulatory monitor as authoritative.

Do I need a different kind of N95 for Alaska smoke?

No. CDC advises wearing a NIOSH Approved N95 respirator if you must go outdoors during wildfire smoke, and notes that an N95 or P100 is most protective. A respirator only works if it fits tightly to your face.[18][19] What matters more in Alaska is fit for cold-weather layering, endurance across long smoke stretches, and having enough on hand. In many bush communities, shipping delays mean supplies should be laid in before fire season, not ordered during it.

Is telehealth allowed for me if I live in a village or on the road system?

Yes. Alaska's professional licensing division states that a provider may deliver health care services via telehealth without an initial in-person exam, and that Alaska laws are based on where the patient receives service.[23] Telehealth can cover asthma and COPD management, prescription refills, and many of the same-day treatment decisions that come up during a smoke event. In the Alaska Tribal Health System, Community Health Aides or Practitioners are the patient's first contact and work with a physician or other provider.[9] Non-beneficiaries and non-tribal residents can use community clinics, the regional hospital, or a private telehealth service. For anything involving airway distress, altered mental status, chest pain, or new neurologic symptoms, telehealth is not the right first step, in-person or emergency care is.

How much does wildfire smoke actually raise my heart risk?

A 2025 study in the Journal of the American College of Cardiology linked long-term wildfire smoke PM2.5 exposure to incident heart failure in older Medicare beneficiaries, with a per-microgram effect notably higher than for non-smoke PM2.5.[5] A 2025 JAMA Network Open study of older adults in western states found that high smoke levels were associated with more respiratory hospitalizations; the rise in cardiovascular hospitalizations was not statistically significant.[11] In Alaska, smoke days were linked to more heart failure-related emergency visits among Alaska Native people.[21] In practical terms: if you have known heart disease, take your medications, avoid outdoor exertion when Interior AQI runs Red or worse, and use indoor filtration during multi-day events.

Next Steps

If you are reading this in advance of smoke season, use the May checklist above to prepare. If you are reading this during a smoke event and something has changed, use your written action plan first, contact your community clinic or a telehealth clinician for step-up decisions or new but non-emergent symptoms, and escalate to emergency care or medevac for airway, breathing, circulation, or neurologic changes.
