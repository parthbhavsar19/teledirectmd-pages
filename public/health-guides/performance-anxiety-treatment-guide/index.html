Performance Anxiety: Causes, Propranolol Dosing & Treatment Options

Propranolol, beta-blocker mechanism, social anxiety disorder vs. situational anxiety, CBT, and telehealth prescribing.

Written by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: Nov 2026 · Updated: Nov 2026
16 min read

Can propranolol help performance anxiety?

Propranolol is a beta-blocker that blunts the body's adrenaline response: racing heart, shaky hands, trembling voice and sweating. Taken as a single dose before a speech, audition, interview or exam, it can reduce those physical symptoms without sedation. In a double-blind study of musicians, beta blockade reduced the physical symptoms of stage fright, and music critics rated performance quality higher (Brantigan et al., American Journal of Medicine, 1982). It treats the body's symptoms, not the anxious thoughts. A 2019 systematic review of 43 studies in professional musicians found cognitive behavioral therapy and beta-blockers were the most studied treatments, with beneficial results for CBT, and noted the studies had methodological weaknesses (Fernholz et al., Psychological Medicine, 2019). For an ongoing anxiety disorder, a 2016 meta-analysis concluded the evidence was insufficient to support routine propranolol use (Steenen et al., 2016). Propranolol is not safe for everyone: the FDA label lists it as contraindicated in cardiogenic shock, sinus bradycardia and greater than first degree heart block, bronchial asthma, and known hypersensitivity to propranolol (propranolol tablets label, DailyMed, revised January 2026). Performance anxiety is not one of the label's indications, so this use is off-label, and a physician decides whether it fits you. A licensed clinician, through telehealth or in-person evaluation, can confirm the diagnosis and recommend next steps.

Heart pounding before a presentation. Hands shaking during an audition. Voice wavering right when you need it steady. Many people experience performance anxiety, and many don't realize there is a medication, used off-label, that can quiet those physical symptoms for a specific event.

Beta blockers such as propranolol have been used for decades for situational anxiety such as stage fright and exam- or interview-related anxiety.[1] Performance anxiety is not one of the indications on the FDA label, so this use is off-label.[7] The supporting studies are small and mostly older, and a 2022 review concluded that the evidence is insufficient to support routine use for stage fright.[1] In clinical practice, it can still be a reasonable prescribing decision when the clinical picture is right.

That last part matters. "When the clinical picture is right" means a physician has confirmed you're dealing with situational anxiety rather than a broader anxiety disorder, has checked for contraindications, and has determined that a beta-blocker is the appropriate tool. This guide covers the pharmacology, the evidence and its limits, the diagnostic distinctions, and the treatment options from medication to therapy.

What Is Performance Anxiety?

Performance anxiety is the experience of significant physical and psychological distress triggered by the anticipation or execution of a public performance or evaluation. The fear centers on being observed, judged, or failing, and the body's stress response kicks in whether or not the threat is real.

The term covers several distinct contexts, each with slightly different clinical features:

  • Public speaking anxiety: A common form. Symptoms range from mild nervousness to full freeze responses.
  • Musical performance anxiety (MPA): Particularly well-studied. A systematic review of 43 studies in professional musicians found MPA prevalence between 16.5% and 60%.[11] Among performing artists, instrumentalists seem to be the most affected, followed by singers, dancers, and actors.[1]
  • Test and exam anxiety: Triggered by high-stakes academic or professional evaluations. The cognitive overlay of fear of failure and mind-blanking often compounds the physical symptoms.
  • Athletic performance anxiety: Affects athletes at competitive events, particularly in precision or individual sports.
  • Sexual performance anxiety: Situational anxiety that interferes with sexual function. It is a distinct presentation requiring thoughtful evaluation, because medical and psychological contributors overlap.
  • Professional performance anxiety: Surgeons, presenters, and job interviewees: any high-stakes professional task where being observed raises anxiety and impairs precision.

What unites these presentations is the mechanism. Many of the symptoms of performance anxiety, including tremor and palpitations, come from increased release of adrenaline (epinephrine) and norepinephrine from the sympathetic nervous system and adrenal glands.[1] The result is the classic fight-or-flight response, deployed at exactly the wrong moment.

Social Anxiety Disorder vs. Situational Performance Anxiety

This distinction is one of the most clinically important concepts in this guide. It determines whether as-needed propranolol is a reasonable option, or whether a different treatment plan is needed entirely.

Situational Performance Anxiety

Situational performance anxiety is triggered by specific, discrete performance events. Between these events, there is no significant anxiety about social interactions. You can have a conversation, attend a party, or eat in a restaurant without distress. The anxiety is event-specific and context-specific. When the event is over, it resolves.

This is the group for whom as-needed propranolol is usually considered. The clinical logic is simple: you know when the triggering event occurs, you take the medication in advance to target the physical symptoms, and there is no ongoing psychological burden between events.

Social Anxiety Disorder (SAD)

The DSM-5 defines social anxiety disorder by marked fear or anxiety about one or more social situations in which the person is exposed to possible scrutiny by others.[3] The fear can extend beyond formal performances into everyday social interactions. To meet diagnostic criteria, the fear must be out of proportion to the actual threat, persistent (typically lasting six months or more), and cause clinically significant distress or impairment.[3]

The DSM-5 does include a "performance only" specifier for SAD, applied when the fear is restricted to speaking or performing in public.[3] This is not the same as situational performance anxiety in the everyday sense. The key clinical question is whether the anxiety causes significant ongoing impairment and meets the full DSM criteria, or whether it is better characterized as a normal (if uncomfortable) situational stress response.

Feature Situational Performance Anxiety Social Anxiety Disorder
Trigger Specific performance events (speech, recital, exam) Broad range of social interactions and observation situations
Baseline anxiety Low to absent between events Persistent anxiety, avoidance, anticipatory worry in daily life
Functional impairment Limited to performance contexts Significant across occupational, social, and personal domains
Duration Event-specific; resolves after performance Persistent; typically >6 months by diagnostic criteria
First-line treatment Often managed with as-needed propranolol (off-label); CBT for cognitive symptoms CBT (first-line therapy)[13]; SSRIs/SNRIs (first-line medications)[4]
Why the Distinction Matters for Treatment

A patient with social anxiety disorder who takes propranolol before a presentation may see their heart rate slow, but they may still ruminate about the presentation for weeks beforehand, dissect their performance afterwards, and struggle with the other social situations that trigger their anxiety. A meta-analysis found the evidence insufficient to support routine propranolol use for anxiety disorders.[10] CBT, SSRIs and SNRIs are the established treatments for SAD.[13][4] Misidentifying the diagnosis can leave SAD undertreated.

How Propranolol Works: The Beta-Blocker Mechanism

Propranolol is a nonselective beta-adrenergic receptor antagonist, meaning it blocks both beta-1 receptors (present on heart muscle cells) and beta-2 receptors (which relax smooth muscle in many organs and vessels).[2] For performance anxiety, the key is what happens in the body rather than the brain.

When you're anxious, your sympathetic nervous system and adrenal glands release adrenaline and norepinephrine. These stress hormones produce many of the recognizable physical symptoms of acute anxiety:[1]

  • Racing or pounding heart (palpitations)[1]
  • Trembling hands or voice (tremor)[1]
  • Dry mouth, which beta blockade eliminated in the Brantigan musician studies[8]
  • Sweating, one of the categories in which musicians preferred propranolol[1]

Propranolol is a competitive beta blocker: it prevents adrenaline and norepinephrine from binding at beta receptors, which can reduce or eliminate symptoms such as tremor and palpitations, even if your mind still knows the stakes are high.[1]

Beta-blocking medications used to treat stage fright act peripherally rather than centrally.[1] What to expect: propranolol is not a tranquilizer. In the Brantigan studies, its effect on stage fright was achieved without tranquilization.[8] It reduces the physical signals of anxiety, and for some people that makes the anxious thoughts easier to manage.

The Body-Mind Feedback Loop

Physical anxiety symptoms usually worsen the patient's distress.[1] A performer notices their hands shaking, which can make them more anxious and worsen the tremor. Reducing those physical symptoms may interrupt that cycle. In the Brantigan studies, experienced music critics judged the quality of musical performance significantly improved with beta blockade.[8] The drug does not add skill; the aim is to reduce physical interference with skill the performer already has.

How propranolol is used for performance anxiety

  • This use is off-label: the FDA label's indications include high blood pressure, angina, atrial fibrillation, heart attack, migraine and essential tremor, but not performance anxiety.[7] Off-label use for situational or performance anxiety is common.[2]
  • It is taken as needed before a specific event, not every day.
  • Studies have used a single 40 mg dose taken about an hour before the event. In a randomized, double-masked crossover trial, 5 ophthalmology residents took 40 mg or placebo 1 hour before 73 microsurgery cases; propranolol reduced self-rated tremor and anxiety, with no difference in complications.[6] In an uncontrolled study, 32 high school students with test anxiety took 40 mg one hour before retaking the SAT and scored a mean 130 points higher than on their first attempt.[9] Your dose is set at the visit.
  • In the Elman trial, every participant first took a test dose without side effects before the study began.[6] Trying a dose on an ordinary day before an important event follows the same logic.
  • Reported side effects include lightheadedness, fatigue, vivid dreams, a slow heart rate, low blood pressure, and nausea.[7]
  • The label warns that beta-blockade can hide warning signs of low blood sugar, such as pulse rate and blood pressure changes, "especially in labile insulin-dependent diabetics."[7]
  • On the label, peak blood levels occur about 1 to 4 hours after an oral dose, and the plasma half-life is 3 to 6 hours.[7]

The Evidence Base for Propranolol in Performers

Beta blockers have been used for situational anxiety for decades.[1] Most studies of propranolol for stage fright are small, placebo-controlled, non-comparative studies, and a 2022 review found that no randomized clinical trials were available on propranolol for stage fright.[1] The studies below are the ones most often cited.

Brantigan et al. (1982): The Musician Study

One of the most cited studies in this area included two trials with a total of 29 musicians, using a double-blind design.[8] Propranolol was given 1.5 hours before recitals on two successive days, and performers preferred propranolol in every category rated, including nervousness, anxiety, tremor, sweating, accuracy, style, control, tempo, rhythm, memory, and comfort. Propranolol lowered both average and maximum pulse during performance.[1] Experienced music critics judged the quality of performance significantly improved, and the authors reported the effect was achieved without tranquilization.[8]

Faigel (1991): SAT Performance

In an uncontrolled study, 32 high school students with test anxiety took a single 40 mg dose of propranolol one hour before retaking the SAT and scored a mean 130 points higher than on their first attempt.[9] Without a control group, practice from the first attempt cannot be separated from the drug effect.

Elman et al. (1998): Surgical Performance

In a randomized, double-masked crossover trial, 5 ophthalmology residents took 40 mg propranolol or placebo 1 hour before 73 microsurgery cases. Propranolol reduced self-rated tremor and anxiety, and complication rates did not differ between propranolol and placebo.[6] The trial is small, but it tested propranolol in a setting where physical precision and judgment are needed at the same time.

Propranolol vs. Benzodiazepines: Head-to-Head Data

In a randomized study of 40 healthy volunteers exposed to laboratory stressors, beta-blockers reduced heart rate increases due to mental stress, whereas diazepam reduced skin conductance.[15] In a double-blind crossover study in student volunteers, propranolol 80 mg was compared with lorazepam 1 or 2.5 mg. The high dose of lorazepam impaired several performance tests and increased dizziness ratings; lorazepam, but not propranolol, increased sedation ratings; and propranolol decreased pulse while lorazepam increased it. Both drugs increased simple reaction time, neither drug changed anxiety ratings, and the authors concluded that neither was beneficial for short-term anxiety associated with intellectual stress.[14]

Propranolol vs. Benzodiazepines: How They Compare for This Use

Benzodiazepines such as diazepam, lorazepam and alprazolam are sometimes used for performance nerves, and they carry potential risks of tolerance, dependence, and misuse.[4] The comparison matters because patients sometimes arrive having been prescribed, or having sourced, a benzodiazepine for their performance anxiety. The table summarizes what the limited evidence shows.

The clinical bottom line: the head-to-head evidence is thin. In one small experimental study, high-dose lorazepam impaired performance tests and propranolol did not, but neither drug reduced anxiety ratings and both slowed simple reaction time.[14] Propranolol has been widely prescribed for performance anxiety, and benzodiazepines carry potential risks of tolerance, dependence, and misuse.[4] For a performer who needs to stay sharp, a contraindication check comes first either way.

Contraindications: Who Should Not Take Propranolol

Label Contraindications: Do Not Take Propranolol If You Have:
  • Bronchial asthma: a listed contraindication.[7] Propranolol may provoke an asthma attack.[7]
  • Sinus bradycardia: a slow heart rhythm, listed as a contraindication.[7]
  • Greater than first degree heart block: listed as a contraindication.[7]
  • Cardiogenic shock: listed as a contraindication.[7]
  • Known hypersensitivity to propranolol hydrochloride[7]

Relative Contraindications: Caution Required

  • COPD and other bronchospastic lung disease: The label says that, in general, patients with bronchospastic lung disease should not receive beta blockers.[7] In COPD, cardioselective beta-blockers are typically preferred when beta-blocker therapy is indicated.[2]
  • Diabetes mellitus: Beta-blockade may hide early warning signs of low blood sugar, such as pulse rate and blood pressure changes, especially in labile insulin-dependent diabetes.[7] This needs careful discussion before prescribing.
  • Heart failure: The label says beta blockers should be avoided in overt congestive heart failure.[7]
  • Low blood pressure: Hypotension is a listed adverse reaction.[7]
  • Raynaud's phenomenon or poor circulation: Arterial insufficiency, usually of the Raynaud type, is a listed adverse reaction.[7]
  • Calcium channel blockers (verapamil or diltiazem): The label reports significant bradycardia, heart failure, and cardiovascular collapse with verapamil, and bradycardia with diltiazem in patients with cardiac disease.[7]

Disclosing your full medical history during a physician evaluation is not a formality. These are real contraindications with real consequences, and a careful history is how the physician checks for them before prescribing.

Treating Social Anxiety Disorder: A Different Pathway

If the clinical picture points toward social anxiety disorder rather than situational performance anxiety, the treatment plan changes substantially. As-needed propranolol is not a treatment for SAD; it addresses one dimension of a broader disorder, and a meta-analysis found the evidence insufficient to support its routine use in anxiety disorders.[10]

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT): First-Line

CBT works by identifying and restructuring the beliefs and thinking patterns that drive social anxiety, such as catastrophizing about negative evaluation, overestimating the probability of humiliation, and underestimating one's ability to cope. In a network meta-analysis of 101 trials with 13,164 participants, individual CBT was associated with large effect sizes, and the authors concluded it should be regarded as the best intervention for the initial treatment of social anxiety disorder.[13]

What CBT does that propranolol cannot: it works on the appraisal of threat rather than the body's adrenaline response. In that analysis, individual CBT and SSRIs or SNRIs were the only treatment classes that outperformed an appropriate placebo.[13]

SSRIs and SNRIs: First-Line Medications for SAD

SSRIs and SNRIs are first-line treatments for social anxiety disorder. In a 2020 review's drug table, sertraline and paroxetine are listed as FDA-approved for SAD, and escitalopram and fluvoxamine are listed as off-label uses for SAD.[4] For people who decline psychological treatment, SSRIs show the most consistent evidence of benefit.[13] These medications are taken daily, not before a single event.

SSRIs and SNRIs are fundamentally different from propranolol in their scope: they treat the persistent anxiety that defines SAD, not the acute physical symptoms of a specific event. Patients starting an SSRI for SAD are not taking a pill before each presentation. They are treating an underlying anxiety disorder that affects their daily life.

Exposure Therapy

Exposure therapy, meaning systematic, graduated exposure to feared social situations, is often integrated into CBT for SAD. The principle is that anxiety decreases through repeated, controlled confrontation with the feared situation. For a patient with performance anxiety in the context of SAD, this might involve a hierarchy of speaking tasks, starting with low-stakes situations and progressing to more challenging ones. In the network meta-analysis, exposure and social skills interventions outperformed waitlist.[13]

Combination Approaches

SSRIs and CBT are often used together for social anxiety disorder, but few studies have tested the combination. In one double-blind randomized trial of 48 adults with SAD, adding escitalopram to internet-delivered CBT produced significantly more clinical responders than CBT plus placebo, and larger reductions in social anxiety severity at 15-month follow-up.[5] That trial compared CBT plus escitalopram with CBT plus placebo, not with either treatment alone, and it was small.

For a patient with SAD who also has specific, high-stakes performance events, such as a conference presentation or a major audition, there may be a role for propranolol PRN as an adjunct to their primary SAD treatment. The key word is adjunct. Propranolol in this context is a tool for a specific event, not a substitute for treating the underlying disorder.

When Medication Alone Is Not Enough

Propranolol targets the physical dimension of performance anxiety. But performance anxiety is not a purely physical problem for everyone. Some performers find that even with zero tremor and a controlled heart rate, the cognitive and emotional experience of anxiety remains disabling.

Signs that medication alone is insufficient:

  • You take propranolol, physical symptoms are controlled, but significant anxiety and negative self-talk persist during performance
  • You avoid performances entirely despite having access to medication
  • Anticipatory anxiety in the days or weeks before an event is causing significant distress
  • The anxiety is impairing preparation, so you cannot practice effectively because of anxiety about the upcoming event
  • Performance anxiety is expanding to affect more situations over time

Any of these patterns suggests that the cognitive and behavioral components of anxiety warrant attention through therapy. In professional musicians, CBT and beta-blockers are the most studied treatments for performance anxiety, with beneficial results reported for CBT, although better-controlled CBT studies are still needed.[11] Some performers use propranolol for specific events while working with a therapist on the thinking patterns behind their anxiety.

Telehealth Prescribing for PRN Propranolol

Situational performance anxiety is well-suited to telehealth prescribing. Much of the clinical evaluation is history-based: symptoms, triggers, frequency, baseline heart rate, and contraindication screening.

What a telehealth visit for performance anxiety should include:

  • Detailed symptom history: what triggers anxiety, which symptoms predominate, frequency of events
  • Medical history review: specifically screening for asthma, COPD, cardiac arrhythmias, bradycardia, heart failure, and diabetes
  • Current medications: particularly checking for calcium channel blockers, other antihypertensives, and any medications with known interactions
  • Diagnostic assessment: confirming situational performance anxiety rather than SAD, panic disorder, or another anxiety condition requiring different management
  • Baseline vital signs: patients with access to a home blood pressure cuff can provide resting heart rate and blood pressure, which is helpful for dose selection
  • Dosing guidance, including a test dose on an ordinary day before the actual event

Obtaining beta-blockers for anxiety through a legitimate, HIPAA-compliant telehealth platform that requires an evaluation by a licensed healthcare provider is a reasonable route. Telehealth can improve access here: many patients won't schedule an in-person appointment for what feels like a "minor" issue, even when the impact on their professional and personal life is significant.

Avoid any platform that offers prescription medications without a clinician evaluation. The contraindication screen is not optional.
