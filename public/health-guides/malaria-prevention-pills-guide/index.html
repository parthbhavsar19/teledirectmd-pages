Malaria Pills for Travel: Choosing, Timing and Side Effects

An adult guide to choosing a malaria prevention medicine, when to start and stop it, side effects, pregnancy, drug interactions and when a fever after travel is an emergency.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MDLast reviewed November 21, 202612 min read

How do adult travelers choose and time malaria prevention pills?

The right malaria pill depends on the destination's drug resistance, trip length, side effects, pregnancy and other medicines. Atovaquone-proguanil and doxycycline start 1 to 2 days before travel, mefloquine at least 2 weeks before, and every regimen continues after you leave. Primaquine and tafenoquine need a G6PD blood test first. No pill is 100% effective, so a fever during or after travel needs prompt care and a malaria test.[1][2][4]

A fever during or after travel to a malaria area is urgent. Seek medical care right away, say where you traveled and ask for a malaria test, even if you took every pill.[1][4] Confusion, trouble breathing or fainting are emergency warning signs: call 911 or go to an emergency department.[10]

Key Takeaways

  • Malaria prevention pills are taken before, during and after travel, and the start and stop dates differ by drug.[1]
  • Atovaquone-proguanil and doxycycline start 1 to 2 days before travel, while mefloquine starts at least 2 weeks before.[1]
  • No malaria prevention pill is 100% effective, so a fever during or after travel to a malaria area needs prompt medical care and a malaria test.[1][4]
  • Primaquine and tafenoquine require a G6PD blood test before they can be prescribed, because both can destroy red blood cells in people with G6PD deficiency.[1][8]
  • CDC advises getting antimalarial medicines in the United States before the trip, because pills bought abroad can be counterfeit or substandard.[1][4]

Do you need malaria pills for your trip?

Whether you need malaria pills depends on your exact itinerary, not only the country. For destinations where cases occur only sporadically, CDC recommends mosquito avoidance alone and no pills.[1]

CDC's country-by-country information shows where malaria spreads within each country and which medicines are recommended there.[2] Clinicians weigh the specific cities, type of accommodation, season and style of travel, along with pregnancy and other health conditions.[2]

Risk differs a great deal from traveler to traveler and from region to region, even within one country.[1] Of US malaria cases in 2019 with a known place of infection, 93% were acquired in Africa, and 76% of US civilians with malaria who gave a reason for travel were visiting friends and relatives.[1]

Growing up in a malaria area does not protect you now

Partial immunity from living where malaria spreads is lost quickly, so CDC says travelers visiting friends and relatives have the same risk as other travelers without immunity, and people who have had malaria before still need prevention.[1] CDC's traveler page adds that this protection weakens the longer you live away from a malaria area.[4]

Which malaria pill is best?

No single pill is best everywhere. The medicines CDC recommends for a given country have comparable effectiveness there, so the choice depends on drug resistance at the destination, trip length, side effects, your health and cost.[3][2]

In four randomized trials that compared mefloquine, atovaquone-proguanil and doxycycline head to head in 1,822 short-term travelers without immunity, only one case of malaria occurred.[9] The choice then depends on how much weight you give specific side effects, the number of pills and cost.[9]

When do you start malaria pills, and how long do you take them after?

Every regimen starts before you arrive and continues after you leave the malaria area. Atovaquone-proguanil and doxycycline start 1 to 2 days before travel, while mefloquine starts at least 2 weeks before.[1]

Starting before the trip puts the medicine in your blood before you are exposed.[1] If you are worried about tolerating a drug, it can be started earlier; CDC gives the example of starting mefloquine 3 to 4 weeks ahead so side effects show up before departure.[1]

Switching from a daily pill to a weekly pill partway through is not recommended, because the weekly drug takes time to reach protective levels.[1] The doses above are adult reference doses; children's doses are based on weight, and this guide covers adults only. Your own regimen is set with your clinician.

Is there a malaria vaccine for travelers?

No. CDC's traveler prevention page, last reviewed March 12, 2024, says no malaria vaccine is currently available in the US, so travelers rely on pills plus bite prevention.[4]

CDC advises avoiding mosquito bites even when you take medicine to prevent malaria.[4]

Can you still get malaria while taking pills?

Yes, it is possible. CDC says prevention pills are highly effective but not 100% effective, so any fever during or after travel needs prompt medical care and a malaria test.[1]

Risk rises when people skip pills or bite precautions, or take a drug that does not match the resistance pattern at the destination.[1] Symptoms can start as early as 7 days after an infective bite and as late as several months or more after exposure.[1] CDC advises seeking care for fever or a flu-like illness for up to one year after returning.[4]

Common symptoms are fever and flu-like illness, chills, headache, muscle aches, tiredness, nausea, vomiting and diarrhea.[5]

What are the side effects of malaria pills?

Malaria prevention medicines are generally well tolerated, and minor side effects usually do not require stopping the drug.[1] Each medicine has its own pattern, so the best choice often depends on which side effects matter most to you.[9]

Atovaquone-proguanil (Malarone)

CDC lists abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting and headache as the most common side effects.[1] In prevention trials, the FDA label lists diarrhea, dreams, mouth ulcers and headache as common adverse reactions (4% or more), at a similar or lower rate than with a comparison drug.[6]

Doxycycline

Doxycycline can cause sun sensitivity that looks like an exaggerated sunburn, and it is linked to more vaginal yeast infections.[1] Taking it with a meal reduces nausea, and swallowing it with plenty of fluid and not lying down soon after lowers the risk of irritating the esophagus.[1]

Mefloquine

Mefloquine can cause abnormal dreams, anxiety, depression, dizziness, headache and insomnia, and rarely serious reactions such as psychosis or seizures.[1] Its FDA label carries a boxed warning that neuropsychiatric reactions can persist after the drug is stopped, and dizziness, ringing in the ears and loss of balance have been permanent in some cases.[7] If psychiatric or neurologic symptoms start while taking it for prevention, the label says to stop it and switch to another medicine.[7]

How the three common choices compare

A 2017 Cochrane review estimated that, compared with atovaquone-proguanil, mefloquine users had more insomnia (13% versus 3%), abnormal dreams (14% versus 7%), anxiety (6% versus 1%) and depressed mood (6% versus 1%).[9] Compared with doxycycline, mefloquine users reported less sun sensitivity (2% versus 19%), vaginal thrush (2% versus 16%) and indigestion (4% versus 14%), though most of those comparisons came from cohort studies rated low or very low certainty.[9]

Chloroquine and primaquine

Chloroquine can make psoriasis worse.[1] In people with normal G6PD levels, the most common side effect of primaquine is stomach upset, which taking it with food can reduce or prevent.[1]

Malarone vs doxycycline: what is the difference?

Both are daily pills that start 1 to 2 days before travel; the main differences are how long you take them afterward and cost. Atovaquone-proguanil continues for 7 days after you leave and doxycycline for 4 weeks, and CDC notes doxycycline tends to be the least expensive.[1][3]

Doxycycline can suit people already taking it daily to prevent acne, who do not have to take an additional medicine, and it can also prevent some other infections such as rickettsial infections and leptospirosis.[1] CDC describes atovaquone-proguanil as well tolerated with uncommon side effects, but it tends to cost more on long trips and is not recommended during pregnancy or with severe kidney impairment.[1]

If you take minocycline for acne, CDC advises stopping it 1 to 2 days before travel and taking doxycycline instead, then restarting minocycline after the doxycycline course ends.[1]

What happens if you miss a dose or vomit after taking it?

Take the missed dose as soon as possible, and do not double the dose the next day. For a daily pill, keep going at the new time of day; for a weekly pill only 1 to 2 days late, return to your original weekly day.[1]

If a weekly dose is more than 2 days late, blood levels may not be protective: take it as soon as possible and make that the new weekly day.[1] Weekly drugs give a wider margin of error than daily drugs because they last longer in the body.[1]

For atovaquone-proguanil, the FDA label says to take a repeat dose if you vomit within 1 hour of a dose.[6]

Taking too much is dangerous. An overdose of antimalarial medicine, especially chloroquine, can be fatal and is a medical emergency, so keep pills in childproof containers out of children's reach.[1]

Can you take malaria pills while pregnant or breastfeeding?

CDC says the best option in pregnancy is to avoid travel to malaria areas, because malaria can be more severe in pregnancy and no regimen is completely effective. If travel cannot be delayed, mefloquine is the only recommended pill for areas with chloroquine resistance, and chloroquine can be used where resistance has not been reported.[1]

Atovaquone-proguanil is not recommended in pregnancy because safety data are limited, doxycycline is contraindicated, and neither primaquine nor tafenoquine should be used.[1] The tafenoquine label recommends a pregnancy test before starting it in people who could become pregnant.[8]

Breastfeeding

Breast milk does not carry enough drug to protect a nursing infant, so a baby who needs protection needs their own medicine.[1] Atovaquone-proguanil is not recommended while breastfeeding an infant under 5 kg (11 lb), and primaquine and tafenoquine require the infant's G6PD status to be known first.[1][3]

What medicines interact with malaria pills?

Several common medicines interact with malaria pills, so share your full medicine list before a regimen is chosen. Examples on the FDA labels include blood thinners, some antibiotics and antifungals, and other antimalarial drugs.[6][7]

  • Warfarin and related blood thinners: proguanil may increase their effect, so blood clotting tests or a dose change may be needed with atovaquone-proguanil.[1][6]
  • Rifampin or rifabutin: these lower atovaquone levels, and using them with atovaquone-proguanil is not recommended.[6]
  • Metoclopramide: it may reduce how much atovaquone is absorbed, so the label says to use it only if other anti-nausea medicines are not available.[6]
  • Ketoconazole: it should not be taken with mefloquine or within 15 weeks of the last mefloquine dose because of a risk of a potentially fatal heart rhythm change.[7]
  • Quinine, quinidine or chloroquine with mefloquine: combinations can cause electrocardiogram abnormalities or raise the risk of seizures.[7]
  • Oral typhoid vaccine: finish it at least 24 hours before taking a dose of doxycycline.[1]

Why do primaquine and tafenoquine need a G6PD test?

Primaquine and tafenoquine can cause life-threatening destruction of red blood cells (hemolysis) in people with G6PD deficiency. A quantitative laboratory test must rule out G6PD deficiency before either drug is prescribed.[1][8]

The test only has to be done once: after a normal level is documented, it does not need to be repeated the next time either drug is considered, though getting it adds cost and delay.[3]

Tafenoquine is approved for prevention only in adults 18 and older and is taken with food.[8] Primaquine is mainly used for short trips to places where most malaria is the Plasmodium vivax type, and as a separate course after prolonged exposure.[1]

Do you need a prescription, and can you buy malaria pills abroad?

Atovaquone-proguanil, mefloquine and tafenoquine carry FDA prescription drug labels, so they need a prescription in the US. CDC advises getting antimalarial medicines in the US before you travel.[4][6][7][8]

CDC discourages buying malaria prevention medicines abroad because their quality is often unknown: products may be counterfeit, made under substandard practices or contaminated.[1] Long-term travelers should buy enough before leaving the US to last their entire time in malaria areas.[1]

Anyone prescribed mefloquine should receive the FDA medication guide, and the label says an information wallet card is supplied to carry while taking it.[1][7]

How do you avoid mosquito bites on the trip?

Use an EPA-registered repellent on exposed skin, cover up, and sleep in a screened or air-conditioned room or under a permethrin-treated bed net. Malaria mosquitoes bite mainly between dusk and dawn, so evenings and nights matter most.[1][4]

  • Repellent: choose one with DEET, picaridin, IR3535, oil of lemon eucalyptus, para-menthane-diol or 2-undecanone; CDC says DEET products offer the best protection against mosquito bites.[4]
  • Sunscreen: apply sunscreen first and repellent second.[1]
  • Clothing: wear loose long sleeves, long pants and socks, and treat clothing and gear with 0.5% permethrin or buy pretreated items; never put permethrin on skin.[4]
  • Sleeping: use a well-screened or air-conditioned room, or a permethrin-treated bed net.[4]

None of these measures protects 100% of the time, which is why pills and bite prevention are used together where risk is high.[4]

When is a fever after travel an emergency, and what can a video visit cover?

Treat any fever or flu-like illness during travel to a malaria area, or in the year after you return, as urgent: get medical care right away, say where you traveled and ask for a malaria test.[1][4]

Call 911 or go to an emergency department

Suspected malaria, especially falciparum malaria, is a medical emergency because people can deteriorate rapidly and unpredictably.[1] Severe malaria can cause confusion, seizures, breathing failure, kidney failure, yellowing of the skin and eyes, coma and death.[1][5]

Breathing problems, confusion or another change in mental status, and fainting or loss of consciousness are warning signs of a medical emergency in adults.[10] Call 911 when a condition is life threatening or could become life threatening on the way to the hospital.[10]

Possible malaria should be evaluated urgently at a facility that can test and treat quickly, with blood test results available within hours.[1] If you get sick while still traveling, seek the best available care as soon as possible and keep taking your prevention pills until you have a diagnosis.[1]

What a video visit can and cannot cover

Before a trip, a video visit can cover the itinerary, accommodations, current medicines, pregnancy status and medical history, which are the details CDC says clinicians need to choose a regimen.[2][4] A video visit cannot draw blood for a G6PD test, examine you or test for malaria, so a fever after travel always needs in-person testing.[1]

This guide is for adults 18 and older. It does not cover treatment of active malaria, which needs urgent in-person care.
