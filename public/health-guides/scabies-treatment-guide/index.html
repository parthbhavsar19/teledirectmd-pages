Scabies: Symptoms, Diagnosis & Permethrin Treatment Explained

Permethrin application, oral ivermectin dosing, crusted scabies, household decontamination, and the itch that lingers after treatment, explained by our clinical team.

Written by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: February 2026 · Updated: Jul 2026
15 min read

How is scabies diagnosed and treated?

Scabies is a highly contagious skin infestation caused by the human itch mite, Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis. Per CDC clinical guidance, permethrin 5% cream is applied from the neck down and washed off after 8 to 14 hours, and a second application about a week later may be needed. Oral ivermectin, which is not FDA approved for scabies, is given as two doses of 200 mcg/kg with food, 7 to 14 days apart; its safety has not been established in pregnancy or in children weighing less than 15 kg. CDC advises treating all household members and close contacts at the same time as the person with scabies. Crusted (Norwegian) scabies is treated with oral ivermectin together with a topical agent, often as 3, 5, or 7 doses depending on severity, and warrants in-person evaluation. Itching can continue for several weeks after treatment even when all the mites and eggs are killed. A licensed clinician, through telehealth or in-person evaluation, can confirm the diagnosis and recommend next steps.

Key Takeaways

  • Scabies is caused by Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis, the human itch mite, which tunnels just beneath the surface of the skin. The itch is an allergic (hypersensitivity) reaction to the mites and their feces.[1]
  • Permethrin 5% cream is a first-line treatment: apply from the neck down, wash off after 8 to 14 hours, and a second application about a week later may be needed.[2] Some apparent treatment failures reflect application errors.[5]
  • Oral ivermectin is an off-label alternative with similar efficacy: two doses of 200 mcg/kg taken with food, 7 to 14 days apart.[2]
  • Every household member and close contact should be treated at the same time, even those with no symptoms, or reinfestation is likely.[1][5]
  • Itching can continue for several weeks after treatment even when all the mites and eggs are killed;[1] in one cohort it lasted a median of 52.5 days.[6]
  • Crusted (Norwegian) scabies requires oral ivermectin together with a topical agent such as permethrin, often over several doses.[2]

Most patients who come to us with scabies have been itching for weeks, sometimes months, before they get the right diagnosis. In clinical experience, scabies is one of the most commonly misidentified skin conditions in primary care. It gets labeled as eczema, contact dermatitis, or a stress rash, and the patient keeps scratching while the mites keep spreading.

The good news: scabies is curable. The frustrating reality: it requires precise treatment technique, treatment of everyone in the household at the same time, and patience through several weeks of post-treatment itch that many patients mistake for treatment failure.

This guide covers the biology of the mite, how to distinguish scabies from look-alike conditions, how first-line medications are used correctly, the management of crusted scabies, and what to do when the itch persists after the mites are gone.

What Is Scabies? Understanding the Mite

Scabies is an infestation of the skin caused by Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis, the human itch mite.[1] Adult female mites measure about 0.3 to 0.5 mm, and transmission of animal scabies mites to humans is rare.[5] Pets do not need treatment, because the human itch mite cannot survive on animals.[7]

The female mite tunnels just beneath the surface of the skin, and her burrows contain the mite, her eggs, and her feces (called scybala).[1][5] An otherwise healthy person with scabies may carry fewer than 10 to 15 mites at a time,[1] a surprisingly small infestation that can still cause relentless, body-wide itch.

The intense itching is not caused directly by the burrowing. It is a hypersensitivity (allergic) reaction to the mites and their feces.[1] This explains two clinically important facts. First, a person infested for the first time typically takes 4 to 8 weeks to develop symptoms.[1] Second, itching may continue for several weeks after treatment even if all the mites and eggs are killed.[1]

Where Burrows Appear

Burrows are the pathognomonic sign of scabies: thread-like whitish, grayish, or brownish lines about 10 mm long, sometimes ending in a small pearl-like blister.[5] In adults, the rash and burrows most often appear:[1]

  • Between the fingers
  • In the skin folds of the wrists, elbows, knees, and armpits
  • Around the waist and on the buttocks
  • On the penis (itchy bumps on the glans penis in adult men are almost always a sign of scabies)[5]
  • On the nipples
  • On the shoulder blades

Infants and very young children often have a rash on the head, face, neck, palms, and soles of the feet,[1] areas that are usually spared in healthy adults. The elderly and people with weakened immune systems may also have face and scalp involvement.[5]

How Scabies Spreads

Scabies is most commonly spread by direct, prolonged skin-to-skin contact with a person who has scabies.[1] Transmission through brief contact, such as a handshake, is rare because it usually requires 15 to 20 minutes of contact; sexual contact is a significant route.[5]

Scabies mites generally do not survive more than two to three days away from human skin, so sharing clothing, towels, or bedding is a less common route than direct contact.[1] Outbreaks occur more often in crowded settings such as nursing homes, extended care facilities, jails and prisons, and childcare facilities.[1]

Diagnosis: Scabies vs. Eczema vs. Contact Dermatitis

Scabies is one of the great mimics in dermatology. I have seen it misdiagnosed repeatedly as atopic dermatitis, nummular eczema, contact dermatitis, drug reactions, and even autoimmune conditions. By the time the correct diagnosis is made, many patients have spent weeks using steroid cream for presumed eczema while the mites keep burrowing.

Clinical Clue: Think Scabies When Multiple People Itch

One of the most reliable diagnostic signals is household spread. If a patient presents with a new, intensely itchy rash AND mentions that a partner, family member, or roommate has developed similar symptoms around the same time, scabies should be at the top of the differential diagnosis, even before a skin scraping is performed. Eczema and contact dermatitis do not spread between people.

Confirming the Diagnosis

A definitive diagnosis comes from finding the mite, eggs, or fecal pellets on microscopic examination of a skin scraping taken from a burrow.[5] The clinician gently scrapes the skin with a blunt blade, often with a drop of mineral oil to help the material stick to the blade.[5] A negative scraping does not rule out scabies: a person can still have scabies even if mites, eggs, or feces are not found.[1][5]

Dermoscopy (a magnified skin surface examination) is also useful. The classic finding is a brown-black triangle at the end of a burrow (the mite itself, called the delta sign) followed by a whitish trail (the burrow, called the jet trail).[5]

Scabies can also be diagnosed clinically when a person has a history of contact with someone who has scabies, itching that began after that contact, and lesions in a typical distribution.[5] In practice, many patients start treatment on those clinical grounds without waiting for microscopic confirmation.

Treatment: First-Line Medications

All scabies treatment requires a prescription. No over-the-counter, non-prescription products are approved to treat human scabies.[2][8] CDC lists two first-line medications for classic scabies: permethrin 5% cream (topical) and oral ivermectin (systemic), and the two have similar efficacy for cure.[2] The choice between them depends on patient age, weight, pregnancy status, and extent of disease.

Permethrin 5% Cream: First-Line Topical Treatment

Permethrin is approved by the FDA for the treatment of scabies in people who are at least 2 months of age.[2][10] The FDA label says it should be used during pregnancy only if clearly needed,[10] and the American Academy of Dermatology describes it as FDA approved for women who are pregnant.[3] Absorption through the skin is 2% or less of the amount applied.[10]

Permethrin Application: Step-by-Step

1. Bathe or shower first. Apply the cream to clean, dry skin.[7]
2. Apply from the neck down.[2] Cover every skin surface, including the skin around the nails, between the fingers and toes, and the crease between the buttocks.[7] Do not skip areas.
3. Leave on for 8 to 14 hours, then wash off.[2] Many people apply it at bedtime and wash it off on waking.[3]
4. If you wash your hands, reapply to your hands.[7]
5. Infants, children, and the elderly often need the scalp, temples, and forehead treated as well. Never apply it to the nose, lips, or eyelids, or around the eyes or mouth.[7]
6. A second application about a week later may be needed.[2] Scabies medicines are generally less effective against eggs, so treatment is repeated a week later to kill newly hatched mites.[5]

Some apparent permethrin resistance may actually reflect application errors rather than true drug resistance.[5] In my clinical experience, patients who report that permethrin "didn't work" often missed the hands, skipped the feet, or did not cover every surface. Treating the cream like sunscreen, applied to every surface with attention to the small details, makes a real difference.

Oral Ivermectin: Systemic Treatment

Oral ivermectin is not FDA approved for the treatment of scabies, but topical permethrin and oral ivermectin have similar efficacy for cure.[2] For classic scabies, CDC guidance is two doses of 200 mcg/kg, taken with food, 7 to 14 days apart; scabies experts recommend taking it with a meal to increase bioavailability.[2] The FDA label, written for the drug's approved uses, instructs patients to take the tablets on an empty stomach with water.[9] A second dose is used because scabies medicines are generally less effective against eggs.[5]

The FDA label gives the weight-based table below for a single dose of about 200 mcg/kg, written for its approved use in strongyloidiasis. The same 200 mcg/kg per dose is used for scabies.[9][2]

The safety of ivermectin in children weighing less than 15 kg and in pregnant women has not been established.[2] The FDA label states that ivermectin should not be used during pregnancy because its safety in pregnancy has not been established.[9] Because data in pregnancy and breastfeeding are limited, permethrin is generally preferred in these groups.[5] To date, ivermectin use for scabies has not been associated with serious adverse events.[5]

Crusted (Norwegian) Scabies in Immunocompromised Patients

Crusted scabies differs from classic scabies in severity, mite burden, contagiousness, and treatment. An otherwise healthy person with classic scabies may carry fewer than 10 to 15 mites,[1] while crusted scabies involves unchecked multiplication of mites in the skin, resulting in thousands or even millions of them.[5]

Host factors play a larger role than the mite's own characteristics, and more than half of patients with crusted scabies have identifiable immunosuppressive risk factors. Groups at risk include:[5]

  • People with HIV infection (the most common comorbidity, in 45% of patients, in one case series)
  • People with other causes of a weakened immune system
  • Elderly people, whose immune response may be reduced
  • People who may not feel itch or cannot scratch normally, such as those with paralysis, amputations, Alzheimer's disease, or finger deformities
  • People with Down syndrome or nutritional disorders

Clinical Presentation

Crusted scabies causes widespread, thick, crusted plaques. The crust can exceed 1 cm in thickness, ranges from creamy or gray to yellow-brown, and can resemble psoriasis.[5] The nails can become thickened and discolored and are a significant source of relapse.[5] Although itching is commonly assumed to be absent, about half of patients have some degree of itch.[5]

Crusted scabies is very contagious.[1] It is often transmitted through objects such as bed covers and sofas, and through shed scales, and patients with crusted scabies are involved in the majority of outbreaks.[5] Delayed diagnosis and treatment of crusted scabies can often cause outbreaks.[1]

Treatment Protocol for Crusted Scabies

For crusted scabies, both oral and topical agents should be used: ivermectin is given together with a topical agent, and permethrin is applied to the entire body together with an oral agent.[2]

Crusted Scabies: Ivermectin Dosing by Severity

Each dose of ivermectin is 200 mcg/kg. There is no consensus on the optimal frequency; depending on infection severity, some experts recommend:[2]

  • Three doses: approximately days 1, 2, and 8
  • Five doses: approximately days 1, 2, 8, 9, and 15
  • Seven doses: approximately days 1, 2, 8, 9, 15, 22, and 29

Experts also vary on how often to apply permethrin 5% cream, from an initial daily application to application every 2 to 3 days for the first 1 to 2 weeks. A topical keratolytic cream may be applied to areas of crusting on days that permethrin is not given, until the crusting has resolved.[2]

Treating Contacts and Decontaminating Your Home

Treating only the diagnosed patient while leaving household contacts untreated is a reliable way to fail: if you get treatment and the people you live with or have close contact with do not, you can get the mites again.[7] In one clinic cohort, reinfestation by untreated family members or a partner explained some treatment failures, which had nothing to do with the medication itself.[6]

Simultaneous Contact Treatment

CDC advises treating all household members and close contacts at the same time as the person with scabies, and notifying people you had direct skin-to-skin contact with during the previous two months so they can seek treatment.[1] The American Academy of Dermatology also lists recent sexual partners among those who need treatment.[3] Close contacts should be treated at the same time even if they have no symptoms.[5] A person infested for the first time may have no symptoms for 4 to 8 weeks,[1] and the absence of itching in a family member does not necessarily mean the absence of scabies.[5]

What patients are told in practice: treat everyone in the house on the same night. Set all the permethrin cream out on the same counter, with everyone's name on it. Do it together. Coordinating treatment as a household, rather than treating one person at a time as they become symptomatic, is what breaks the cycle.

Fomite Decontamination: What to Wash and What Not to Panic About

Scabies mites generally do not survive more than two to three days away from human skin.[1] This puts the decontamination task in perspective: you are not dealing with an organism that can live for weeks in your carpet or furniture. Still, washing recently used items is an important precaution.

  • Machine wash: Wash all clothing, towels, and bedding used next to the skin during the three days before starting treatment.[1] Wash in hot water and dry in a hot dryer; temperatures above 50°C (122°F) for 10 minutes kill mites and eggs.[1][4]
  • Items that cannot be washed: Dry-clean them, or seal them in a closed plastic bag for at least 72 hours to a week.[1][8] This includes items such as children's fabrics and stuffed animals.[5]
  • Vacuum carpets, area rugs, and upholstered furniture on the day you start treatment.[7]
  • Do not use insecticide sprays or fumigants to treat scabies or crusted scabies.[8]
  • Pets do not need treatment. The human itch mite cannot survive on animals.[7]

The Itch After Treatment: What to Expect

Here is what every patient is told before they leave with a prescription: the itch will very likely continue after treatment, and that is expected. Post-scabies itch can lead to extra visits and calls,[6] and it is easy to mistake for treatment failure.

The itching of scabies is a hypersensitivity (allergic) reaction to the mites and their feces, so itching may continue for several weeks after treatment even if all the mites and eggs are killed.[1] The permethrin label also notes that patients may experience persistent itching after treatment.[10]

Post-Scabies Itch: What the Research Shows

In a single-center retrospective cohort of 103 adults, about one-third (34%) had post-scabies itch, which lasted a median of 52.5 days (interquartile range, 28 to 135 days), longer than the 4 to 6 weeks reported in earlier published guidance.[6] Post-scabies itch was more frequent in the older combined-treatment group than in the younger permethrin-only group (42% vs. 25%), a trend that did not reach statistical significance.[6]

Managing Post-Scabies Pruritus

Symptom management during the post-treatment period focuses on calming the itch and inflammation:

  • Topical corticosteroids (steroid cream) ease redness, swelling, and itch.[3] In the cohort above, 83% of patients with post-scabies itch were treated with them.[6]
  • Oral antihistamines help control the itch and help you sleep.[3]
  • Gentle skin care was part of management for about half of the patients with post-scabies itch in that cohort.[6]
  • For severe or prolonged itch, clinicians in that cohort also used gabapentin, doxepin, or narrow-band UVB phototherapy in a small number of patients.[6]

When to Be Concerned: Distinguish Post-Treatment Itch from Reinfestation

Post-scabies itch and an ongoing infestation can feel identical. CDC notes that retreatment may be needed if:[8]

  • Itching is still present more than 2 to 4 weeks after treatment
  • New burrows appear

Two other points help sort it out. A close contact who was not treated and is now itching points toward reinfestation, because people who are not treated can pass the mites back.[7] Itchy nodules alone are not proof of active infestation: they can persist for weeks or months after effective treatment and typically do not contain mites.[5]

If new burrows appear or the itch persists beyond 2 to 4 weeks, re-evaluation, and possibly retreatment along with treatment of contacts, is appropriate. Do not retreat on itch alone without a clinical assessment: permethrin itself can cause irritant contact dermatitis, which leads about 5% of patients to stop treatment.[5]

Scabies in Institutional Settings

Scabies outbreaks in nursing homes, extended care facilities, jails and prisons, and childcare facilities present a public health challenge that goes beyond treating individual patients.[1] A single case of crusted scabies is considered an outbreak.[5]

Why Institutional Outbreaks Are Different

Several features make institutional management more complex than household treatment:

  • Many residents may be elderly, immunocompromised, or cognitively impaired, and some may not feel itch or scratch normally, which can delay recognition.[5]
  • The institutional environment (shared dining areas, common rooms, hands-on care) creates repeated exposure opportunities among staff, residents, and relatives.
  • Patients with crusted scabies are involved in the majority of outbreaks.[5]
  • Staff generally can return to work the day after receiving a dose of treatment with permethrin or ivermectin, and gloves should be used when giving hands-on care to any patient or resident with suspected or confirmed scabies.[4]

Outbreak Response Framework

CDC public health strategies for institutional outbreaks include:[4]

  • Identify and treat all exposed persons (staff, relatives, and patients or residents), and offer treatment to household members of staff, such as spouses, children, and roommates.
  • Keep records and detect cases early. Record each patient's or resident's name, age, sex, and room number, and run an active program for early detection of infested patients, residents, and staff.
  • Use infection control practices, such as gloves and gowns, for hands-on care.
  • Environmental measures: Machine wash and dry bedding and clothing using hot water and hot dryer cycles; store items that cannot be laundered in a closed plastic bag for several days to a week; clean and vacuum rooms routinely. Environmental disinfestation is neither necessary nor warranted.
  • Screen new patients, residents, and staff for scabies, and notify the local health department if there are multiple cases.

Telehealth and Scabies: What a Virtual Visit Can Do

In my clinical experience, scabies is well-suited to telehealth evaluation in most cases. The diagnosis rests heavily on history and pattern recognition: where is the rash, how long has it been present, does it affect others in the household, is the itch worse at night, and has anything improved it? These are all questions a clinician can ask and assess during a video visit.

Classic scabies presentations, such as burrows between the fingers, intense nocturnal itch, and simultaneous symptoms in household members, allow for a confident clinical diagnosis without an in-person skin scraping. A prescriber can assess photographs of the rash, ask the right screening questions, and issue prescriptions for permethrin 5% cream or oral ivermectin, along with detailed written instructions for application and household decontamination.

Telehealth is particularly well-suited to scabies because:

  • Scabies carries real stigma,[5] and patients who are embarrassed to seek in-person care may find a virtual visit easier.
  • The prescriber can coordinate simultaneous treatment prescriptions for multiple household members in a single visit.
  • Post-treatment follow-up (to distinguish normal post-treatment itch from reinfestation) is an ideal telehealth use case.
  • Patients in underserved areas or with limited transportation can access care that would otherwise require a dermatology referral.
When In-Person Evaluation Is Needed

Some presentations call for in-person assessment: suspected crusted scabies (which needs close clinical examination and often a skin scraping to confirm the diagnosis and assess severity), suspected secondary bacterial infection (warmth, pus, swelling, fever), patients who have failed two or more treatment courses, and cases where the diagnosis is genuinely uncertain after a thorough virtual assessment. A telehealth clinician should recognize these situations and facilitate a timely in-person referral.

Managing Symptoms and Secondary Complications

Scabicide treatment addresses the infestation itself. Several additional measures help manage the consequences of chronic scratching and the immune response:

  • Antihistamines. Oral antihistamines help control the itch and help you sleep.[3]
  • Topical corticosteroids. Steroid cream eases redness, swelling, and itch during and after treatment.[3]
  • Antibiotics for secondary infection. Scratching damages the skin barrier, and secondary bacterial infections such as impetigo and furuncles can develop.[5] An antibiotic is used to clear an infection.[3] Increasing redness, warmth, swelling, or pus needs prompt evaluation.
  • Gentle skin care. Gentle skin care was part of post-scabies itch management for about half of the patients in the cohort study described above.[6]
  • Nails. Apply the cream to the skin around the nails.[7] In crusted scabies, the nails are a significant source of relapse.[5]

Classic scabies has recognizable patterns, such as burrows between the fingers, intense nighttime itch, and similar symptoms in household members, that allow a clinician to make a confident clinical diagnosis via telehealth. A physician can prescribe permethrin 5% cream or oral ivermectin, provide application instructions, and give guidance on household decontamination during a virtual visit. Patients with atypical presentations, suspected crusted scabies, or those who have already failed treatment may benefit from an in-person evaluation that includes a skin scraping.

CDC guidance for classic scabies is two doses of 200 mcg per kilogram of body weight, taken with food, 7 to 14 days apart; scabies experts recommend taking it with a meal to increase absorption. Ivermectin is not FDA approved for scabies, but topical permethrin and oral ivermectin have similar efficacy for cure. Its safety has not been established in children weighing less than 15 kg or in pregnant women, and the FDA label says it should not be used during pregnancy. For crusted scabies, ivermectin is used together with a topical agent, often as 3, 5, or 7 doses depending on severity.

The mite spreads mainly through direct, prolonged skin-to-skin contact, and regular body washing does not significantly reduce the risk of transmission. The stigma around scabies is a real barrier to patients seeking care promptly, which is why this point matters. Scabies outbreaks occur in nursing homes, prisons, schools, childcare centers, military barracks, and ordinary households.

See a clinician if you have an intensely itchy rash that is worse at night, involves the finger webs or wrists, or is affecting others in your household. Also seek evaluation if you have a rash that has not responded to OTC eczema treatments, if you notice burrow-like lines on your skin, or if you are immunocompromised (HIV, transplant, chemotherapy) and develop any new skin condition.
