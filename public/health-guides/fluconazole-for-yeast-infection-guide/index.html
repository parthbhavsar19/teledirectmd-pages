Fluconazole for Yeast Infection: Is One 150 mg Pill Enough?

A physician's evidence-based guide to oral fluconazole for vaginal yeast infection: how it works, the 150 mg single dose, effectiveness, safety, interactions, and when it fails.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: November 23, 2026
25 min read

Is one fluconazole pill enough for a yeast infection?

For most uncomplicated vaginal yeast infections, yes. CDC's 2021 treatment guidelines list fluconazole (Diflucan) 150 mg as a single oral dose, and azole treatment relieves symptoms and clears cultures in 80 to 90 percent of people who complete therapy.[2] Severe infections are treated with two 150 mg doses taken 72 hours apart or 7 to 14 days of a topical azole, and in pregnancy CDC recommends only 7-day topical azoles.[2] In a 2025 network meta-analysis of 50 randomized trials, every oral and topical treatment studied was highly effective compared with placebo (over 75 percent), and single-dose fluconazole was slightly more effective than multiple-day topical therapy for early clinical cure.[6] Because it is taken by mouth it can interact with other medicines, and the FDA label says use in pregnancy should be avoided except for severe or potentially life-threatening fungal infections.[1] When an infection persists or returns despite fluconazole, fluconazole-resistant Candida or a non-albicans species may be responsible, and alternative treatments exist.[8]
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated November 23, 2026.

Key facts

  • Labeled dose for a vaginal yeast infection: 150 mg by mouth, once.[1]
  • First relief came at a median of 22.9 hours and complete relief at a median of 73.5 hours in a U.S. trial.[14]
  • Severe infection: CDC adds a second 150 mg dose 72 hours after the first.[2]
  • Recurrent infection: three doses on days 1, 4, and 7, then weekly for 6 months.[2]
  • Only 33.7 percent of women who self-diagnosed and bought an over-the-counter antifungal had a yeast infection.[16]
  • Pregnancy: CDC advises against fluconazole and recommends only 7-day topical azoles.[2]

Key Takeaways

  • Fluconazole is a prescription oral antifungal.[3] CDC lists a single 150 mg oral dose for uncomplicated vaginal yeast infection.[2]
  • In a 2025 meta-analysis of randomized trials, single-dose fluconazole and topical azoles were all highly effective, with fluconazole slightly ahead for early clinical cure.[6]
  • It blocks a fungal enzyme (lanosterol 14-alpha-demethylase) that converts lanosterol to ergosterol, and more than 90 percent of an oral dose is absorbed.[1]
  • Weekly 150 mg fluconazole is the classic maintenance therapy for recurrent infections, keeping about 91 percent of women in the landmark trial disease-free at six months.[7]
  • The FDA label says to avoid it in pregnancy except for severe or potentially life-threatening fungal infections, and it interacts with medicines including warfarin and some statins.[1]
  • When fluconazole no longer works, fluconazole-resistant Candida or a non-albicans species such as C. glabrata may be the cause.[8][2]

For many people, a single pill is the simplest way to treat a yeast infection, and the oral option in CDC guidelines is fluconazole.[2] This guide explains what fluconazole is, how fast it works, when a second dose is used, how well it works, its real safety considerations, and what to do when it stops working.

What is fluconazole, and is it the same as Diflucan?

Fluconazole is a triazole antifungal taken by mouth, best known by the brand name Diflucan.[1] The FDA first approved Diflucan in 1990.[3] The FDA label lists vaginal candidiasis (vaginal yeast infection due to Candida) as an approved use, and the recommended dose for it is 150 mg as a single oral dose.[1] CDC lists the same single 150 mg oral dose, a regimen that makes treating an uncomplicated infection a one-tablet event.[2]

Fluconazole (Diflucan) tablets are prescription products in the United States,[3] in contrast to the topical azoles miconazole, clotrimazole, and tioconazole, which CDC lists as over-the-counter vaginal treatments.[2]

Is your yeast infection uncomplicated or complicated?

The single-tablet regimen is meant for uncomplicated infection. CDC sorts vulvovaginal candidiasis into two groups based on how it presents, the organism, host factors, and response to therapy, and estimates that about 10 to 20 percent of women have complicated infection that needs special diagnostic and treatment steps.[2] The 2016 IDSA candidiasis guideline puts uncomplicated infection at about 90 percent of cases.[13]

CDC advises that women with underlying immunodeficiency, poorly controlled diabetes, or immunosuppressive therapy might not respond as well to short-term therapies, and that more prolonged (7 to 14 days) conventional treatment is necessary.[2]

How does fluconazole work?

Fluconazole is a highly selective inhibitor of a fungal enzyme called lanosterol 14-alpha-demethylase, which converts lanosterol to ergosterol, a sterol the fungus needs. The resulting loss of normal sterols may be responsible for fluconazole's fungistatic effect, meaning it halts fungal growth.[1]

When swallowed, more than 90 percent of a fluconazole dose is absorbed. Peak blood levels arrive 1 to 2 hours after a dose in fasting volunteers, and the plasma half-life is about 30 hours (range 20 to 50 hours). In label studies, a single 150 mg dose reached vaginal tissue at tissue to plasma ratios of 0.94 to 1.14 over the first 48 hours, and reached vaginal fluid over the first 72 hours.[1]

How long does fluconazole take to work?

Most relief comes over the first three days. In a U.S. randomized, open-label trial of women with moderate to severe symptoms, half of the 79 women analyzed after a single 150 mg fluconazole tablet noticed their first relief by 22.9 hours, 55.7 percent by 24 hours, and 81 percent by 48 hours. Complete relief took a median of 73.5 hours, about 3 days; at 72 hours, 93.7 percent had some relief and 47.8 percent had complete relief.[14] In the same trial, a single-dose butoconazole vaginal cream brought first relief faster, but time to complete relief did not differ significantly between the two.[14]

How much fluconazole do you take, and when is a second dose used?

  • Uncomplicated infection: a single 150 mg oral dose.[1][2]
  • Severe infection: CDC recommends fluconazole 150 mg in two sequential doses, the second 72 hours after the first, or topical azole therapy for 7 to 14 days.[2] The 2016 IDSA guideline recommends 150 mg every 72 hours for a total of 2 or 3 doses.[13]
  • Other complicated infection: IDSA describes topical therapy for 5 to 7 days or fluconazole 150 mg every 72 hours for 3 doses.[13]
  • Recurrent infection suppression: an induction course to clear the current episode, then fluconazole once weekly for six months. CDC lists induction with a 100, 150, or 200 mg dose every third day for three doses (days 1, 4, and 7), then that dose weekly.[2] IDSA recommends 10 to 14 days of induction with a topical agent or oral fluconazole, then 150 mg weekly for 6 months.[13] The landmark NEJM trial used three 150 mg doses 72 hours apart, then 150 mg weekly.[7]

In both guidelines, the extra doses are tied to severe, complicated, or recurrent infection rather than to the uncomplicated single-dose regimen.[2][13] Fluconazole can be taken with or without food, and single-dose therapy for vaginal candidiasis does not need adjustment for impaired kidney function.[1]

How well does one fluconazole pill work?

For uncomplicated vulvovaginal candidiasis, CDC reports that azole treatment relieves symptoms and produces negative cultures in 80 to 90 percent of patients who complete therapy.[2] IDSA reports that single-dose or 3-day fluconazole achieves a response above 90 percent in uncomplicated infection.[13]

Reported cure rates vary with how cure is defined and when it is measured:

  • U.S. label trials (448 women on fluconazole): one month after treatment, clinical cure was 69 percent with fluconazole and 72 percent with 7 days of clotrimazole or miconazole, and therapeutic cure (symptoms gone plus negative microscopy and culture) was 55 percent in both groups. Women with fewer than 4 episodes a year had 80 percent clinical cure with the single tablet; women with 4 or more episodes a year had 57 percent.[1]
  • 1995 U.S. multicenter trial (429 women): at day 14, 94 percent on single-dose fluconazole and 97 percent on 7-day clotrimazole were clinically cured or improved; at day 35, 75 percent of both groups remained clinically cured. Women with a history of recurrent infection were significantly less likely to respond.[15]
  • 2015 phase 3 study in Japan (157 women): on day 28 in the main efficacy analysis, clinical cure was 81.6 percent and mycologic eradication 85.9 percent.[5]
  • 1992 pilot trial (22 women): a single 200 mg oral dose and 3 days of terconazole suppositories showed no statistically significant difference in cure or symptom relief.[4]

A 2025 network meta-analysis of 50 randomized trials (7,208 participants) found every treatment studied highly effective compared with placebo. Single-day 150 mg fluconazole was slightly more effective than multiple-day topical therapy for early clinical cure, while late clinical cure did not differ significantly between oral and topical treatment.[6]

Oral fluconazole offers one-pill convenience, while topical azoles act mainly where they are applied and avoid the drug interactions of an oral azole. IDSA's 2016 guideline concluded that oral and topical antifungal formulations achieve entirely equivalent results.[13] The 2025 meta-analysis concluded that, given the high efficacy of both oral and topical drugs, a strict ranking of treatments for uncomplicated infection is probably inappropriate.[6]

Can you tell it is a yeast infection on your own?

Often not. Typical yeast infection symptoms are itching, vaginal soreness, pain with sex, external burning when urinating, and abnormal discharge, but CDC notes that none of these symptoms is specific for yeast infection.[2] In a study of 95 women with symptoms who bought an over-the-counter antifungal to treat a presumed yeast infection, clinical examination and laboratory tests confirmed yeast infection in only 33.7 percent. The others had bacterial vaginosis (18.9 percent), mixed vaginitis (21.1 percent), trichomonas (2.1 percent), other diagnoses (10.5 percent), or normal findings (13.7 percent), and women with a past clinical diagnosis of yeast infection were no more accurate than women without one.[16]

CDC similarly advises that a prior clinician diagnosis does not make women more likely to diagnose themselves correctly, and that anyone whose symptoms persist after an over-the-counter product, or return within 2 months of treatment, should be evaluated clinically and tested.[2] CDC recommends a wet mount with 10 percent KOH for all women with symptoms or signs, notes that yeast infection is associated with a normal vaginal pH (below 4.5), and adds that finding Candida without symptoms is not a reason to treat, because about 10 to 20 percent of women carry yeast in the vagina.[2] An estimated 75 percent of women have at least one episode in their lifetime, and 40 to 45 percent have two or more.[2]

What are the side effects of fluconazole?

In U.S. comparative studies of the single 150 mg dose for vaginal candidiasis, side effects possibly related to fluconazole occurred in 26 percent of 448 patients, compared with 16 percent of 422 patients on intravaginal treatments. Most were mild to moderate.[1]

Indigestion and dizziness were each reported in about 1 percent.[1] The label notes that dizziness or seizures may occasionally occur, and the patient information advises not to drive or operate machinery until you know how the medicine affects you.[1] Rare cases of serious liver reactions have been reported; the label advises caution in people with liver dysfunction and stopping the drug if signs of liver disease develop.[1]

Rarely, anaphylaxis and angioedema (swelling) have been reported, and exfoliative (peeling) skin disorders have occurred during treatment.[1] If you develop a rash, blistering, or swelling after taking fluconazole, seek care promptly.

Which medicines interact with fluconazole?

Fluconazole is a moderate inhibitor of the liver enzymes CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 and a strong inhibitor of CYP2C19, so it can raise the levels of medicines those enzymes process. Because of its long half-life, this effect persists 4 to 5 days after the last dose.[1] Always tell your prescriber about every medicine and supplement you take before a course of fluconazole, even a single dose.

During post-marketing surveillance there have been rare cases of QT prolongation and torsade de pointes, mostly in seriously ill patients with multiple risk factors, and the label notes that people with low potassium or advanced heart failure are at increased risk.[1]

Can you take fluconazole while pregnant or breastfeeding?

The current FDA label says use in pregnancy should be avoided except in patients with severe or potentially life-threatening fungal infections, when the anticipated benefit outweighs the possible risk to the fetus. It notes that epidemiological studies suggest a potential risk of spontaneous abortion and congenital abnormalities after 150 mg taken as a single or repeated dose in the first trimester, although these studies have limitations and the findings have not been confirmed in controlled clinical trials. Separately, case reports describe a distinct pattern of birth defects after high doses (400 to 800 mg a day) during most or all of the first trimester.[1] CDC notes that a single 150 mg dose might be associated with spontaneous abortion and congenital anomalies and should not be used in pregnancy; it recommends only topical azole therapies, applied for 7 days.[2] In a 2024 randomized trial of 96 pregnant women, a single 300 mg sertaconazole vaginal suppository and a single 500 mg clotrimazole vaginal suppository had comparable cure rates, and no side effects were reported. These single-dose regimens differ from the 7-day course CDC recommends.[11]

Fluconazole was present in low levels in breast milk after a single 150 mg dose, and the label advises caution when it is given to a nursing woman.[1] A 2024 review notes that topical therapy is preferred for breastfeeding mothers.[8] If you are breastfeeding, discuss the options with your clinician.

How is fluconazole used for recurrent yeast infections?

CDC usually defines recurrent infection as three or more symptomatic episodes in less than a year, and estimates it affects fewer than 5 percent of women.[2] IDSA's 2016 guideline uses four or more episodes within one year.[13] For these women, fluconazole has a second, long-standing role as maintenance therapy. In the landmark 2004 NEJM trial by Sobel and colleagues, 387 women first received three 150 mg doses 72 hours apart and were then randomized to weekly fluconazole 150 mg or placebo for six months. At six months, 90.8 percent on fluconazole remained disease-free versus 35.9 percent on placebo, and the median time to recurrence was 10.2 months versus 4.0 months.[7] Protection faded after treatment stopped: at 12 months, six months after the last dose, 42.9 percent versus 21.9 percent remained disease-free, and the authors concluded that a long-term cure remains difficult to achieve.[7] CDC likewise notes that suppressive maintenance therapy is effective but rarely curative long-term,[2] and IDSA anticipates a 40 to 50 percent recurrence rate after maintenance stops.[13]

Newer oral options for recurrent infection have been tested in phase 3 trials against placebo. With oteseconazole, about 4 to 7 percent of women had a recurrence through week 48, versus about 39 to 43 percent on placebo.[12] With once-monthly ibrexafungerp, 70.8 percent had no mycologically proven recurrence by the test-of-cure visit, versus 58.5 percent on placebo.[9] Both are covered in the recurrent yeast infection guide.

Why didn't fluconazole work?

If a yeast infection does not clear with fluconazole, or returns quickly, the leading explanations are:

  • It was not a yeast infection. In the over-the-counter study above, only about one in three women who self-diagnosed actually had a yeast infection.[16]
  • A non-albicans species. CDC notes that C. glabrata and other non-albicans species are found in 10 to 20 percent of women with recurrent infection, and that conventional antifungal therapies work less well against them. CDC recommends vaginal culture or PCR testing for complicated infection, and susceptibility testing for people who remain culture-positive despite maintenance therapy.[2] IDSA notes that most Candida species respond to oral fluconazole, with the exception of C. krusei and C. glabrata,[13] and the FDA label describes superinfection with species such as C. krusei that are often inherently not susceptible to fluconazole.[1]
  • Acquired fluconazole resistance in C. albicans. IDSA described it in 2016 as extremely rare but documented after prolonged azole exposure,[13] while CDC's 2021 guideline reports that azole resistance in C. albicans is becoming more common in vaginal isolates.[2] A 2024 review links it to a growing share of recurrent cases, particularly in women with significant prior fluconazole exposure.[8]
  • A history of recurrent infection. In both the label trials and the 1995 U.S. trial, women with recurrent infection responded less often to a single dose.[1][15]

For non-albicans infection, CDC recommends a longer course (7 to 14 days) of a non-fluconazole azole, oral or topical, and, if it recurs, boric acid 600 mg in a gelatin capsule placed in the vagina once daily for 3 weeks, a regimen with clinical and mycologic eradication rates of about 70 percent.[2] For C. glabrata that does not respond to oral azoles, IDSA lists vaginal boric acid 600 mg daily for 14 days or nystatin vaginal suppositories 100,000 units daily for 14 days.[13] Boric acid is not used in pregnancy because of teratogenic effects. A 2024 review of fluconazole-resistant infection also discusses nystatin and the newer agents oteseconazole and ibrexafungerp.[8] Ibrexafungerp works differently from the azoles and was more effective than placebo for acute infection in a phase 3 trial.[10]

Do sex partners need treatment?

Usually not. CDC notes that uncomplicated yeast infection is not usually acquired through sexual intercourse and that data do not support treating sex partners. A minority of male partners develop balanitis, with red areas on the head of the penis plus itching or irritation, and these men benefit from treatment with topical antifungal agents. For complicated infection, CDC found no data to support treating partners and makes no recommendation.[2]

Do you need a prescription for fluconazole?

Yes, in the United States. Fluconazole (Diflucan) tablets are prescription products,[3] so a clinician must prescribe them, either in person or through a telehealth visit. Prices vary by pharmacy and insurance plan. Over-the-counter topical azoles, such as clotrimazole and miconazole, are an alternative that needs no prescription.[2]

What can be handled by video, and what needs an in-person visit?

A video visit suits the parts of care that rely on history: confirming that symptoms fit an uncomplicated pattern, checking pregnancy status, and reviewing every medicine for interactions. It cannot include a pelvic exam or a lab sample. CDC recommends a wet mount with KOH for women with symptoms, vaginal culture or PCR for complicated infection, and susceptibility testing when someone remains culture-positive despite maintenance therapy.[2] Those steps need an in-person visit or a lab.

  • Often suitable for video: a first or infrequent episode with typical, mild to moderate symptoms in a non-pregnant adult, with a medication list that has been reviewed for interactions.
  • Needs in-person evaluation and testing: symptoms that persist after treatment or return within 2 months,[2] recurrent or severe infection, suspected non-albicans infection,[2] pregnancy,[2] or pelvic or lower abdominal pain.[17]

When should you seek urgent care?

  • Signs of a serious allergic reaction after a dose, such as shortness of breath, wheezing, swelling of the face, mouth, or neck, hives, blisters, or peeling skin: the label advises stopping the medicine and calling a clinician or going to the nearest emergency room right away.[1]
  • Signs of liver problems, such as yellowing of the skin or eyes, dark urine, light-colored stools, vomiting, or loss of appetite: tell a clinician right away.[1]
  • Pelvic or lower abdominal pain, especially with fever. CDC's guidance on pelvic inflammatory disease applies to women with pelvic or lower abdominal pain, and lists severe illness, nausea and vomiting, or a temperature above 38.5°C (101°F) among the reasons to consider hospital care.[17]
  • Pregnancy: do not take fluconazole without a clinician; CDC recommends only topical azoles, applied for 7 days.[2]
  • Symptoms that persist after treatment or return within 2 months: CDC advises clinical evaluation and testing.[2]
