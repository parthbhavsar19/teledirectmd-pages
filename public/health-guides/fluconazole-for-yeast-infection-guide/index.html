Fluconazole for Yeast Infection

A physician's evidence-based guide to oral fluconazole for vaginal yeast infection: how it works, the 150 mg single dose, effectiveness, safety, interactions, and when it fails.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: September 8, 2026
20 min read

How well does a single 150 mg dose of fluconazole treat a yeast infection?

Fluconazole (Diflucan) is the oral option in CDC's 2021 treatment guidelines for uncomplicated vaginal yeast infection, taken as a single 150 mg dose.[2] In a 2025 network meta-analysis of 50 randomized trials, every oral and topical treatment studied was highly effective compared with placebo (over 75 percent), and single-dose fluconazole was slightly more effective than multiple-day topical therapy for early clinical cure.[6] CDC notes that azole treatment relieves symptoms and clears cultures in 80 to 90 percent of people who complete therapy.[2] Fluconazole works by blocking a fungal enzyme, lanosterol 14-alpha-demethylase, that converts lanosterol to ergosterol.[1] Because it is taken by mouth it can interact with other medicines, and the FDA label says use in pregnancy should be avoided except for severe or potentially life-threatening fungal infections. CDC recommends only 7-day topical azoles in pregnancy.[1][2] When an infection persists or returns despite fluconazole, fluconazole-resistant Candida or a non-albicans species may be responsible, and alternative treatments exist.[8]
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated September 8, 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • Fluconazole is a prescription oral antifungal.[3] CDC lists a single 150 mg oral dose for uncomplicated vaginal yeast infection.[2]
  • In a 2025 meta-analysis of randomized trials, single-dose fluconazole and topical azoles were all highly effective, with fluconazole slightly ahead for early clinical cure.[6]
  • It blocks a fungal enzyme (lanosterol 14-alpha-demethylase) that converts lanosterol to ergosterol, and more than 90 percent of an oral dose is absorbed.[1]
  • Weekly 150 mg fluconazole is the classic maintenance therapy for recurrent infections, keeping about 91 percent of women in the landmark trial disease-free at six months.[7]
  • The FDA label says to avoid it in pregnancy except for severe or potentially life-threatening fungal infections, and it interacts with medicines including warfarin and some statins.[1]
  • When fluconazole no longer works, fluconazole-resistant Candida or a non-albicans species such as C. glabrata may be the cause.[8][2]

For many people, a single pill is the simplest way to treat a yeast infection, and the oral option in CDC guidelines is fluconazole.[2] This guide explains what fluconazole is, how it works, how well it works, its real safety considerations, and what to do when it stops working.

What Is Fluconazole

Fluconazole is a triazole antifungal taken by mouth, best known by the brand name Diflucan.[1] The FDA first approved Diflucan in 1990.[3] For vaginal candidiasis, CDC lists it as a single 150 mg oral dose, a regimen that makes treating an uncomplicated infection a one-tablet event.[2]

Fluconazole (Diflucan) tablets are prescription products in the United States,[3] in contrast to the topical azoles miconazole, clotrimazole, and tioconazole, which CDC lists as over-the-counter vaginal treatments.[2]

How It Works

Fluconazole is a highly selective inhibitor of a fungal enzyme called lanosterol 14-alpha-demethylase, which converts lanosterol to ergosterol, a sterol the fungus needs. The resulting loss of normal sterols may be responsible for fluconazole's fungistatic effect, meaning it halts fungal growth.[1]

When swallowed, more than 90 percent of a fluconazole dose is absorbed, and its plasma half-life is about 30 hours (range 20 to 50 hours). In studies described on the label, a single 150 mg dose reached both vaginal tissue and vaginal fluid.[1]

Dosing for Yeast Infection

  • Uncomplicated infection: a single 150 mg oral dose.[2]
  • Severe infection: fluconazole 150 mg in two doses, the second 72 hours after the first (or topical azole therapy for 7 to 14 days), per CDC guidance.[2]
  • Recurrent infection suppression: an induction course to clear the current episode, then fluconazole once weekly for six months. CDC lists induction with a 100, 150, or 200 mg dose every third day for three doses, then that dose weekly.[2] The landmark NEJM trial used three 150 mg doses 72 hours apart, then 150 mg weekly.[7]

Fluconazole can be taken with or without food.[1]

Effectiveness

For uncomplicated vulvovaginal candidiasis, CDC states that azole treatment relieves symptoms and produces negative cultures in 80 to 90 percent of patients who complete therapy.[2] In a 2015 phase 3 study in Japan, 157 women received a single 150 mg dose; in the main efficacy analysis on day 28, the clinical cure rate was 81.6 percent and the mycologic eradication rate was 85.9 percent.[5] An early pilot trial of 22 women compared a single 200 mg oral dose with 3 days of terconazole vaginal suppositories and found no statistically significant difference in cure or symptom relief.[4]

A 2025 network meta-analysis of 50 randomized trials (7,208 participants) found every treatment studied highly effective compared with placebo. Single-day 150 mg fluconazole was slightly more effective than multiple-day topical therapy for early clinical cure, while late clinical cure did not differ significantly between oral and topical treatment.[6]

Oral fluconazole offers one-pill convenience, while topical azoles act mainly where they are applied and avoid the drug interactions of an oral azole. The 2025 meta-analysis concluded that, given the high efficacy of both oral and topical drugs, a strict ranking of treatments for uncomplicated infection is probably inappropriate.[6]

Side Effects

In U.S. comparative studies of the single 150 mg dose for vaginal candidiasis, side effects possibly related to fluconazole occurred in 26 percent of patients, compared with 16 percent on intravaginal treatments. The most common were headache (13 percent), nausea (7 percent), and abdominal pain (6 percent), followed by diarrhea (3 percent).[1] Rare cases of serious liver reactions have been reported; the label advises caution in people with liver dysfunction and stopping the drug if signs of liver disease develop.[1]

Rarely, anaphylaxis and angioedema (swelling) have been reported, and exfoliative (peeling) skin disorders have occurred during treatment.[1] If you develop a rash, blistering, or swelling after taking fluconazole, seek care promptly.

Drug Interactions

Fluconazole is a moderate inhibitor of the liver enzymes CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 and a strong inhibitor of CYP2C19, so it can raise the levels of medicines those enzymes process. Because of its long half-life, this effect persists 4 to 5 days after the last dose. Interactions described on the label include:[1]

  • Warfarin and similar anticoagulants (prothrombin time can rise; monitoring and dose adjustment may be needed)
  • Certain statins such as atorvastatin and simvastatin (higher risk of muscle injury, including rhabdomyolysis)
  • QT-prolonging drugs (fluconazole has been linked to rare QT prolongation and torsade de pointes, and use with erythromycin, pimozide, or quinidine is contraindicated)
  • Phenytoin, an anti-seizure drug, and sulfonylurea diabetes pills such as glipizide and glyburide (levels rise; low blood sugar has occurred with sulfonylureas)

Always tell your prescriber about every medicine and supplement you take before a course of fluconazole, even a single dose.

Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

The current FDA label says use in pregnancy should be avoided except in patients with severe or potentially life-threatening fungal infections, when the anticipated benefit outweighs the possible risk to the fetus. It notes that epidemiological studies suggest a potential risk of spontaneous abortion and congenital abnormalities after 150 mg taken as a single or repeated dose in the first trimester, although these studies have limitations and the findings have not been confirmed in controlled clinical trials. Separately, case reports describe a distinct pattern of birth defects after high doses (400 to 800 mg a day) during most or all of the first trimester.[1] CDC states that a single 150 mg dose might be associated with spontaneous abortion and congenital anomalies and should not be used in pregnancy; it recommends only topical azole therapies, applied for 7 days.[2] In a 2024 randomized trial of 96 pregnant women, a single 300 mg sertaconazole vaginal suppository and a single 500 mg clotrimazole vaginal suppository had comparable cure rates, and no side effects were reported. These single-dose regimens differ from the 7-day course CDC recommends.[11]

Fluconazole was present in low levels in breast milk after a single 150 mg dose, and the label advises caution when it is given to a nursing woman.[1] A 2024 review notes that topical therapy is preferred for breastfeeding mothers.[8] If you are breastfeeding, discuss the options with your clinician.

Fluconazole for Recurrent Infection

For women with recurrent infection, which CDC usually defines as three or more symptomatic episodes in less than a year, fluconazole has a second, long-standing role as maintenance therapy.[2] In the landmark 2004 NEJM trial by Sobel and colleagues, 387 women first received three 150 mg doses 72 hours apart and were then randomized to weekly fluconazole 150 mg or placebo for six months. At six months, 90.8 percent on fluconazole remained disease-free versus 35.9 percent on placebo, and the median time to recurrence was 10.2 months versus 4.0 months.[7] Protection faded after treatment stopped: at 12 months, six months after the last dose, 42.9 percent versus 21.9 percent remained disease-free, and the authors concluded that a long-term cure remains difficult to achieve.[7] CDC likewise notes that suppressive maintenance therapy is effective but rarely curative long-term.[2]

Newer oral options for recurrent infection have been tested in phase 3 trials against placebo. With oteseconazole, about 4 to 7 percent of women had a recurrence through week 48, versus about 39 to 43 percent on placebo.[12] With once-monthly ibrexafungerp, 70.8 percent had no mycologically proven recurrence by the test-of-cure visit, versus 58.5 percent on placebo.[9] Both are covered in the recurrent yeast infection guide.

When Fluconazole Fails

If a yeast infection does not clear with fluconazole, or returns quickly, the two leading explanations are:

  • A non-albicans species. CDC notes that C. glabrata and other non-albicans species are found in 10 to 20 percent of women with recurrent infection, and that conventional antifungal therapies work less well against them. CDC recommends vaginal culture or PCR testing for complicated infection, and susceptibility testing for people who remain culture-positive despite maintenance therapy.[2] A 2024 review adds that some non-albicans species have inherent fluconazole resistance.[8]
  • Acquired fluconazole resistance in C. albicans, which research links to a growing share of recurrent cases, particularly in women with significant prior fluconazole exposure.[8]

For non-albicans infection, CDC recommends a longer course (7 to 14 days) of a non-fluconazole azole, oral or topical, and, if it recurs, boric acid 600 mg in a gelatin capsule placed in the vagina once daily for 3 weeks.[2] Boric acid is not used in pregnancy because of teratogenic effects. A 2024 review of fluconazole-resistant infection also discusses nystatin and the newer agents oteseconazole and ibrexafungerp.[8] Ibrexafungerp works differently from the azoles and was more effective than placebo for acute infection in a phase 3 trial.[10]

Cost and Access

Fluconazole (Diflucan) tablets are prescription products in the United States,[3] so a clinician must prescribe them, either in person or through a telehealth visit. Prices vary by pharmacy and insurance plan. Over-the-counter topical azoles, such as clotrimazole and miconazole, are an alternative that needs no prescription.[2]

Red Flags

Seek care if symptoms persist after treatment or return within 2 months; CDC advises clinical evaluation and testing in that situation.[2] Seek care promptly for a rash, blistering, or swelling after a dose, which can signal a drug reaction.[1] Also seek care if you are pregnant, or if you have unexplained fever, pelvic pain, or a new kind of discharge, since these can point to a different diagnosis.
