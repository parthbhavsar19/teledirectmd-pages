Gout vs Pseudogout

A physician's guide to the two crystal arthritides that look alike: gout (urate crystals) and pseudogout (calcium pyrophosphate crystals), how they differ, and how they are treated.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed September 8, 2026
17 min read

What is the difference between gout and pseudogout?

Both cause sudden, painful joint swelling, hence the shared name, but they are caused by different crystals. Gout comes from monosodium urate crystals that form when urate builds up, often first hitting a big toe, and is treated long-term with urate-lowering drugs, with allopurinol preferred first-line per the 2020 ACR gout guideline. Pseudogout, properly called calcium pyrophosphate deposition (CPPD) disease, comes from calcium pyrophosphate crystals that form in cartilage, most often strikes the knee and then the wrist, is rare before age 60, and has no urate-lowering or disease-modifying drug. Instead, pseudogout flare treatment is anti-inflammatory (a steroid injected into the joint, colchicine, an NSAID, or an oral steroid; see the EULAR recommendations), and associated conditions such as hyperparathyroidism, hemochromatosis, and low magnesium are screened for. The only way to tell them apart with certainty is to examine joint fluid and identify the crystal type.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated September 8, 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • Gout is caused by monosodium urate crystals; pseudogout (CPPD) by calcium pyrophosphate crystals. They are different diseases. [1][11]
  • Gout's first flare is often in a big toe; pseudogout most often affects the knee, followed by the wrist, and rarely the big toe. [11][1]
  • Gout results from chronically high urate, but a high urate level alone does not diagnose it; pseudogout is not a urate disease, and the two can coexist. [5][8][1]
  • Only crystal analysis of joint fluid identifies which crystal is present and definitively tells them apart. [6][8]
  • Pseudogout has no urate-lowering or disease-modifying drug; acute attacks are treated with a steroid injected into the joint, colchicine, an NSAID, or an oral steroid. [1][2]
  • Pseudogout should prompt a check for associated conditions such as hyperparathyroidism, hemochromatosis, and low magnesium, especially in people younger than 60. [1][6]

Two Different Crystals

The two diseases are close mimics because both produce a sudden, hot, swollen, painful joint, and an acute pseudogout attack can be indistinguishable from gout or a joint infection.[1] The difference is at the microscopic level. Gout forms needle-shaped monosodium urate crystals in the joints when urate builds up.[11] Pseudogout, or calcium pyrophosphate deposition (CPPD) disease, forms rhomboid, positively birefringent calcium pyrophosphate crystals in cartilage, and these crystals often show only weak birefringence.[1]

Because treatments diverge, it matters which crystal is present. Gout is managed toward a serum urate target below 6 mg/dL, with allopurinol as the preferred first-line urate-lowering drug.[3] Pseudogout has no disease-modulating medicine; it is managed with anti-inflammatory treatment of attacks and screening for any associated metabolic condition.[1][2]

Which Joints Each Affects

The first metatarsophalangeal joint (the big toe) is the classic gout joint: many people have their first gout flare in a big toe, and EULAR lists involvement of a single foot or ankle joint, especially the big toe, as a feature that supports a clinical diagnosis of gout.[11][8] Gout can also affect other joints.[11] Acute pseudogout is most common in the knee, followed by the wrist, and an acute big-toe attack is rare.[1] Chronic CPPD also favors the shoulder, wrist, and knuckle (metacarpophalangeal) joints, and attacks can involve the ankles too.[1][12]

Joint pattern is a helpful clue but not proof. A first big-toe flare in a man with high urate fits the features that point toward gout, while a knee or wrist attack in someone over 60 fits CPPD.[8][1] The diagnosis is confirmed by crystals, not by location alone.[1][6]

Serum Urate: High vs Normal

Gout results from chronic elevation of uric acid above the saturation point for urate crystal formation.[5] The 2020 ACR gout guideline defines hyperuricemia as a serum urate of 6.8 mg/dL or higher, and sets a target below 6 mg/dL for people taking urate-lowering therapy.[3] Even so, a diagnosis of gout should not be based on high urate alone, and many people with high serum urate never develop gout.[8][11] Pseudogout runs on a different chemical: inorganic pyrophosphate plays a role in CPPD that is analogous to the role of urate in gout.[1] The two conditions can coexist: as many as 5% of people with gout have calcium pyrophosphate crystals in their joint fluid.[1]

Who Gets Which

Gout is more common in men and usually develops in middle age; women usually do not develop it before menopause.[11] Risk factors include male sex, obesity, alcohol intake, diuretic use, a diet rich in meat and seafood, and chronic kidney disease.[9] Pseudogout is strongly age-related and rare in people younger than 60; on X-ray, cartilage calcification doubles in prevalence with each decade over 60 and is seen in 44% of people older than 84.[1] As many as 5% of people with gout also have calcium pyrophosphate crystals in their joint fluid, which is one reason a single hot joint still needs a careful look.[1]

How the Diagnosis Is Made

The definitive test is joint-fluid analysis: removing a small sample of fluid with a needle and examining it under a polarized-light microscope. Finding monosodium urate crystals gives a definite diagnosis of gout, and definitive diagnosis of CPPD relies on finding calcium pyrophosphate crystals.[8][6] Under compensated polarized light with a red filter, the two crystals show opposite color patterns, and calcium pyrophosphate crystals are positively birefringent and rhomboid-shaped.[1] In the 2023 ACR/EULAR classification criteria, calcium pyrophosphate crystals in joint fluid are on their own enough to classify a person with joint symptoms as having CPPD disease.[7] The same fluid can be sent for a cell count, Gram stain, and culture, which is how septic arthritis, a joint infection that can look identical, is confirmed.[13][10] A delay in diagnosing and treating septic arthritis can result in permanent harm or death.[10]

Imaging adds support. In gout, ultrasound can show a double-contour sign and dual-energy CT can show urate deposits; studies found both useful but imperfect.[4][8] In CPPD, X-rays can show calcium in cartilage (chondrocalcinosis), but this X-ray sign is not highly sensitive or specific, and ultrasound appears more useful in peripheral joints.[1][6] These findings support but do not replace crystal confirmation when the diagnosis is uncertain.[1][8]

What Causes Pseudogout

CPPD is mostly a disease of aging, but it can be the presenting sign of an underlying metabolic condition. Well-established associations include hyperparathyroidism, hemochromatosis, low magnesium (hypomagnesemia), and hypophosphatasia, and screening for them is recommended, particularly in people younger than 60.[1][6]

Unlike gout, pseudogout has no known dietary associations and is not driven by urate.[1] Attacks often occur during an acute illness, after a joint injury, or after surgery, a different trigger profile from gout, where alcohol and purine-rich foods are linked to a higher risk of flares.[1][3]

Acute attacks of pseudogout are treated with anti-inflammatory strategies borrowed from gout care: a corticosteroid injected into the joint is often first-line for joints that can be injected, and colchicine, an NSAID, or a systemic corticosteroid are alternatives.[1] For an acute attack, cool packs, temporary rest, and draining the joint combined with a steroid injection are often enough.[2] For gout flares, colchicine, NSAIDs, or glucocorticoids are the strongly recommended first-line options.[3] The major divergence is long-term: gout has urate-lowering therapy, with allopurinol preferred first-line, while CPPD has no disease-modulating treatment, so recurrent pseudogout is managed with an NSAID or low-dose colchicine for prevention and screening for any associated metabolic disorder.[3][1][2]

Can You Have Both?

Yes. Gout and CPPD can coexist: as many as 5% of people with gout have calcium pyrophosphate crystals in their joint fluid.[1] When both are present, urate-lowering therapy addresses the gout, while flares are treated with anti-inflammatory medicines used for both conditions.[3][1] This is another reason crystal identification, rather than guessing from urate alone, guides care.[8]

When a Hot Joint Needs Urgent Care

Seek same-day emergency evaluation for fever or chills with a hot joint, rapidly spreading redness, a wound near the joint, inability to bear weight, or severe illness.[10][13] The most important distinction in an acutely hot joint is not gout versus pseudogout, it is either versus septic arthritis, which requires prompt joint-fluid analysis and antibiotics.[9][10] Fever and chills can also occur with a pseudogout attack itself, so a hot joint with fever should never be assumed to be crystal arthritis without evaluation.[1][10]
