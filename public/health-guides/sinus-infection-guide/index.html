Sinus Infection Treatment: The 10-Day Decision

An adult guide to symptom patterns, watchful waiting, antibiotic choices and the warning signs that should not wait.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MDLast reviewed October 29, 202620 min read

When does an adult sinus infection need antibiotics?

Most adult sinus infections do not need immediate antibiotics. Bacterial disease becomes more likely when compatible symptoms last at least 10 days without improvement, worsen after initial improvement, or are severe. Uncomplicated cases may use watchful waiting with follow-up; when an antibiotic is chosen, 5 to 7 days is usual for most adults. Eye or neurological warning signs need urgent assessment.[1][2][3][7][8]

The 10-day threshold is not a reason to delay urgent care. Eye swelling, vision changes, a severe worsening headache or neurological symptoms require urgent direct assessment. Do not wait for a routine video visit or a course of antibiotics to finish.[1][2][3][8][12]

Key Takeaways

  • The duration and pattern of symptoms matter. Persistent compatible symptoms for at least 10 days without improvement, double worsening or severe illness raises concern for bacterial disease.[1][2][3][7]
  • Yellow or green mucus does not, by itself, establish a bacterial infection or a need for antibiotics.[3][7][8]
  • The 2025 AAO-HNS guideline supports watchful waiting for uncomplicated adult bacterial rhinosinusitis when follow-up is assured. Observation is an active plan, not abandonment.[1][3][7]
  • If an antibiotic is chosen, the 2025 guideline lists amoxicillin with or without clavulanate for 5 to 7 days for most adults. Patient-specific risks and other guidance affect the selection.[1][2][3]
  • A 2026 observational comparison found similar claims-defined failure rates for amoxicillin and amoxicillin-clavulanate. It did not establish that everyone with sinus symptoms needs treatment.[5]
  • For sinus rinsing, use distilled, sterile or appropriately boiled and cooled water. Water safe to drink is not automatically safe to put into the nose.[9]
  • Eye swelling, visual changes, severe worsening headache or neurological symptoms require urgent direct assessment rather than a routine video-treatment pathway.[1][2][3][8][12]

Sinus infection symptoms: when does the pattern suggest bacteria?

Do I need antibiotics for a sinus infection?

Not automatically. Acute rhinosinusitis describes inflammation of the nasal passages and sinuses; it often follows a viral respiratory infection. A bacterial infection is more likely in certain symptom patterns, but congestion, facial pressure or colored discharge alone is not enough to choose an antibiotic. Start with the illness timeline and any warning signs.[1][2][3][7][8]

Persistent symptoms without improvement

The 2025 AAO-HNS guideline uses compatible symptoms lasting at least 10 days without improvement as one pattern supporting acute bacterial rhinosinusitis. The symptom combination matters: purulent nasal drainage accompanied by nasal obstruction, facial pain or pressure is different from an isolated headache. Reaching day 10 is a reason for assessment, not automatic proof of bacteria.[1][2][3]

Double worsening

An illness that initially improves and then becomes worse again can also suggest bacterial disease. Describe what improved, what returned and when. A new fever or a clear return of nasal symptoms after improvement provides more useful information than calling every lingering cold a sinus infection.[1][2][3][7]

Severe illness or complications

Severe symptoms deserve earlier review. IDSA and CDC guidance include a severe presentation as another diagnostic pattern; the 10-day observation is not a requirement to endure a dangerous illness at home. Eye or neurological warning signs need urgent direct assessment, not just an earlier antibiotic prescription.[1][2][3][7][8]

Give the timeline, not only a symptom label

Before an assessment, write down the first day of symptoms and whether you had a period of clear improvement. Describe any measured fever, where the pain is, and whether it is becoming more intense. Include medicines already tried, recent antibiotic use and what happened with previous drug reactions. This history helps the clinician separate a persistent but improving illness from a new deterioration. It is more useful than deciding in advance that the problem must be bacterial.[1][2][3]

Sinus infection vs cold or allergies: what is different?

A cold can inflame the sinuses without becoming a bacterial infection. Nasal blockage, discharge and facial pressure overlap, so the word “sinus” does not identify the cause. In an early illness that is steadily improving, symptom care is often more appropriate than an antibiotic. A clinician looks for the persistent, worsening or severe patterns described above.[1][2][3][7][8]

Allergy-related congestion

Rhinitis can cause congestion that resembles a sinus infection. A history of itching, sneezing and recurring triggers can help direct the assessment. Nasal allergy medicines address that process, not an undiagnosed bacterial infection; a product marketed for allergies should not be presented as a cure for a common cold.[3][12][14]

Why routine scans are usually unnecessary

Uncomplicated acute rhinosinusitis is usually assessed clinically. Routine imaging is not recommended simply to decide whether an ordinary case needs antibiotics. A scan or a hands-on examination may become appropriate when complications, an alternative diagnosis or a persistent problem is suspected.[1][2][3]

Does yellow or green mucus mean you need an antibiotic?

No. Discharge color alone does not reliably separate viral from bacterial rhinosinusitis. A clinician weighs the full symptom combination, duration, severity and whether the illness improved before getting worse. An isolated photograph of mucus is not a bacterial test.[1][3][7][8]

For a useful history, note when symptoms started, whether they are improving, how much pain or fever is present, and whether you can eat, drink and function. Tell the clinician about recent antibiotics, major immune problems, relevant allergies and previous unusual or recurrent episodes. These details can change the assessment even when the discharge looks similar.[1][2][3]

Do not use a darker color as a reason to start leftover amoxicillin. That can expose you to side effects without resolving the actual problem, and it complicates later treatment decisions. A worsening illness needs reassessment rather than a self-directed switch to a broader drug.[1][2][3][11]

How long does a sinus infection last?

Recovery varies with the cause and severity. Some uncomplicated episodes settle over the first couple of weeks, while general patient guidance notes that sinusitis can take up to about 4 weeks to clear. That upper range is not an instruction to wait a month without review if symptoms have not improved by day 10, or if they worsen at any point.[1][3][4][8][12]

Time since onset is different from trial follow-up

The 2018 Cochrane review found that, without antibiotics, 46% of participants were cured after one week of trial follow-up and 64% after two weeks. The trials enrolled people at different stages of illness. These observations should not be relabeled as a promise that a particular person will be cured one or two weeks after their first symptom.[4]

Persistent and recurrent symptoms

Symptoms lasting 12 weeks or longer raise a different question about chronic rhinosinusitis and require an appropriate assessment, including objective confirmation when indicated. Repeated episodes or persistent one-sided symptoms also deserve review rather than a standing series of urgent-care antibiotic refills. Duration alone does not establish a chronic sinus diagnosis.[1][3][12]

Improvement is a trend, not a perfect day

Ask whether the overall illness is getting better rather than expecting every symptom to stop at once. Some congestion during recovery does not, by itself, prove antibiotic failure. On the other hand, clear deterioration after improvement is a reason to revisit the diagnosis. Keep the original symptom-onset date and the date of any treatment decision separate so that a follow-up clinician can interpret the timeline correctly.[1][2][3]

This guide concerns short-term adult illness and triage. It is not a substitute for ENT follow-up, evaluation of structural problems or the management of immunocompromised patients with atypical infections.[1][2][3]

Can a sinus infection go away on its own?

Yes, many uncomplicated cases improve without antibiotics, which is why current guidance supports selected observation. The fact that symptoms may resolve naturally does not make every case safe to watch at home. Complicated illness, severe deterioration and significant immune problems require a different level of assessment.[1][2][3][4][7]

What the antibiotic-versus-placebo evidence shows

The 2018 Cochrane review included 15 trials with 3,057 adults. Antibiotics produced a modest average benefit in uncomplicated cases, while causing more adverse effects. The participants were not a general sample of every severe sinus presentation, and the review could not provide firm conclusions for severe disease or immune suppression.[4]

These findings support avoiding unnecessary prescriptions, not ignoring bacterial diagnostic patterns or refusing treatment in every case. The decision depends on severity, the ability to follow up, personal risks and a shared plan. There is a meaningful difference between a clinician recommending observation and a patient being left without instructions.[1][2][3][4]

What not to infer

Improvement without antibiotics does not prove the original cause, and temporary improvement after a tablet does not establish that the tablet was necessary. Many infections change over time. Use the overall course and agreed reassessment thresholds rather than a single good or bad day as the entire treatment rule.[1][3][4]

What does watchful waiting mean in the 2025 sinusitis guideline?

The 2025 AAO-HNS guideline recommends offering watchful waiting without antibiotics for adults with uncomplicated acute bacterial rhinosinusitis when follow-up is assured. It is an active plan that includes symptom relief, a clear review point and a way to obtain treatment or direct assessment if the illness is not following the expected course.[1][3][7]

Observation and antibiotic treatment both need a follow-up plan. Dangerous symptoms override the ordinary timetable.[1][2][3][7][8]

The 3-to-5-day reassessment plan

For uncomplicated disease being observed, current guidance uses a short reassessment interval, generally 3 to 5 days after the diagnosis and management decision. That is not another 3 to 5 days to endure eye swelling, visual changes or a rapidly worsening headache. Worsening at any time calls for reassessment, and dangerous symptoms call for urgent care.[1][2][3][7][8]

Before choosing observation, make sure you know who to contact, what would trigger an earlier review, and whether a delayed prescription is part of the agreed plan. Do not invent your own rescue regimen from leftover medicines. A follow-up plan may include a new examination rather than simply adding an antibiotic.[1][2][3][8]

A delayed prescription needs actual instructions

If a clinician provides a delayed prescription, ask exactly when it should be started and when you should contact the practice instead. The existence of a prescription does not mean every subsequent symptom change is safe to manage at home. If you cannot obtain follow-up, tell the clinician before choosing observation; assured follow-up is part of the recommendation, not an optional extra. Severe deterioration changes the plan even if the original illness appeared uncomplicated.[1][3][7][8]

What do the 2012, 2015 and 2025 guideline dates mean?

IDSA's 2012 guidance is a separate society document with its own treatment recommendations. The current AAO-HNS adult sinusitis guideline is the 2025 update to its prior 2015 document. The date of a webpage or a search result is not evidence that a new comprehensive guideline was issued in 2026.[1][2][3]

Use the actual scope and date of each recommendation. A new comparative study can inform a discussion without replacing a society guideline or proving that a broader drug is the best choice for every adult.[1][2][5]

Sinus infection antibiotics: which medicine is best for an adult?

There is no single best antibiotic for every person with sinus symptoms. First decide whether an antibiotic is indicated. For most adults with uncomplicated bacterial rhinosinusitis in whom treatment is chosen, the 2025 AAO-HNS guideline lists amoxicillin with or without clavulanate for 5 to 7 days. Diagnosis, allergy history, recent antibiotic exposure and other personal risks affect the prescription.[1][2][3][7][11]

Amoxicillin versus amoxicillin-clavulanate: a real guideline distinction

IDSA's preference for the combination and AAO-HNS's inclusion of amoxicillin with or without clavulanate should not be flattened into a claim that one guideline says all adults must receive the same product. A clinician considers coverage needs and individual harms, including gastrointestinal intolerance and contraindications.[1][2][3][11]

Why a Z-Pak or a “stronger” drug is not the default

Macrolides such as azithromycin are not routine empiric choices in US bacterial sinusitis guidance because resistance matters. Fluoroquinolones also carry substantial risks; the moxifloxacin prescribing information reserves its sinusitis use for patients without other treatment options. Medication selection should not be reduced to choosing the broadest familiar name.[2][3][7][10]

A 2024 prescribing-pattern study examined guideline concordance in practice. Such records can describe how medicines are used; they do not demonstrate that each treated patient had a confirmed bacterial infection or that a popular prescription is the right one for you.[6]

Amoxicillin for sinus infection: what did the 2026 study actually find?

A 2026 JAMA study compared amoxicillin-clavulanate with amoxicillin among US adults aged 18 to 64 with acute sinusitis recorded in insurance claims. In the propensity-matched analysis, claims-defined treatment failure occurred in 3.0% versus 3.1%, respectively. Those similar observed rates are useful context, but they are not a randomized proof of equivalent bacterial cure.[5]

What the study measured

Failure was a composite of a new antibiotic or specified sinusitis-related acute care within 1 to 14 days. A change in prescription or an encounter is not the same outcome as a culture proving that bacteria survived. The study did not require a bacteriologically confirmed infection for every patient and did not include an untreated comparison group.[5]

What it cannot decide for you

The results do not show whether either antibiotic is better than no antibiotic for a viral presentation, and they should not be generalized without qualification to children, adults 65 or older or patients with complicated disease. Propensity matching addresses measured differences, not every possible source of confounding.[5]

The study did not establish a clear difference in the overall antibiotic-associated adverse-event outcome: the relative-risk confidence interval included 1. It did observe more secondary infections with the combination. Those are different outcomes and should not be collapsed into a claim that every side effect was more common. A patient's actual benefit-harm decision still depends on the need for bacterial treatment, coverage and personal contraindications.[1][2][5][11]

How long should amoxicillin take to work?

Symptoms should be monitored rather than assigned a guaranteed response time. Current AAO-HNS guidance calls for reassessment when someone worsens or fails to improve after 3 to 5 days on an appropriate antibiotic. A rapidly worsening illness or a new eye or neurological warning sign needs assessment sooner, not completion of a waiting interval.[1][2][3]

How can you get rid of sinus symptoms safely at home?

For uncomplicated illness, symptom care can include rest, fluids, a suitable pain reliever and selected nasal treatments. A warm compress may feel soothing. These measures are intended to improve comfort and function; they do not establish the cause or remove the need for reassessment if the course changes.[1][3][8][12]

Choose a small, understandable plan

Start by deciding which symptom you are trying to relieve. A medicine for pain is not the same as an allergy treatment or a short-term decongestant. Read the active ingredients before combining products and keep a list for the clinician or pharmacist. If you have already tried several cold preparations, report their names and how long you used them. Adding another product without reviewing the first ones can make side effects and duplicate ingredients harder to recognize.[3][8][12][13][14]

Pain relief

Acetaminophen or an NSAID such as ibuprofen may be suitable for pain or fever, but follow the actual product label and personal medical advice. Combination cold medicines can duplicate ingredients. Ask a clinician or pharmacist about your health conditions, pregnancy and other medicines before choosing a product rather than assuming all OTC options are interchangeable.[1][3][8][12]

Intranasal corticosteroids

The sinusitis guideline allows selected intranasal steroids for symptom relief. This is different from advertising every OTC nasal steroid as a cure for a common cold. Fluticasone products have specific allergy, rhinitis or chronic-rhinosinusitis indications depending on the formulation; use the product and directions that fit the actual problem.[1][2][3][14]

Fluticasone is not an immediate rescue decongestant. Its patient monograph describes regular use for its indicated conditions and warns about nosebleeds and other adverse effects. Discuss recent nasal surgery or injury, eye disease, other medicines and pregnancy before choosing a nasal steroid when those issues are relevant.[14]

Decongestants: short-term relief is not treatment of the cause

Some decongestants may briefly reduce stuffiness, but they are not a substitute for diagnosis and are not appropriate for everyone. IDSA does not recommend routine topical or oral decongestants as adjunctive treatment for bacterial rhinosinusitis. If you use an OTC product for temporary congestion, review its risks and limits with a pharmacist or clinician.[2][3][12][13]

Oxymetazoline nasal spray should not be used longer than 3 days; longer use can worsen or bring back congestion. The monograph also calls for advice with high blood pressure, heart or thyroid disease, diabetes, urinary problems, relevant medicines and pregnancy. This is not an instruction for everyone with sinus pressure to start it.[13]

How do you rinse your sinuses safely?

Saline irrigation is a symptom-care option, but water safety is essential. Use water labeled distilled or sterile, or tap water that has been properly boiled and cooled. Water that is safe to drink is not automatically safe to introduce into the nose, and an ordinary drinking-water filter may not be adequate.[1][3][9]

Do not use unboiled tap water

CDC advises boiling water at a rolling boil for 1 minute, or 3 minutes above 6,500 feet, then cooling it before use. Follow the device and saline-product directions, and do not assume a brief warming step or plain tap water is equivalent to the specified preparation.[9]

Prepare the saline mixture as directed and clean and dry the device according to its instructions. Avoid sharing devices. If you are immunocompromised or unsure whether rinsing is appropriate, ask your clinician first rather than copying another person's routine.[3][9][12]

When to get help after a rinse

Serious infections from contaminated rinse water are rare, but CDC advises urgent care for headache, fever, confusion or vomiting after nasal rinsing. Do not dismiss a new severe illness because irrigation is usually a low-risk symptom measure.[9]

Is a sinus infection contagious?

The cause matters. A respiratory virus that triggers sinus symptoms can spread to other people; sinus inflammation itself is not a single contagious organism. Do not use the label “sinus infection” as proof that you cannot transmit a cold, flu or another respiratory virus.[3][8][12][15]

Returning to normal activities

For a respiratory-virus illness, CDC advises staying home until symptoms have been improving overall and you have been fever-free without fever-reducing medicine for at least 24 hours. After returning to activities, use added precautions for the next 5 days, such as cleaner air, hygiene, a well-fitting mask or distancing around others.[15]

These are community respiratory-virus precautions, not a sinus-antibiotic clearance clock. Starting amoxicillin does not prove that an underlying virus is no longer contagious, and a clinician's or workplace's instructions may differ, especially in healthcare or outbreak settings.[3][11][15]

Practical prevention

Cover coughs, wash hands and avoid sharing personal nasal products or rinse equipment. Consider the people around you who are at greater risk of severe respiratory disease. Feeling somewhat better does not guarantee that transmission risk has reached zero.[9][13][14][15]

What if watchful waiting or an antibiotic is not helping?

A treatment plan needs an exit rule. If an observed uncomplicated illness does not improve within the agreed 3-to-5-day interval, or worsens sooner, contact the clinician for reassessment. The next step may be to reconsider bacterial disease, begin an indicated antibiotic or arrange an examination, rather than continue the same plan indefinitely.[1][2][3][7]

If an appropriate antibiotic has been started, worsening or no improvement after 3 to 5 days also needs reassessment. The clinician should revisit the diagnosis, possible complications, adherence and other contributors before deciding whether to change treatment. Do not automatically double a dose or move to a broader drug on your own.[1][2][3][11]

Medication side effects

Tell the prescriber about diarrhea, vomiting, a new rash or other troubling effects. Severe allergy symptoms such as trouble breathing or swelling require emergency help. Amoxicillin-clavulanate labeling also warns about serious hypersensitivity, liver injury and antibiotic-associated diarrhea; personal history and kidney function affect prescribing.[11]

The combination is contraindicated if it previously caused cholestatic jaundice or liver dysfunction. Make sure that history is not recorded merely as ordinary stomach upset.[11]

If amoxicillin-clavulanate is prescribed, follow the exact formulation and directions. The label recommends taking it at the start of a meal to reduce gastrointestinal intolerance, and some tablet strengths are not interchangeable simply by doubling tablet numbers. A pharmacist can help clarify a confusing prescription.[11]

Make follow-up specific

When you contact the clinician, distinguish “still congested but improving” from “new fever after getting better” or “pain is much worse.” State the drug name, the first dose date, any missed doses and any side effects rather than only saying an antibiotic did not work. A new severe symptom can make a direct examination more important than another prescription. The purpose of follow-up is to check the diagnosis and safety of the plan, not to guarantee an automatic medication change.[1][2][3][11]

Repeated episodes are a different problem

Frequent recurrences, persistent one-sided symptoms or an illness extending into a chronic pattern need a broader evaluation. This guide does not turn recurrent unexplained sinus symptoms into an ongoing same-day-care antibiotic pathway.[1][3][12]

When is a sinus infection urgent, and what can telehealth assess?

Seek urgent direct assessment

Eye swelling or redness, reduced or double vision, a severe or rapidly worsening headache, confusion, other neurological symptoms, neck stiffness or marked systemic illness can indicate a serious complication or another urgent diagnosis. Do not wait until day 10, finish a routine observation interval or rely on a video image to rule out these problems.[1][2][3][8][12]

Significant immune suppression, an unusual severe presentation or persistent symptoms despite treatment lowers the threshold for an examination. A routine remote pathway cannot replace imaging or direct specialist assessment when the clinical situation calls for it.[1][2][3]

A stable adult can start with a structured history

For a new, uncomplicated illness without warning signs, a clinician can review onset, symptom trajectory, severity, medication history and risks. Some adults can be assessed by video, but telehealth does not provide a full eye, neurological, nasal or dental examination. Be prepared to use in-person care if the findings or uncertainty require it.[1][2][3]

Two appropriate next steps

  • If you are stable and improving, use suitable symptom care and the agreed follow-up plan. Observation is reasonable only while the situation remains uncomplicated and you can obtain reassessment.[1][3][7][8]
  • If symptoms persist without improvement, double worsen or are severe, arrange a clinical assessment. If eye or neurological warning signs appear, bypass routine video care and seek urgent direct evaluation.[1][2][3][8][12]

This guide is for adults with short-term sinus symptoms. It does not cover children, invasive infections, chronic specialist management or a standing prescription plan. The separate virtual sinus-care guide discusses the encounter setting; this page provides the broader clinical decision framework.[1][2][3]
