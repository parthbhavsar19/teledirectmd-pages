UTI Symptoms in Women: Early Signs, Look-Alikes, and Next Steps

Recognize the early signs of a urinary tract infection, tell it apart from a yeast infection, bacterial vaginosis, or an STI, and know when to get care.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed August 30, 2026
13 min read

What are the first signs of a UTI in women?

The earliest signs are usually burning or pain with urination (dysuria), a sudden and strong need to urinate (urgency), and urinating more often than usual (frequency). In women with urinary symptoms, painful urination plus frequency without vaginal discharge or irritation raises the probability of a bladder infection to more than 90%, per the Bent 2002 JAMA review. Some women also notice cloudy, bloody, or strong-smelling urine, or pressure or discomfort in the lower abdomen. Itching, a thick white discharge, or a fishy odor points away from a UTI and toward a yeast infection or bacterial vaginosis instead. If you are pregnant, contact your OB or an in-person clinician promptly; UTIs in pregnancy need different evaluation.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated August 30, 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • Painful urination plus frequency, without vaginal discharge or irritation, raises the probability of a bladder infection to more than 90% in women with urinary symptoms.[1]
  • Itching, a thick white discharge, or a fishy odor usually points to a yeast infection or bacterial vaginosis, not a UTI.[1][12][20]
  • New burning with discharge, pelvic pain, or bleeding between periods can be chlamydia or gonorrhea, which need STI testing rather than a routine UTI workup.[11][14]
  • Women get UTIs more often than men because the urethra is short and sits close to the anus.[15][3]
  • First-line antibiotics for uncomplicated cystitis include nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and fosfomycin.[2][4][23]
  • Fever, flank pain, nausea, or vomiting can mean the infection has reached the kidneys and needs urgent care.[17][3]
  • If you are pregnant, contact your OB or an in-person clinician promptly; UTIs in pregnancy need different evaluation.[19][2][6][26]

What Are the First Signs of a UTI in Women?

A urinary tract infection, or UTI, usually starts in the bladder, where it is called cystitis. The most common early signs are dysuria (pain or burning when you urinate), urgency (a sudden, hard-to-ignore need to go), and frequency (needing to urinate more often than usual, often with only small amounts each time).[16][4]

These symptoms are characteristic. In a woman with urinary symptoms, painful urination plus frequency without vaginal discharge or irritation raises the probability of a bladder infection to more than 90%, enough to make the diagnosis from the history alone. That estimate comes from a landmark JAMA review by Bent and colleagues, which looked at nine studies of how well the history and examination diagnose acute uncomplicated UTI in women; the more than 90% figure comes from the one included study that examined combinations of symptoms.[1]

Other signs can appear alongside these. Urine may look cloudy or bloody, or have a strong or foul smell, and some women feel pressure, cramping, or discomfort in the lower abdomen, just above the pubic bone.[3][16][4] On their own, these are weaker clues: in the Bent review, blood in the urine raised the likelihood of a UTI far less than the combination of painful urination and frequency without vaginal symptoms.[1]

What matters most for the next step is what is not there. Vaginal discharge, itching, or irritation makes a simple bladder infection less likely and points toward a vaginal or sexually transmitted cause, which changes both testing and treatment.[1][19]

What Does a UTI Feel Like vs a Yeast Infection?

A UTI and a vaginal yeast infection (vulvovaginal candidiasis) can both cause burning, but they feel different and point to different treatments. A UTI centers on urination: pain or burning while you urinate, urgency, and frequency, usually without vaginal discharge. A yeast infection centers on the vulva and vagina: itching, soreness, burning felt on the outside when urine passes, and a discharge that can be thick, white, and chunky, like cottage cheese.[1][9][20]

The two can be linked. Antibiotics change the normal balance of germs in the vagina, which is one reason a yeast infection can follow a course of antibiotics for a UTI.[20]

Pain or burning with urinationCommon, central symptomMay burn when urine touches irritated skin

Because the treatments are different, it is worth getting the right diagnosis rather than guessing. A yeast infection is treated with an antifungal medicine, such as a single oral dose of fluconazole or a short course of a vaginal antifungal, while a bacterial UTI is treated with an antibiotic.[9][2]

UTI vs Bacterial Vaginosis: How to Tell

Bacterial vaginosis (BV) is an imbalance of the bacteria in the vagina, not an infection of the urinary tract. Its hallmark is a thin white or gray vaginal discharge with a strong fish-like odor, often more noticeable after sex. BV can also cause burning when you pee and vaginal itching, but the CDC's list of BV symptoms does not include the urgency and frequency typical of a bladder infection, and many people with BV have no symptoms at all.[12][13]

The key difference is the discharge. Vaginal discharge makes a UTI less likely, while a thin, milky discharge and a fishy odor are among the criteria clinicians use to diagnose BV. If you have a thin, fishy-smelling discharge, the problem is likely vaginal and should be evaluated as BV rather than treated as a bladder infection.[1][13]

The CDC describes BV as the most common vaginal condition in women ages 15 to 44, which is why a clinician will ask about discharge, odor, and sexual history when urinary symptoms are present.[12]

Can an STI Feel Like a UTI?

Yes. Chlamydia and gonorrhea are well-established causes of urethritis, an infection of the urethra whose symptoms can include dysuria, or burning with urination, much like a UTI. Infection of the cervix is often silent, but some women notice abnormal vaginal discharge or bleeding between periods, and chlamydia can cause pelvic or abdominal pain. These are not typical of a simple bladder infection.[11][14]

Several clues point toward an STI rather than a UTI: a new sex partner, more than one partner, a partner who has an STI, discharge from the urethra or vagina, or pelvic pain. In these situations, testing for chlamydia and gonorrhea is appropriate, and treatment should target the specific organism rather than assuming a routine bladder infection.[11][14]

Because the treatments differ, getting the right test matters. The CDC recommends nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT) to detect chlamydia, and testing for both chlamydia and gonorrhea with NAAT when a woman has signs of a cervical infection.[14][11]

Why Do Women Get UTIs More Often Than Men?

Anatomy is the main reason. In women, the urethra, the tube that carries urine out of the body, is shorter and closer to the anus than in men. Most UTIs start when bacteria, often from the skin or rectum, enter the urethra and travel to the bladder, and Escherichia coli causes most cases.[15][3][22][21]

Sexual activity is a common trigger, because intercourse can introduce bacteria from the genital area into the urethra. In a prospective study of young women, the risk of a UTI rose with recent intercourse, recent use of a diaphragm with spermicide, and a history of recurrent infections. After menopause, hormonal changes and lower estrogen levels shift the vaginal flora and also raise the risk.[21][25][3]

The numbers are striking. About half of all women will have a bladder infection in their lifetime, and about a quarter of those will have repeat infections, while men under age 50 rarely get bladder infections.[15][5]

When Is It Not a UTI (Interstitial Cystitis, Stones, Irritants)?

Several conditions can cause bladder pain, urgency, and frequency without a bacterial infection; the AUA guideline describes a large group of patients with bladder, urethral, or pelvic pain, urinary symptoms, and sterile urine cultures, many with specific identifiable causes. When symptoms linger for weeks, recur without a positive culture, or do not respond to antibiotics, one of these look-alikes may be the real cause.[10]

Interstitial cystitis / bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS) causes bladder pain, pressure, or discomfort, often with urgency and frequency, but urine cultures stay negative and the guideline advises against long-term antibiotics. The American Urological Association guideline defines it as pain, pressure, or discomfort felt to be related to the bladder, with urinary symptoms lasting more than six weeks, in the absence of infection or other identifiable causes, so infection and other causes must be ruled out first.[10]

Kidney stones can cause pain while urinating, blood in the urine, and a constant need to urinate, along with sharp pain in the back, side, lower abdomen, or groin.[18] Chemical irritants, such as bubble bath, perfumes, or lotions, can irritate the vaginal tissue and cause painful urination without any infection. After menopause, changes in the vaginal tissue (atrophic vaginitis) can also cause painful urination.[19]

The practical point is that not every burning or urgent bladder is an infection. A urine culture is recommended when symptoms do not resolve or come back after treatment, and the AUA guideline advises against more antibiotics for someone whose earlier antibiotics did not help and whose urine culture is negative.[4][10]

How Is a UTI Diagnosed in Women?

For a healthy, premenopausal, nonpregnant woman with classic symptoms and no vaginal discharge, the diagnosis can often be made from the history alone.[21][1][24] When the picture is less clear, or when the woman is pregnant, has recurrent infections, or has atypical symptoms, testing adds useful information.[21][24][8]

A urine dipstick checks for nitrite (a byproduct of typical urinary bacteria), leukocyte esterase, and blood. These are helpful but imperfect: a positive nitrite raises the probability of infection, but nitrite testing has relatively low sensitivity, and in a woman with symptoms a negative dipstick does not reliably rule a UTI out.[8][1]

A urine culture is the reference test. It identifies the bacteria and helps choose the best antibiotic, and it is recommended for suspected kidney infection, symptoms that do not resolve or that come back within two to four weeks after treatment, atypical symptoms, recurrent infection, treatment failure, and complicated infections, which include UTIs in pregnancy.[8][3][4][24] The sample should be collected with as little contamination as possible, usually as a clean-catch midstream sample. In a study of premenopausal women with cystitis, midstream cultures accurately showed bladder E. coli but not enterococci or group B streptococci.[8][7]

What Is the Standard Treatment?

Uncomplicated cystitis in a healthy, nonpregnant woman is treated with a short course of an oral antibiotic. Guidelines name nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX, where local resistance does not exceed 20%), and fosfomycin as first-line options.[4][23][24] The 2011 IDSA guideline rates each as an appropriate choice and says the choice of therapy is made on an individual basis, weighing resistance and the ecological side effects of antibiotics, called collateral damage.[2]

AntibioticTypical courseNotes
Nitrofurantoin5 daysFirst-line option; IDSA notes only minor collateral damage
Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole3 daysAvoid if local E. coli resistance exceeds 20%
FosfomycinSingle doseOne-time oral dose

Fluoroquinolones such as ciprofloxacin should be reserved for situations in which other agents are not appropriate, and the IDSA guideline notes their propensity for collateral damage.[23][2] Finish all of the antibiotic as prescribed, even if you feel better.[3]

Bladder infection symptoms most often go away within 24 to 48 hours after treatment begins. If they do not improve within about 48 hours, or if they worsen, contact the prescribing clinician, because the organism may be resistant or the diagnosis may need another look.[3][22][24]

When Should a Woman See a Doctor for UTI Symptoms?

Contact a clinician if you have symptoms of a bladder infection, so you can get the right treatment rather than guessing. Several signs should prompt same-day or urgent care, because they can mean the infection has reached the kidneys or that something more serious is happening.[16][22][17]

Go to the emergency room for visible blood in the urine or any urinary symptoms during pregnancy (Dr. Bhavsar's cautious standard for these guides). Get same-day or urgent care if you have fever or chills, flank or upper-back pain, nausea or vomiting, symptoms that do not improve within 48 hours of treatment, or symptoms that keep coming back. UTIs in pregnancy need different evaluation.[17][3][19][22][26]

Recurrent UTIs, meaning at least two infections in six months or at least three in a year, also deserve a clinician's attention, because a clinician may recommend additional steps to help prevent them.[21][22]

When Should a UTI Send You to the ER?

Go to the emergency room or call 911 for any of these:

  • Fever with shaking chills and pain in your back or side
  • Vomiting that keeps you from holding down fluids or medicine
  • Confusion, a racing heart, fast breathing, or feeling faint
  • Visible blood in your urine
  • Any urinary symptoms while you are pregnant

Get in-person care the same day for fever on its own, back or side pain on its own, urinary symptoms in a man, symptoms that are not improving 48 hours after starting an antibiotic, or symptoms that come back soon after treatment.

This red-flag list is the same on every UTI guide on this site. Sending visible blood in the urine and urinary symptoms in pregnancy to the emergency room is Dr. Bhavsar's deliberately cautious standard for these guides.
