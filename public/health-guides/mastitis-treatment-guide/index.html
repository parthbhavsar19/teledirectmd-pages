Mastitis: Symptoms, Antibiotic Treatment & Breastfeeding Guidance

Types, causes, antibiotic selection, breastfeeding during treatment, abscess management, and the ABM 2022 mastitis spectrum protocol, explained by Parth Bhavsar, MD.

Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed Mar 2026
~14 min read

How is mastitis treated, and should you continue breastfeeding?

Mastitis is a spectrum that runs from ductal narrowing and inflammatory mastitis to bacterial mastitis, phlegmon and abscess, and the ABM Protocol #36 (2022) advises reserving antibiotics for bacterial mastitis. Keep breastfeeding: ABM states that bacterial mastitis does not pose a risk to the infant and does not require an interruption in breastfeeding. When antibiotics are needed, ABM lists first-line dicloxacillin (or flucloxacillin) 500 mg four times daily or cephalexin 500 mg four times daily for 10 to 14 days; Cleveland Clinic clinicians advise antibiotics when systemic symptoms such as fever, body aches or shaking chills (rigors) do not improve within 24 to 48 hours of conservative care. About 3 to 11% of people with acute mastitis develop a breast abscess, which needs drainage. A licensed clinician, through telehealth or an in-person visit, can confirm the diagnosis and recommend next steps.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated July 14, 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • Many cases of mastitis are inflammatory, not bacterial, and the ABM 2022 protocol advises reserving antibiotics for bacterial mastitis rather than using them reflexively.
  • Continue breastfeeding. Bacterial mastitis does not pose a risk to the infant and does not require an interruption in breastfeeding.
  • When antibiotics are needed, first-line treatment is dicloxacillin 500 mg QID or cephalexin 500 mg QID for 10–14 days.
  • A breast abscess develops in about 3–11% of people with acute mastitis and needs drainage; ultrasound helps confirm it.
  • Recurrent mastitis calls for an exam and milk culture, a review of breastfeeding and pumping, and imaging when episodes keep returning to the same spot.

Mastitis is a common and often misunderstood complication of breastfeeding. Many patients, and even some clinicians, still don't know that not every case of mastitis is a bacterial infection. The 2022 Academy of Breastfeeding Medicine (ABM) protocol reframed mastitis as a spectrum of conditions resulting from ductal inflammation and stromal edema, in which inflammatory mastitis can come before bacterial mastitis.[1] That distinction drives treatment decisions in ways that matter.

Treating inflammatory mastitis with antibiotics disrupts the breast microbiome and can actually increase the risk of progression to bacterial mastitis, the exact outcome you're trying to prevent. At the same time, bacterial mastitis can progress to phlegmon or abscess, and mastitis contributes to early weaning.[1] Getting the call right requires understanding which type of mastitis you're dealing with and acting accordingly.

This guide covers the full spectrum: types of mastitis, how to tell them apart, when antibiotics are and aren't appropriate, how to manage a breast abscess, what to do if mastitis keeps coming back, and where telehealth fits into the picture. The information reflects the ABM 2022 clinical protocol and guidance from Cleveland Clinic clinicians.

Understanding the Mastitis Spectrum

Mastitis is not a single entity. The ABM now formally describes it as a spectrum of conditions resulting from ductal inflammation and stromal edema.[1] Understanding where on that spectrum a patient sits determines treatment. Each stage has a distinct presentation, cause, and management approach.

Engorgement

Bilateral breast pain, firmness, and swelling that usually appears between days 3 and 5 postpartum. Engorgement is related to interstitial edema and hyperemia during secretory activation (lactogenesis II), not to infection. ABM recommends hand expression, reverse pressure softening of the areola, lymphatic drainage, and ice for symptom relief, not antibiotics.[1]

Ductal Narrowing ("Plugged Duct")

Focal induration and tenderness without systemic symptoms. This results from alveolar distension and/or early mammary dysbiosis. Repeatedly feeding or pumping to clear a "plug" is counterproductive: it suppresses feedback inhibitor of lactation (FIL), increases milk production, and worsens inflammation and ductal narrowing. Squeezing or aggressively massaging the breast to push a "plug" out is ineffective and causes tissue trauma. Physiologic breastfeeding (feeding on demand without excessive pumping) is the right approach.[1]

Inflammatory Mastitis

A painful, erythematous, edematous area of the breast that can produce systemic symptoms including fever, chills, and tachycardia, even without bacterial infection. This is where the ABM 2022 protocol made one of its most clinically significant updates: a Swedish study cited in the protocol found that most women with inflammatory mastitis had complete resolution of symptoms without antibiotics, and ABM advises reassuring patients that many symptoms resolve with conservative care and psychosocial support.[1]

The Inflammatory vs. Bacterial Distinction

Using antibiotics for inflammatory mastitis disrupts the breast microbiome and increases the risk of progression to bacterial mastitis, the opposite of what you want. The ABM 2022 protocol states that antibiotics should be reserved for bacterial mastitis.[1] This is a significant departure from older clinical practice.

Bacterial Mastitis

When ductal narrowing and inflammatory mastitis progress, bacterial mastitis can develop. It presents as cellulitis (worsening redness and induration) in one region of the breast that may spread to other quadrants. Common organisms include Staphylococcus species (such as S. aureus and S. epidermidis) and Streptococcus species. ABM describes this stage as one that needs antibiotics or probiotics to resolve.[1]

Phlegmon

A firm, mass-like area without fluctuance that develops when mastitis worsens; excessive deep tissue massage may promote it. Ultrasound shows a heterogeneous, ill-defined fluid collection rather than the well-defined collection of an abscess. Phlegmon may need extended antibiotics, decided case by case, and close follow-up because it can coalesce into a drainable abscess.[1]

Breast Abscess

An infected fluid collection that develops in approximately 3 to 11% of women with acute mastitis.[1] An abscess needs drainage for source control. Needle aspiration is often recommended first, though most patients need repeat aspirations, and ABM suggests considering drain placement at the first procedure. Breastfeeding from the affected breast should continue after aspiration or drain placement.[1]

Subacute and Recurrent Mastitis

Subacute mastitis can cause needle-like, burning breast pain, nipple blebs, and recurrent areas of induration, with milder local symptoms and usually without the systemic symptoms of acute mastitis. It reflects chronic mammary dysbiosis, with bacterial biofilms narrowing the ducts. Recurrent mastitis has no agreed definition; patients may describe symptoms every 2 to 4 weeks or less often, linked to hyperlactation, dysbiosis, inadequate treatment of prior mastitis, or an underlying cause that was never addressed.[1]

Causes and Risk Factors

The older model of mastitis attributed it primarily to milk stasis allowing bacteria to proliferate. The ABM 2022 protocol notes that milk stasis has been postulated as an instigating factor, but scientific evidence has not proven causation.[1] Current understanding centers on hyperlactation and mammary dysbiosis (a disruption of the milk microbiome), with ductal inflammation and edema setting the stage.[1]

Physiologic Risk Factors

  • Hyperlactation and oversupply: ABM calls this a primary underlying risk factor. Excess milk production feeds a cycle of ductal congestion, inflammation, and dysbiosis.[1]
  • Excessive pumping: Increased milk removal increases milk production, so "pumping to empty" perpetuates hyperlactation, and regular pump use may also predispose to dysbiosis. If you pump, express only about the volume your infant takes.[1]
  • Aggressive massage: Counter to what many patients are told, deep breast massage causes more inflammation, tissue edema, and microvascular injury and can promote phlegmon. Light sweeping of the skin, similar to lymphatic drainage, is the preferred technique.[1]
  • Nipple shields and improper latch: Anything that alters normal milk removal patterns increases the risk of ductal stasis and dysbiosis.

Structural and Skin Risk Factors

  • Cracked or damaged nipple skin: Nipple trauma is associated with mastitis, but ABM notes that the data are limited by confounding and bias, and that newer microbiome evidence shows mastitis is not caused by retrograde spread of bacteria from visible nipple trauma.[1]
  • Nipple piercing: Scar tissue and altered ductal anatomy from prior piercing disrupt normal milk flow and can harbor biofilm-forming organisms.
  • Engorgement: Severe engorgement that is not managed promptly can progress along the spectrum toward inflammatory and bacterial mastitis.

Microbiome-Related Risk Factors

  • Cesarean birth: Associated with delayed lactogenesis II and listed by ABM among the factors behind mammary dysbiosis.[1]
  • Recent antibiotic use: Antibiotic exposure is one of the factors that disrupts the milk microbiome, creating conditions favorable to dysbiosis.[1]
  • Nipple shield use: Alters milk flow dynamics and microbiome transfer from infant to breast.
  • Exclusive pumping: Removes the infant's oral microbiome contribution to breast flora, contributing to dysbiosis.

What does not cause mastitis, according to current evidence: specific foods, poor hygiene, or yeast (ABM finds no scientific evidence for "candida mastitis"). Routine sterilization of pumps and household items is not necessary; clean pump parts appropriately after each use and wash your hands before expressing milk.[1]

How Mastitis Is Diagnosed

Mastitis is a clinical diagnosis. You don't need a lab test to start treatment for a straightforward presentation, but it matters to know what to look for and when to image.

Classic Presentation

Breast pain, tenderness, warmth, redness, and swelling, often in a segmental (wedge-shaped) area of one breast.[1] Fever, chills, and body aches can accompany inflammatory or bacterial mastitis. One important clinical note: erythema may present less conspicuously on darker skin, so symptom history, not just appearance, drives the assessment.[2]

When to Order a Breast Milk Culture

ABM suggests considering a sterile breast milk culture in these situations:[1]

  • No symptomatic improvement after 48 hours of first-line antibiotics
  • Recurrent mastitis episodes
  • Health care workers in areas with a high prevalence of MRSA
  • Milk being expressed for an immunocompromised infant in the NICU
  • Suspected subacute mastitis

MRSA and resistant coagulase-negative Staphylococci will not respond to dicloxacillin or cephalexin.[1] A culture catches this early and guides a pivot to effective therapy.

When to Use Ultrasound

Ultrasound is considered the most useful initial imaging test when a breast abscess is suspected, and it is also used to monitor response to treatment and confirm resolution.[3] Cleveland Clinic clinicians recommend ultrasound and a prompt (same-day) radiology and breast surgery consult when an abscess, phlegmon, or infected galactocele is suspected. Signs that raise that concern:[2]

  • A palpable fluid-filled lump, or a focal, discrete area of swelling and redness
  • Symptoms ongoing or progressing for more than 24 to 48 hours
  • Progressive induration with worsening pain despite antibiotic therapy
  • Uncertainty about whether a lump is an abscess, phlegmon, or galactocele

Ultrasound confirms the presence of a fluid collection, characterizes whether it is well-defined (abscess) or ill-defined (phlegmon), and guides needle aspiration or catheter drainage. A mammogram is occasionally used for galactocele evaluation but is not the primary tool for acute mastitis.

Treatment: Conservative Care and Antibiotics

The Most Important Rule: Keep Breastfeeding

This bears repeating because it contradicts what many patients are told. Bacterial mastitis is not contagious, does not pose a risk to the infant, and does not require an interruption in breastfeeding; ABM states that it is safe for children to consume milk from a breast with bacterial mastitis.[1] Keep feeding on demand, but don't try to "empty" the breast: overfeeding from the affected breast or pumping to empty perpetuates hyperlactation and worsens edema and inflammation.[1]

If the area behind the nipple is so swollen that no milk comes out with breastfeeding or hand expression, ABM advises pausing feeds from that breast during the acute phase, feeding from the other breast, and returning to the affected side as swelling settles. Hand-expressing small volumes for comfort is fine, and feeding first from the less congested breast avoids overstimulating the inflamed one.[1]

Conservative Management (All Stages)

These measures apply across the entire mastitis spectrum and are the primary treatment for inflammatory mastitis:

  • Ibuprofen 800 mg every 8 hours in the acute setting (the example dose ABM gives) reduces edema and inflammation; acetaminophen 1,000 mg every 8 hours can be used for pain relief[1]
  • Ice on the affected area; ABM notes ice can be applied every hour or more often if desired[1]
  • Gentle lymphatic drainage: light sweeping motions toward the lymph nodes in the armpit and above the collarbone, not deep tissue massage[2]
  • Supportive bra: well fitting but not too tight, to support the breast and limit swelling
  • Feed on demand and do not pump to "empty" beyond what the infant requires
  • Rest and hydration: mastitis with systemic symptoms is physically taxing, and adequate fluid intake supports recovery
  • Sunflower or soy lecithin 5–10 g daily by mouth may help reduce inflammation in the ducts and emulsify milk[1]

Avoid: deep breast massage and vibrating devices, saline soaks, castor oil and other topical products, cabbage leaves (no more effective than ice, and they may carry Listeria), unroofing nipple blebs, and prophylactic antibiotics. Prophylactic antibiotics have not been shown to prevent mastitis and may select antibiotic-resistant strains.[1]

When to Start Antibiotics

Antibiotics are indicated for bacterial mastitis. Signs that point toward bacterial rather than inflammatory mastitis include:[1][2]

  • Systemic symptoms such as fever, chills, body aches, or a fast heart rate that last more than 24 hours
  • Severe local symptoms, or no improvement within 24 to 48 hours of appropriate conservative measures
  • Cellulitis (worsening redness and firmness) in one area of the breast
  • Redness that spreads to other parts of the breast

First-Line Antibiotic Regimens

ABM 2022 lists these empiric regimens for bacterial mastitis, and Cleveland Clinic clinicians give the same doses:[1][2]

Antibiotic Dose Duration Notes
Dicloxacillin 500 mg four times daily 10–14 days First-line (flucloxacillin is an alternative where available). Best absorbed on an empty stomach: take at least 1 hour before or 2 hours after meals, with at least 4 fluid ounces (120 mL) of water, and not lying down or right before bed.[4]
Cephalexin (Keflex) 500 mg four times daily 10–14 days First-line; broader coverage including gram-negative rods, and does not need to be taken separately from meals.
Clindamycin 300 mg four times daily 10–14 days Second-line.
TMP-SMX DS (Bactrim) 160/800 mg twice daily 10–14 days Second-line. Not recommended if the infant has G6PD deficiency; use with caution with a premature infant or an infant with hyperbilirubinemia, especially under 30 days old.

ABM notes that the first-line options have a low relative infant dose.[1] The FDA-approved dicloxacillin label notes that penicillins are excreted in breast milk and advises caution when they are given to a nursing woman; the mastitis dose above follows ABM and is higher than the general adult doses listed on that label (125 to 250 mg every 6 hours).[4] ABM also says women should not be told to express and discard their milk, because bacterial mastitis is not a contraindication to breastfeeding.[1]

If symptoms persist after 48 hours of antibiotic treatment, a breast milk culture from the affected breast is recommended to rule out MRSA and other resistant pathogens.[2] MRSA and resistant coagulase-negative Staphylococci will not respond to dicloxacillin or cephalexin.[1] If an abscess or phlegmon is suspected, ultrasound is recommended.[2]

Breast Abscess: Recognition and Management

A breast abscess forms in roughly 3 to 11% of women with acute mastitis.[1] An abscess is an infected fluid collection that needs drainage for source control, not antibiotics alone.[1]

How to Recognize an Abscess

The hallmark is progressive firmness and redness, often with a palpable fluid collection in a well-defined area of the breast. Fever may settle as the body walls off the infection, settle and then return, or keep worsening until the collection is drained.[1] Ultrasound is the most useful initial imaging test to confirm it.[3]

Drainage Options

Needle aspiration with fluid culture is often recommended as the first-line intervention, but most patients need repeat aspirations, which can be stressful and risk breastfeeding discontinuation. ABM therefore suggests considering drain placement at the first procedure, either in the office or through interventional radiology.[1] A radiology reference takes a different view: Radiopaedia describes antibiotics combined with ultrasound-guided aspiration as the treatment of choice and favors repeated aspiration over a percutaneous catheter because of the risk of cutaneous fistula and discomfort, with surgical incision and drainage needed only in rare cases.[3]

When a drain or skin stent is used, ABM advises draining to gravity rather than suction and strictly avoiding vacuum-assisted wound devices on a lactating breast.[1]

Breastfeeding After Abscess Drainage

After aspiration or drain placement, keep breastfeeding from the affected breast. Milk fistula, a concern patients often raise, occurs in fewer than 2% of cases, provided lactation is managed well and hyperlactation is treated if present. Antibiotics are commonly continued for 10 to 14 days, though a shorter course may be appropriate if the surrounding cellulitis clears quickly. Inflammation can take several weeks to settle, so follow-up exams and imaging should confirm resolution.[1]

Recurrent Mastitis: Breaking the Cycle

Recurrent mastitis has no consensus definition; patients may describe symptoms every 2 to 4 weeks, or less often.[1] It is not simply bad luck. It usually signals an underlying issue that has not been addressed, and the workup is systematic.

Step 1: Sterile Breast Milk Culture

ABM advises examining the patient and obtaining a milk culture to confirm true recurrent mastitis rather than treating empirically. Culture identifies uncommon pathogens and resistant bacteria: coagulase-negative Staphylococci, which are common in breast milk, can act as opportunistic pathogens, and MRSA and resistant coagulase-negative Staphylococci will not respond to dicloxacillin or cephalexin.[1]

Step 2: Lactation Evaluation

A referral to a certified lactation consultant (IBCLC) or breastfeeding medicine specialist is one of the most valuable steps for recurrent mastitis. What to evaluate:

  • Hyperlactation and oversupply: a lactation consultant or breastfeeding medicine specialist can help downregulate supply safely[2]
  • Pumping frequency and technique: the wrong flange size, suction that is too high, or pumping for too long can injure the breast[1]
  • Latch and infant positioning, including feeding patterns that keep one breast overstimulated
  • Nipple shield use: ABM advises avoiding nipple shields, which can lead to inadequate milk extraction[1]

Step 3: Probiotics

ABM 2022 describes the probiotic data as mixed: a systematic review suggested probiotics may help treat and prevent mastitis, but a strong recommendation could not be made. If used, ABM says the product should contain Limosilactobacillus fermentum (formerly Lactobacillus fermentum) or, preferably, Ligilactobacillus salivarius (formerly Lactobacillus salivarius) strains, and it suggests considering daily use to prevent recurrent mastitis.[1] A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of six randomized trials in PLOS ONE found that probiotics taken during pregnancy reduced the incidence of mastitis, and that bacterial counts in milk fell in both healthy participants and those with mastitis; the authors noted that high-quality multicenter trials are still needed.[5]

The key is strain specificity. ABM notes that only selected strains of these species may be effective, so trial results cannot be generalized to a whole species.[1] The trials in the 2022 meta-analysis used strains such as L. salivarius CECT5713 and PS2 and L. fermentum CECT5716.[5] Prophylactic antibiotics are not recommended for recurrent mastitis: they have not been shown to prevent it and may select antibiotic-resistant strains.[1]

Step 4: Imaging and Cancer Screening

Multiple recurrences in the same location call for radiology evaluation to rule out an underlying mass or another abnormality such as granulomatous mastitis.[1] Several conditions can mimic recurrent infectious mastitis:

  • Idiopathic granulomatous mastitis (IGM): a chronic inflammatory condition, not a typical infection, that needs specialist evaluation
  • Inflammatory breast cancer (IBC): makes the breast red and swollen, sometimes with dimpled, orange-peel skin (peau d'orange); symptoms generally come on rapidly, and mastitis has similar symptoms.[6] ABM says any concern for IBC warrants urgent referral to breast surgery and oncology.[1]
  • Structural mass: an underlying benign or malignant lesion may be contributing to localized obstruction
When to Screen for Inflammatory Breast Cancer

Cleveland Clinic clinicians advise referral to a breast specialist to rule out inflammatory breast cancer when symptoms do not respond to antibiotics.[2] IBC causes rapid-onset redness and swelling, sometimes with peau d'orange skin, and can look like mastitis; evaluation can include a mammogram, breast ultrasound or breast MRI, and a breast or skin biopsy.[6] Repeating antibiotic courses without improvement is not a substitute for this workup.

Telehealth in Mastitis Care

Mastitis is well-suited to telehealth evaluation for the right patient. The diagnosis is clinical, based on symptom description and visual assessment, and treatment for uncomplicated cases involves oral medications and behavioral changes that a physician can prescribe and explain over a video visit.

A 2025 study of Maine all-payer claims data (people who gave birth in 2016 to 2022 and had a breast pump claim) found that 4.4% were diagnosed with lactational mastitis within one year postpartum. Overall, 15% of cases were first diagnosed at an acute care encounter, and that share was higher among people living in isolated rural areas (22%) and those with Medicaid (24%).[7] The authors suggest these groups may face barriers to care in the primary care setting.

When Telehealth Is Appropriate for Mastitis

  • Typical presentation: breast pain, warmth, redness, and swelling with or without fever, in a currently breastfeeding patient
  • Inflammatory mastitis without systemic symptoms: conservative management guidance
  • Early bacterial mastitis with systemic symptoms: antibiotic prescription when clinically indicated
  • Engorgement and plugged duct management
  • Breastfeeding guidance, feeding technique, and lactation referral coordination

When In-Person or Emergency Care Is Required

  • Palpable fluctuant mass suggesting breast abscess: requires ultrasound and drainage
  • Signs of sepsis: high fever with rapid heart rate, hypotension, confusion, or inability to keep fluids down
  • No improvement after 48–72 hours of appropriate oral antibiotics (possible abscess or MRSA)
  • Recurrent mastitis requiring milk culture, imaging workup, and hands-on lactation assessment
  • Non-lactational breast inflammation or skin changes concerning for inflammatory breast cancer

TeleDirectMD evaluates uncomplicated lactational mastitis via secure video visit, screens for red flags, and provides antibiotic prescriptions when bacterial mastitis is clinically indicated. Patients who need in-person drainage, imaging, or specialist referral are directed promptly to the appropriate level of care.

Red Flags: When to Seek Emergency Care

Go to the Emergency Department If You Have:
  • Fever above 102°F (38.9°C) combined with rapid heart rate, confusion, or inability to keep fluids down: signs of sepsis
  • A fluctuant breast mass that is worsening or not improving with antibiotics: likely abscess requiring drainage
  • No improvement after 72 hours of appropriate oral antibiotics: requires ultrasound and culture-guided management
  • Rapidly spreading redness across the breast or onto the chest wall
  • Diabetes, immunosuppression, or prior MRSA with worsening symptoms: higher risk of rapid progression
  • Skin changes such as peau d'orange, thickening, or rapid enlargement without clear response to antibiotics

Mastitis-associated sepsis, while uncommon, is a medical emergency. Most patients with uncomplicated mastitis, whether inflammatory or bacterial, recover fully with appropriate treatment in the outpatient setting. The goal is to recognize the rare cases that need urgent escalation and act on them without delay.
