Flu Test: Types, Accuracy, and How to Get Tested

Rapid antigen vs molecular tests, how accurate they are, and what a negative result really means.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: August 25, 2026
10 min read

What is a flu test and how is flu diagnosed?

A flu test detects influenza virus in a nasal or throat swab. The two main types are rapid antigen tests and molecular tests. Rapid antigen tests give results in about 10 to 15 minutes but have only moderate sensitivity, roughly 50% to 70%, so a negative result does not rule out flu. Molecular tests, including RT-PCR and rapid molecular assays, are the gold standard with 90% to 100% sensitivity. The IDSA recommends molecular testing over antigen testing when the result will change care. At-home combined flu and COVID-19 tests are available over the counter but use antigen technology, so they carry the same risk of false negatives.

Key Takeaways

  • Rapid antigen flu tests have only moderate sensitivity (50-70%), so a negative result does not rule out flu.[1]
  • Molecular tests (RT-PCR and rapid molecular) are the gold standard, with 90-100% sensitivity.[2]
  • Antigen tests are most accurate on day 1-2 of symptoms; testing too early or too late increases false negatives.[5]
  • A positive antigen test is usually reliable, with roughly 90-95% specificity.[1]
  • At-home combined flu and COVID tests are available but have the same antigen-test limitations.[2]
  • Antiviral treatment should not be delayed while waiting for test results.[4]

Here is the single most useful thing to know about flu testing: a negative rapid test does not reliably rule out flu. Rapid antigen tests, the kind you get at a pharmacy or urgent care, miss a meaningful share of real flu infections. Understanding that one fact will help you interpret your result correctly, which is the whole point of this guide.

This page covers the different types of flu tests, how accurate each one is, when to test, what it costs, and how to get tested. It is the companion to our Flu Season guide and our Influenza and Common Cold guide, which cover symptoms and treatment.

Types of Flu Tests: Antigen vs Molecular

There are two broad families of flu tests, and they work differently.[2]

Test type What it detects Sensitivity Result time
Rapid antigen (RIDT) Viral proteins 50-70%[1] 10-15 minutes
Rapid molecular Viral genetic material 90-95%[2] 15-30 minutes
RT-PCR (lab) Viral genetic material >95%[2] Hours to a few days

Antigen tests look for pieces of the virus's proteins, which is fast but less sensitive. Molecular tests look for the virus's genetic material, which is more sensitive but takes longer. The difference in sensitivity is the single most important thing to understand about flu testing.

How Accurate Is the Flu Test?

Specificity, the ability to correctly identify people who do not have flu, is high across the board. Rapid antigen tests have roughly 90-95% specificity, so a positive result is usually trustworthy. The weakness of antigen tests is not false positives; it is false negatives.[1]

Why a Negative Rapid Test Doesn't Rule Out Flu

This is the point worth internalizing. Because rapid antigen tests miss 30-50% of real flu cases, a negative result is common even when you actually have the flu. The CDC is explicit that false negatives are especially common during peak flu activity, when the chance that your symptoms are flu is highest.[1]

Timing also matters. Antigen tests are most accurate on day 1-2 of symptoms, when the virus is shedding at its peak. Testing in the first few hours, before viral load has built up, or several days in, after it has started falling, both increase the chance of a false negative.[5]

Influenza B is also harder for antigen tests to detect than influenza A, so a negative rapid test is less reassuring when influenza B is circulating.[1]

At-Home Flu Tests

Over-the-counter at-home flu tests are now widely available, and most of them combine flu A, flu B, and COVID-19 in a single swab. They are convenient, but they use antigen technology, which means they carry the same moderate sensitivity and the same false-negative risk as a clinic-based rapid antigen test.[2]

An at-home test is most useful when a quick answer helps you decide whether to isolate or seek care. If the result is negative but your symptoms strongly suggest flu, or you are at high risk of complications, a molecular test or a clinical evaluation is the more reliable path.

Flu and COVID Combined Tests

Because flu and COVID-19 cause nearly identical symptoms, a combined test that checks for both in one swab is often the most practical choice during respiratory season. Many at-home kits and clinic tests now test for flu A, flu B, and COVID-19 at once.[2]

The trade-off is the same: combined antigen tests are convenient but less sensitive than molecular tests. If the answer will change your treatment, such as whether to start an antiviral, a molecular test is more reliable.

Where to Get a Flu Test

Flu tests are available in several settings, each with different trade-offs in speed, cost, and accuracy:

Setting Typical test Notes
Pharmacy Rapid antigen, some rapid molecular Fast and convenient; antigen tests have the false-negative caveat.
Urgent care Rapid antigen or rapid molecular Can also evaluate you clinically and prescribe if needed.
Doctor's office Rapid molecular or RT-PCR More likely to use a molecular test when accuracy matters.
Telehealth Clinical diagnosis, can order a test A video visit can diagnose flu and prescribe antivirals without an in-person swab.
At home Combined antigen test Convenient, but antigen sensitivity applies.

Flu Test Cost

Cost varies widely by setting and insurance. At-home combined flu and COVID tests typically run about $20 to $40 for a kit. Clinic and urgent-care tests are often covered by insurance; without insurance, a rapid test may cost roughly $50 to $100 or more depending on the facility and whether a clinician visit is billed separately.

If cost is a concern, a telehealth visit can be a lower-cost way to get a clinical diagnosis and, when appropriate, a prescription, without the separate charge for an in-person swab.

When Should You Get a Flu Test?

Not everyone with flu symptoms needs a test. Testing is most useful when the result will change what you do:[4][6]

Situation Should you test?
High risk of complications (65+, pregnant, chronic condition) Yes, a confirmed diagnosis can prompt earlier antiviral treatment.
Need to distinguish flu from COVID-19 Yes, a combined test clarifies treatment and isolation.
Live with someone at high risk Worth testing to help protect them.
Mild illness, otherwise healthy adult Optional; resting at home is reasonable.

For a mild illness in an otherwise healthy adult, a test is often optional, and resting at home is reasonable. But do not let waiting for a test result delay treatment if you are high risk and within the 48-hour antiviral window.[4]

When to See a Doctor

Most flu can be managed at home, but seek care if you experience any of the following:[3]

  • Difficulty breathing or shortness of breath
  • Persistent chest pain or pressure
  • Confusion or difficulty staying awake
  • Severe or persistent vomiting
  • Symptoms that improve, then return with fever and a worse cough

People at high risk of complications should contact a clinician early in the illness, because antivirals work best within the first 48 hours.[4]
