Flu Test: Types, Accuracy, and How to Get Tested

Rapid antigen vs molecular tests, how accurate they are, and what a negative result really means.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: August 25, 2026
10 min read

What is a flu test and how is flu diagnosed?

A flu test detects influenza virus in a nasal or throat swab. The two main types are rapid antigen tests and molecular tests. Rapid antigen tests give results in about 10 to 15 minutes but have only low to moderate sensitivity, roughly 50% to 70%, so a negative result does not rule out flu. Rapid molecular assays have 90% to 95% sensitivity, and laboratory RT-PCR has very high sensitivity and specificity. For outpatients, the IDSA recommends rapid molecular assays over rapid antigen tests. FDA-authorized at-home tests that check for both flu and COVID-19 are available. Most are antigen tests, so a negative result can be wrong.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated August 25, 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • Rapid antigen flu tests have only moderate sensitivity (50-70%), so a negative result does not rule out flu.[1]
  • Molecular tests are far more sensitive: about 90-95% for rapid molecular assays, and very high for laboratory RT-PCR.[2]
  • Rapid tests are most accurate on samples collected within 3-4 days of symptom onset, and one study found more false negatives when testing in the first hours of illness.[1][5]
  • A positive antigen test is usually reliable, with roughly 95-99% specificity.[1]
  • FDA-authorized at-home flu and COVID tests are available; most are antigen tests, which can give false negatives.[9][10]
  • Antiviral treatment should not be delayed while waiting for test results.[4]

Here is the single most useful thing to know about flu testing: a negative rapid test does not reliably rule out flu. Rapid antigen tests, the kind you get at a pharmacy or urgent care, miss a meaningful share of real flu infections. Understanding that one fact will help you interpret your result correctly, which is the whole point of this guide.

This page covers the different types of flu tests, how accurate each one is, when to test, what it costs, and how to get tested. It is the companion to our Flu Season guide and our Influenza and Common Cold guide, which cover symptoms and treatment.

Types of Flu Tests: Antigen vs Molecular

There are two broad families of flu tests, and they work differently.[2]

Test type What it detects Sensitivity Result time
Rapid antigen (RIDT) Viral proteins 50-70%[1] 10-15 minutes
Rapid molecular Viral genetic material 90-95%[2] 15-30 minutes
RT-PCR (lab) Viral genetic material Very high[2] About 45 minutes to several hours

Antigen tests look for pieces of the virus's proteins, which is fast but less sensitive. Molecular tests look for the virus's genetic material, which is more sensitive but takes longer. The difference in sensitivity is the single most important thing to understand about flu testing.

How Accurate Is the Flu Test?

The accuracy of a flu test depends almost entirely on which type you get. The chart below shows the sensitivity of each type, which is the test's ability to correctly detect flu when it is present:[1][2]

CDC gives no single sensitivity figure for laboratory RT-PCR; it describes RT-PCR and other molecular assays as having very high sensitivity and specificity.[2] CDC also notes that FDA now requires all rapid antigen tests to achieve 80% or higher sensitivity compared with RT-PCR.[1]

Specificity, the ability to correctly identify people who do not have flu, is high across the board. Rapid antigen tests have roughly 95-99% specificity, so false-positive results are uncommon and a positive result is usually trustworthy. The weakness of antigen tests is not false positives; it is false negatives.[1]

Why a Negative Rapid Test Doesn't Rule Out Flu

This is the point worth internalizing. Because rapid antigen tests detect only about 50-70% of real flu cases, a negative result is common even when you actually have the flu. CDC says false negatives are more likely when flu is widespread in the community, which is typically at the peak of flu season.[1]

Timing also matters. CDC advises collecting the sample as early in the illness as possible, within 3-4 days of symptom onset, and says rapid tests are most accurate on samples collected in that window.[1] Testing very early can also miss flu: in a single-clinic study of 82 outpatients in Tokyo, people with false-negative rapid tests had been tested sooner after their symptoms began, and the authors advised not using the test until 12 hours after symptom onset during flu season.[5]

The evidence on influenza B is mixed. A 2012 meta-analysis of 159 studies found lower rapid-test sensitivity for influenza B (about 52%) than for influenza A (about 65%).[7] A 2017 meta-analysis found similar sensitivity for both types with traditional rapid antigen tests (about 54% for influenza A and 53% for influenza B).[8] Either way, a negative rapid antigen test is not reliable for ruling out either type.

At-Home Flu Tests

FDA has authorized several at-home tests that check for flu A, flu B, and COVID-19 from a single nasal swab. Most of the combined tests on FDA's list of authorized at-home tests are antigen tests, and a few are molecular tests.[10] In October 2024, FDA granted the first marketing authorization outside its emergency use authorities for a home flu and COVID-19 test, an antigen test with results in about 15 minutes. In the data FDA reviewed, it correctly identified 92.5% of positive flu A samples and 90.5% of positive flu B samples. FDA warns that rapid antigen tests carry a risk of false negatives, and that people who test negative but still have symptoms should seek follow-up care with a health care provider.[9] CDC does not have specific guidance on using at-home flu tests.[6]

An at-home test is most useful when a quick answer helps you decide whether to isolate or seek care. If the result is negative but your symptoms strongly suggest flu, or you are at high risk of complications, a molecular test or a clinical evaluation is the more reliable path.

Flu and COVID Combined Tests

Because flu and COVID-19 can cause very similar symptoms, a combined test that checks for both in one swab is often the most practical choice during respiratory season. Some home tests detect influenza A and B viruses and the virus that causes COVID-19 at once.[6][10]

The trade-off is the same: combined antigen tests are convenient but less sensitive than molecular tests. If the answer will change your treatment, such as whether to start an antiviral, a molecular test is more reliable.

Where to Get a Flu Test

Flu tests are available in several settings, each with different trade-offs in speed, cost, and accuracy:

Setting Typical test Notes
Pharmacy Rapid antigen, some rapid molecular Fast and convenient; antigen tests have the false-negative caveat.
Urgent care Rapid antigen or rapid molecular Can also evaluate you clinically and prescribe if needed.
Doctor's office Rapid molecular or RT-PCR More likely to use a molecular test when accuracy matters.
Telehealth Clinical diagnosis, can order a test A video visit can diagnose flu and prescribe antivirals without an in-person swab.
At home Combined antigen test Convenient, but antigen sensitivity applies.

If you need a fast answer to decide whether to isolate or start treatment, a rapid antigen or rapid molecular test makes sense. If accuracy matters more than speed, a laboratory RT-PCR or other molecular assay offers very high sensitivity and specificity.[2]

Flu Test Cost

Cost varies widely by setting, test type, and insurance coverage, and prices change often, so check the price with the retailer, pharmacy, or clinic before you test. Clinic and urgent-care tests may be covered by insurance, and some settings bill the clinician visit separately from the test.

A test is not always required for a diagnosis: a clinician may diagnose flu from your symptoms and clinical judgment without testing.[6]

When Should You Get a Flu Test?

Not everyone with flu symptoms needs a test. Testing is most useful when the result will change what you do:[4][6]

Situation Should you test?
High risk of complications (65+, pregnant, chronic condition) Yes, when the result will affect treatment, but antiviral treatment should not wait for the result.
Need to distinguish flu from COVID-19 Yes, a combined test clarifies treatment and isolation.
Live with someone at high risk Worth testing, because the result can affect treatment decisions that help protect them.
Mild illness, otherwise healthy adult Optional; resting at home is reasonable.

For a mild illness in an otherwise healthy adult, a test is often optional, and resting at home is reasonable. But if you are at high risk of complications, do not let waiting for a test result delay antiviral treatment; the greatest benefit has been seen when treatment starts within 2 days of illness onset.[4]

When to See a Doctor

CDC lists these emergency warning signs of flu in adults. Anyone with them should get medical care right away:[3]

  • Difficulty breathing or shortness of breath
  • Persistent pain or pressure in the chest or abdomen
  • Persistent dizziness, confusion, or inability to arouse
  • Seizures
  • Not urinating
  • Severe muscle pain
  • Severe weakness or unsteadiness
  • Fever or cough that improve but then return or worsen
  • Worsening of chronic medical conditions

The list is not all-inclusive; any severe or concerning symptom warrants care.[3]

CDC recommends that people at higher risk of serious flu complications seek medical care promptly for flu-like symptoms to find out whether they need antiviral medicine.[6] Antivirals help most when started early, within 2 days of illness onset.[4]
