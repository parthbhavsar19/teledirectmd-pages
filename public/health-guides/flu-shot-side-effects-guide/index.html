Flu Shot Side Effects: What's Normal and What's Not

What to expect after the shot, how long side effects last, and when to call a doctor.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: August 25, 2026
9 min read

What are the most common flu shot side effects?

The most common flu shot side effects are soreness, redness, or swelling at the injection site, along with headache, low-grade fever, muscle aches, and fatigue, according to the CDC. These are generally mild and resolve on their own within a few days, and many people report no side effects at all. The flu shot cannot cause the flu, because it is made with inactivated (killed) virus or a single viral protein. Serious side effects such as severe allergic reactions and Guillain-Barré syndrome are very rare, estimated at fewer than 1 to 2 cases of GBS per million people vaccinated.

Every fall, tens of millions of Americans get a flu shot, and a meaningful number of them feel something afterward: a sore arm, a little fatigue, maybe a low fever. Most of that is completely normal, and none of it is the flu. The CDC recommends the vaccine for everyone 6 months and older each season.[9] This guide explains what to expect after the shot, why it happens, how long it lasts, and the difference between a normal reaction and one that needs medical attention.

It is the companion to our Flu Season guide, which covers when to get vaccinated and how well the shot works. Here we focus on the side effects themselves.

Common Side Effects: What's Normal

The most common flu shot side effects are local reactions at the injection site, plus a handful of mild whole-body symptoms:[1]

Side effect How common How long it lasts
Soreness, redness, or swelling at the injection site Very common Usually 1 to 2 days[1]
Headache Common Resolves within a day or two[6]
Muscle aches Common Resolves within a day or two[6]
Fatigue Common Resolves within a day or two[6]
Low-grade fever Occasional Short-lived, usually under 24 hours[6]
Nausea Occasional Short-lived[6]

Many people report no side effects at all. When they do occur, they are a sign that your immune system is responding to the vaccine, not that you are getting sick.[1]

Can the Flu Shot Make You Sick?

No. This is the most persistent myth about the flu shot, and it is worth stating plainly: the flu shot cannot give you the flu. Injectable flu vaccines are made with inactivated (killed) virus or a single viral protein, neither of which can cause infection. The nasal spray vaccine contains weakened live virus that cannot cause flu illness in healthy people.[5][8]

So why do some people feel under the weather after the shot? The mild fever, aches, and fatigue are your immune system building protection, and they are far milder than actual flu. It is also easy to catch an unrelated cold or other virus around the same time you get vaccinated, which can make it feel like the shot made you sick when it did not.[5]

Sore Arm and Injection Site Reactions

A sore arm is by far the most common side effect, and it is nothing to worry about. The muscle reacts to the injection itself, causing localized soreness, redness, and sometimes mild swelling at the site.[1] It usually appears within hours and fades over one to two days.

A cool compress and gently moving the arm help. You can also use the arm normally; keeping it still tends to make stiffness worse, not better.[6]

Why Side Effects Happen

Side effects are a sign that the immune system is doing its job. When the vaccine introduces an inactivated virus or viral protein, your body recognizes it as foreign and mounts a response, producing antibodies that will protect you if you encounter the real virus later. The soreness, mild fever, and fatigue are byproducts of that immune activation, not an infection.[1]

This is also why side effects tend to be mild and brief: the immune response is short-lived, and it resolves once protection is established, typically within a few days.

Rare and Serious Side Effects

Serious side effects from the flu shot are rare. The table below contrasts what is normal with what warrants medical attention:[7]

Reaction Normal (no action needed) Seek medical care
Injection site Soreness, redness, mild swelling for 1 to 2 days Severe pain, spreading redness, or swelling that worsens after 2 days
Fever Low-grade, under 24 hours High fever or fever lasting more than a couple of days
Allergic reaction Not applicable Hives, facial or throat swelling, difficulty breathing, fast heartbeat, dizziness
Neurologic Not applicable Weakness or tingling in the limbs (possible Guillain-Barré syndrome)

Guillain-Barré Syndrome: What the Evidence Shows

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is a rare neurological condition in which the immune system attacks the nerves, causing weakness and sometimes paralysis. It is one of the things people worry about most when considering the flu shot, so it is worth looking at the actual numbers.[2]

GBS occurs in about 1 to 2 out of every 100,000 people in the United States each year, and most cases are not related to vaccination. Some studies have found a possible small association between the injectable flu vaccine and GBS, estimating fewer than 1 to 2 cases per million people vaccinated, while other studies have found no association at all.[2]

Here is the context that matters: the risk of GBS after influenza infection itself is higher than the risk after vaccination. Getting the flu is a far more common trigger for GBS than getting the flu shot.[3]

Allergic Reactions and Egg Allergy

Severe allergic reactions to the flu shot, including anaphylaxis, are very rare and usually occur within minutes to hours of vaccination. Signs include hives, swelling of the face or throat, difficulty breathing, a fast heartbeat, and dizziness. These require immediate emergency care.[7]

If you have an egg allergy, the guidance has changed in your favor. Since the 2023-2024 season, the CDC recommends that people with egg allergy may receive any flu vaccine appropriate for their age and health, without additional precautions beyond those recommended for anyone else. Studies show egg-allergic people are not at increased risk of severe reactions to egg-based vaccines.[4][10]

The one true contraindication is a history of a severe, life-threatening allergic reaction to a previous dose of flu vaccine or to a vaccine component. If that applies to you, discuss alternatives with your clinician.[7]

How to Manage Side Effects

Most side effects need no treatment and pass on their own. If they are bothersome, a few simple steps help:[6]

  • Cool compress on the injection site for soreness or swelling.
  • Move the arm normally rather than keeping it still.
  • Rest and fluids if you feel tired or achy.
  • Over-the-counter pain relievers like acetaminophen or ibuprofen can be used for discomfort, though routine use to prevent side effects is not recommended.

To be clear about the difference, here is how a normal vaccine reaction compares to actual flu:

Feature Flu shot reaction Actual flu
Onset Within hours to 2 days of the shot Sudden, often within hours
Fever Low-grade, if any High, often 101-104°F
Body aches Mild Severe
Duration 1 to 3 days 5 to 7 days or longer
Respiratory symptoms None Cough, sore throat, congestion

When to See a Doctor

Most flu shot side effects resolve on their own. Seek care if you experience any of the following:[1]

  • Signs of a severe allergic reaction: hives, swelling of the face or throat, difficulty breathing, fast heartbeat, or dizziness
  • A high fever or a fever that lasts more than a couple of days
  • Severe or worsening pain, redness, or swelling at the injection site
  • Weakness or tingling in the limbs
  • Symptoms that get worse instead of better after a few days

Severe allergic reactions are a medical emergency. If you have trouble breathing or swelling of the face or throat, call 911.
