Perioral Dermatitis

A physician's guide to the rash around your mouth: steroid connection, zero therapy, and the treatments that actually clear it.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: September 8, 2026
12 min read

What is the best treatment for perioral dermatitis?

The first and most important step is stopping all facial corticosteroids and products, often called zero therapy, because steroid use is the most common association. The rash may flare as a steroid cream is withdrawn, so tapering matters. For more severe or stubborn cases, oral tetracycline is the best-supported first-line medicine, and topical options such as metronidazole, erythromycin, or pimecrolimus are used for moderate disease, although the overall evidence is low or very low certainty. Treatment usually runs 4 to 8 weeks, and recurrence can occur if treatment is too short. A clinician can confirm the diagnosis and distinguish it from rosacea or acne.

Key Takeaways

  • Perioral dermatitis is a common facial rash of small red bumps around the mouth that often spares a thin rim of skin just next to the lips.[3]
  • The most common association is steroid use by any route, including skin creams, inhalers, and nasal sprays; cosmetics and heavy facial products are other triggers.[3]
  • It mostly affects women aged about 20 to 45, though it also occurs in children and in men.[3]
  • The first treatment is stopping steroids and all facial products, called zero therapy, and the rash may flare as the steroid is withdrawn.[3]
  • Oral tetracycline has the best supporting evidence and is first line for more severe disease; topical metronidazole, erythromycin, or pimecrolimus are used for moderate disease, and the overall evidence for every option is low or very low certainty.[5][7][2]
  • Treatment usually runs 4 to 8 weeks, sometimes longer, and recurrence can occur if treatment is too short.[3]
  • Going back onto a topical steroid can improve the rash briefly, but there is a risk of a rebound flare when it is stopped again.[8]

Perioral dermatitis is a paradox baked into a skin condition: it often looks like the kind of rash someone would reach for a steroid cream to fix, yet the steroid cream is frequently what caused it in the first place. The name describes it, a rash around the mouth, perioral, that leaves a telltale clear rim of skin just next to the lips. It is harmless but stubborn, and the sequence that leads to it is usually more predictable than patients expect.

This guide explains what perioral dermatitis is, why the steroid connection matters more than any other single fact, and the evidence behind each treatment. Clinical claims are cited to the numbered sources listed at the end.

What Is Perioral Dermatitis?

Perioral dermatitis is a chronic inflammatory rash made up of small red bumps, sometimes with tiny pus-filled heads, that clusters around the mouth and nose.[1][3] Its defining feature is the zone it avoids: a thin rim of skin right next to the lips, called the vermilion border, usually stays clear even when the surrounding skin is involved.[3] In more severe cases it can extend to the chin, cheeks, eyelids, and forehead.[3] Because it can also appear around the nostrils and eyes, it is also called periorificial dermatitis.[3]

The skin often feels dry, tight, and sensitive, and can burn or itch. It looks acne-like, which is part of why it is so often mistaken for acne, but it is a distinct condition with a different cause and a different treatment.

What Causes It? The Steroid Connection

The single most important trigger is topical corticosteroids, the creams and ointments often used for rashes, eczema, or itching on the face.[3] The pattern is a familiar one: a person uses a steroid cream for a minor facial irritation, the skin briefly improves, and when the steroid is stopped the rash comes back worse than before. Each cycle draws the person back to the steroid, and the condition deepens. Steroids from any route have been linked to it, including asthma inhalers and allergy nasal sprays.[3]

Other reported triggers include cosmetics, toothpaste and dental fillings, and thick paraffin-based or petroleum-based moisturizers.[3] Hormonal changes linked to birth control pills, pregnancy, and premenstrual flares have also been observed.[3] One proposed common thread is disruption of the skin barrier, which is why treatment starts with removing products rather than adding them.

Who Gets Perioral Dermatitis?

Perioral dermatitis is most common in women aged about 20 to 45 and is slightly more common in people with lighter skin, though it also occurs in men and children.[3] In children it is often seen between 7 months and 13 years of age,[3] and a granulomatous variant is more common in childhood.[7] It is not contagious[4] and has no effect on overall health,[3] but it can have a significant effect on quality of life.[1]

What Does It Look Like?

The typical picture is clusters of small, red or skin-colored bumps around the mouth and in the folds next to the nose, sitting on a red, slightly scaly background.[3] Each bump is small, usually under 2 millimeters, and some have a tiny white head. The key signature is that narrow clear band right against the lip edge, which is what separates perioral dermatitis from a rash that simply spreads up to the lips.[3]

The skin often feels dry, tight, and sensitive, sometimes burning or itchy. When this rash appears after topical steroid use, the steroids may have also caused their own side effects, thinning and redness that obscure the picture, which is another reason a clinician's eye matters.

Perioral Dermatitis vs Rosacea vs Acne

These three are commonly confused, and the distinction changes treatment. Perioral dermatitis usually spares the lip border, lacks the blackheads and whiteheads of true acne, and, unlike rosacea, does not usually cause flushing or broken blood vessels.

The First Step: Zero Therapy

Before any prescription, the treatment is subtraction. Stop all topical corticosteroids, stop all facial cosmetics and topical products, and wash the face with warm water alone until the rash clears, using at most a bland, preservative-free moisturizer for dryness.[3] This is called zero therapy, and in mild cases it may be enough on its own.[3][5] This applies to steroids put on the skin. Do not stop a prescribed asthma inhaler, steroid nasal spray, or oral steroid on your own: medically necessary nasal, inhaled, and oral steroids should be continued, even though they can slow recovery.[3]

The hard part is topical steroid withdrawal. When a topical steroid is stopped, the rash may flare before it improves,[4] which is a common reason people go back to the steroid and restart the cycle. The way through is to taper slowly over weeks: switch to a lower-potency steroid before stopping, ideally substituting a steroid-sparing cream such as pimecrolimus.[3] However you get there, do not restart the steroid once it is stopped.[3]

Topical Treatments: Metronidazole and Azelaic Acid

Once the steroids are stopped, topical treatment can help settle the remaining bumps. No medicine is specifically approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for perioral dermatitis,[1] and the trial evidence for the available options is low or very low certainty.[2] Topical metronidazole 0.75 to 1 percent, erythromycin 1 percent, and pimecrolimus 1 percent are the commonly listed options.[3] The evidence for metronidazole is relatively weak, and one trial found it inferior to oral tetracycline.[5] Azelaic acid is also used,[6] but in a systematic review of trials, azelaic acid gel may have made no difference to severity after 6 weeks.[2] None of these is a corticosteroid. Pimecrolimus, a steroid-sparing calcineurin inhibitor, can reduce severity quickly, particularly in people who had been using topical steroids;[5] tacrolimus, a related cream, has more limited evidence.[3]

Oral Antibiotics: Tetracyclines

For more severe or stubborn perioral dermatitis, oral antibiotics are the next step. Tetracycline is the recommended first-line choice, and the response is usually quick.[3] Many trials support oral tetracycline as first line because it shortens the time for the bumps to clear,[5] although a systematic review rated this evidence as low certainty.[2] In more severe cases, the best validated choice is oral tetracycline at a subantimicrobial dose, below the level used to kill bacteria, continued until the rash fully clears.[7] Doxycycline and minocycline, from the same family, may also help in more resistant cases.[6] Treatment usually lasts 4 to 8 weeks but may need to be extended.[3]

For pregnant women and children under 8, erythromycin is the first-line oral choice instead.[3] For very resistant cases, oral isotretinoin can be considered, though the evidence is low level.[3][7]

How Long Does It Take to Clear?

With the right approach, treatment usually runs 4 to 8 weeks, though some people need longer,[3] and the rash may take a few weeks or a few months to clear completely.[4] Recurrence can occur if treatment is too short.[3] The flare that can come with steroid withdrawal is expected and temporary, not a sign that the approach is wrong.

Untreated, perioral dermatitis can linger for months to years. If it comes back, the same treatment can be used again.[3]

What to Avoid

Do not do these

Do not restart topical steroids, even if the rash initially improves, since this restarts the cycle.[3] Do not pile on heavy, occlusive moisturizers or thick ointments, which can worsen the rash.[3] And do not expect a quick fix from a single product while the underlying steroid exposure is still happening.

When to See a Doctor

Perioral dermatitis is not dangerous, but a few signs should prompt a visit rather than self-treatment. See a clinician if the rash spreads to the eyes, if you have already been using a topical steroid and the rash seems to be worsening on each cycle, or if the diagnosis is uncertain and the spots look more like true acne, rosacea, or an infection. A clinician can confirm the diagnosis and supervise the steroid taper, which is the step most people find hardest to do correctly.
