Trichomoniasis

A physician's guide to symptoms, testing, metronidazole and tinidazole treatment, and why treating partners matters.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: September 8, 2026
12 min read

What is the best treatment for trichomoniasis?

Trichomoniasis is treated with a nitroimidazole antibiotic: the CDC recommends metronidazole 500 mg twice daily for 7 days for women, a single 2 g dose of metronidazole for men, or tinidazole 2 g as a single dose as an alternative for either. Treating all sex partners at the same time is vital, since most repeat infections likely come from reinfection. People should abstain from sex until everyone is treated and any symptoms resolve. Sexually active women are retested about 3 months later. A licensed clinician can confirm the diagnosis with a highly sensitive test and prescribe the right regimen.

Trichomoniasis, often shortened to trich, is the sexually transmitted infection most people have heard of least. It is the most common nonviral STI on Earth, yet because most people who carry it have no symptoms, it passes quietly between partners and is often picked up only because someone was tested, not because someone felt sick. The good news is that it is curable with a short course of a well-established antibiotic. The catch is that partners need treatment at the same time to avoid passing it back and forth.

This guide covers what trichomoniasis is, how it is detected, the exact treatment regimens, and why partner treatment is not an optional add-on. Every clinical claim is cited to a guideline, drug label, study or CDC page.

What Is Trichomoniasis?

Trichomoniasis is a sexually transmitted infection caused by a single-celled (protozoan) parasite called Trichomonas vaginalis.[2] It is estimated to be the most prevalent nonviral STI worldwide and affects about 2.6 million people in the United States.[1] In women it is most commonly found in the lower genital tract (vulva, vagina, cervix or urethra); in men it is most commonly found inside the penis, in the urethra.[2]

Symptoms: Often Silent

The defining fact about trichomoniasis is that most people with it have few or no symptoms, roughly 70 to 85 percent.[1] When symptoms do appear in women, the vaginal discharge can be diffuse, malodorous or yellow-green, with or without vulvar irritation.[1] CDC's patient page also lists itching, burning, redness or soreness of the genitals, discomfort when peeing, and a clear, white, yellowish or greenish discharge with a fishy smell.[2] Men sometimes have symptoms of urethritis, epididymitis or prostatitis,[1] and may notice itching or irritation inside the penis, burning after peeing or ejaculating, or discharge from the penis.[2]

How It Spreads

Trichomoniasis passes between partners during penile-vaginal sex, and among women who have sex with women through infected vaginal fluids or shared objects (fomites).[1] It is not common for the parasite to infect other body parts, like the hands, mouth or anus, and people with trichomoniasis can pass the infection to others even if they have no symptoms.[2] Untreated infections might last from months to years, and when an infection comes back, CDC says most recurrences likely result from reinfection, such as from an untreated partner.[1]

How It Is Diagnosed

Testing has improved substantially. Nucleic acid amplification tests, or NAATs, detect the parasite's genetic material and are highly sensitive, detecting more infections than microscopy; the FDA-cleared NAATs CDC describes have reported sensitivities of about 95 to 100 percent.[1] The older method, examining a sample under a microscope (a wet mount), is inexpensive and quick but has low sensitivity, 44 to 68 percent compared with culture, and CDC advises following a negative wet mount with a more sensitive test when possible.[1] Trichomoniasis cannot be diagnosed from symptoms alone; a laboratory test confirms it.[2] For an accurate answer, NAAT is the test to ask for.

Trichomoniasis vs BV vs Yeast

Vaginal discharge has several causes, and the distinction changes treatment. Trichomoniasis, bacterial vaginosis and a yeast infection are common causes, and they are easy to confuse without testing. None of the yeast symptoms below is specific to yeast,[4] and trichomoniasis cannot be diagnosed on symptoms alone.[2]

Treatment: Metronidazole and Tinidazole

Trichomoniasis is treated with a class of antibiotics called nitroimidazoles, the only class of medications with clinically demonstrated efficacy against it.[1] The specific regimen depends on sex and circumstances.

PersonRecommended regimenCure rate
WomenMetronidazole 500 mg by mouth, twice daily for 7 daysabout 84 to 98% for recommended metronidazole regimens in trials[1]
MenMetronidazole 2 g by mouth, single doseabout 84 to 98% for recommended metronidazole regimens in trials[1]
Either (alternative)Tinidazole 2 g by mouth, single doseabout 92 to 100%[1]
Women with HIVMetronidazole 500 mg by mouth, twice daily for 7 daysA single 2 g dose was less effective in a trial[1]

The 7-day course of metronidazole is now recommended for women over the old single-dose approach, because multidose treatment is more effective, particularly for women who are symptomatic or have had trichomoniasis before.[1] In a randomized trial of 623 women without HIV, 11 percent of the 7-day group still tested positive about 4 weeks after treatment, compared with 19 percent of the single-dose group.[5] Secnidazole, taken as a single 2 g packet of oral granules, is also FDA-approved for trichomoniasis[7] and a review found it efficacious and well tolerated,[6] although CDC's 2021 trichomoniasis regimens list only metronidazole and tinidazole.[1] Topical metronidazole gel is not recommended because it does not reach therapeutic levels in the urethra and perivaginal glands and is less effective than oral metronidazole.[1]

Treating Partners Is Not Optional

This is the step that determines whether treatment actually sticks. Concurrent treatment of all sex partners is vital for preventing reinfection: current partners should be referred for treatment, and sex should wait until everyone has completed treatment and any symptoms have resolved.[1] Reinfection occurs in about 1 in 5 people within 3 months after treatment.[2] Testing for other STIs, including HIV, syphilis, gonorrhea and chlamydia, should happen at the same time.[1]

What If It Comes Back?

Most repeat infections likely result from reinfection, but true treatment failure also happens: metronidazole resistance occurs in 4 to 10 percent of cases of vaginal trichomoniasis.[1] When infection persists without reexposure, retreatment uses higher-dose or longer nitroimidazole courses, and clinicians can request drug-resistance testing from CDC to guide care.[1]

SituationRetreatment regimen
Reexposed to an untreated partnerRepeat the same metronidazole regimen (women: 500 mg twice daily for 7 days; men: a single 2 g dose)[1]
Not reexposed, persistent infectionWomen: metronidazole or tinidazole 2 g once daily for 7 days; men: metronidazole 500 mg twice daily for 7 days[1]
Documented resistanceCan include metronidazole or tinidazole 2 g daily for 7 days, guided by resistance testing[1]

Complications

Untreated trichomoniasis is not benign. It is associated with a 1.5-fold increased risk of acquiring HIV and with more HIV vaginal shedding, and among women with HIV it is associated with a higher risk of pelvic inflammatory disease.[1] During pregnancy it is linked to premature rupture of membranes, preterm birth and small-for-gestational-age infants.[1] Treatment reduces symptoms and signs and might reduce transmission, although CDC notes that data are lacking on whether screening and treating women without symptoms in high-prevalence settings reduces these harms.[1]

A Note on Pregnancy

Trichomoniasis during pregnancy is associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, and symptomatic pregnant people should be tested and treated at any stage of pregnancy.[1] Data indicate metronidazole poses a low risk to the developing fetus, while tinidazole should be avoided in pregnancy.[1] This guide's treatment discussion is for adults who are not pregnant, and any infection during pregnancy should be managed by the obstetric team rather than through general telehealth care.

Prevention

Prevention is straightforward and familiar. Consistent and correct condom use is the best way for sexually active people to prevent it, douching is not recommended because it might increase the risk of vaginal infections including trichomoniasis, and partners should be treated together so the infection does not bounce back.[1] Because most infections are silent, CDC suggests talking about STI risk before sex with a new partner and asking your clinician whether you should be tested.[2]

When to See a Doctor

See a clinician if

You have any vaginal or penile discharge, itching, burning on urination, or you have had a partner diagnosed with an STI. Testing is recommended for women seeking care for vaginal discharge, and current partners of someone with trichomoniasis should be treated.[1] Because most infections are silent, ask a clinician about testing after any new partner even without symptoms.[2]
