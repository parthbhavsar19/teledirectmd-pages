Medical Care in the US for Visitors From Mexico

A physician's evidence-based guide for the 13.4 million Mexicans who visited the US by land in 2024: how a US pharmacy handles your Metfor, Norvas, and Rivotril prescriptions, how the FDA 90-day rule and DEA controlled-substance rules apply when you cross into the US, and why diabetes deserves attention in Mexican-American adults.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: August 16, 2026
15 min read

How can a visitor from Mexico keep their prescriptions and chronic conditions on track during a US trip?

In 2024, 13.4 million Mexicans visited the US by land (NTTO 2024). Federal law requires prescription drugs to be dispensed on the prescription of a practitioner licensed by law (21 U.S.C. 353(b)), so a Mexican prescription generally cannot be filled at a US pharmacy; a visit with a US-licensed clinician, including by telehealth, can result in a US prescription for non-controlled chronic medications (blood pressure, thyroid, oral diabetes, statins, most inhalers) if the clinician judges it appropriate. Under the FDA Personal Importation Policy foreign nationals may bring or ship a 90-day supply of drug products, and FDA says a drug approved in another country but unapproved in the US is illegal to import. Some medications commonly prescribed in Mexico are US Schedule IV controlled substances (Rivotril / clonazepam, Tafil / alprazolam, Ambien / zolpidem, tramadol), and DEA allows foreign visitors to bring up to 50 dosage units of controlled substances for personal use (DEA). Per the CDC National Diabetes Statistics Report, crude diagnosed diabetes prevalence among Mexican or Mexican American adults was 11.1% (2019-2021), compared with 13.3% in Puerto Rican adults and 8.5% in non-Hispanic White adults. Entities covered by Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act, such as hospitals that receive federal financial assistance, must provide language assistance free of charge, including qualified interpreters (HHS OCR letter, December 2024; full implementation by July 5, 2025).
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated August 16, 2026.

In 2024, the National Travel and Tourism Office counted 13.4 million Mexicans who made overnight visits to the US by land.[1] Over 50 percent of that travel originated in six of Mexico's 31 states, five of which border the US (Baja California, Sonora, Chihuahua, Coahuila, and Nuevo Leon), and 45.8 percent of these visitors went to California, followed by Texas (36.5 percent) and Arizona (11.1 percent).[1]

This guide supplements the broader pillar guide on medical care for international visitors and the general satellites, Running Out of Medication in the US and Chronic Conditions While Your Parents Visit the US, with the Mexico-specific version. The differences that actually matter are: (1) Mexican brand names do not carry over to US pharmacies, (2) the border-versus-interior distinction changes the practical path, although the FDA 90-day and DEA controlled-substance rules are the same, (3) diabetes is common among Mexican-American adults, and (4) Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act requires covered entities, including federally funded hospitals, to provide free language assistance, including qualified interpreters.[9]

Why Your Mexican Prescription Cannot Be Filled at a US Pharmacy

Federal law requires a prescription drug to be dispensed only on the prescription of a practitioner licensed by law to administer it.[2] A clinician who wrote your original prescription in Tijuana, Ciudad Juarez, Nogales, Mexicali, Nuevo Progreso, Monterrey, Guadalajara, or Mexico City and is licensed only in Mexico generally does not meet that standard, so a US pharmacy generally cannot fill the prescription, even for an identical medication.

The practical result: for stable visitors on chronic non-controlled medications, a visit with a US-licensed clinician, including a physician-led telehealth visit, can result in a US prescription that a US pharmacy can fill, if the clinician judges it appropriate. Cash prices for the same prescription vary by pharmacy, and free coupon services such as GoodRx can help you pay less than the pharmacy's cash price.[6]

A few notes on gaps in this map:

  • Lercadip (lercanidipine) and other medications sold in Mexico may not have a US version. FDA says that a drug approved in another country but unapproved in the US is illegal to import.[3] If a medication you take is not sold in the US, ask a US-licensed clinician about an FDA-approved alternative.
  • Combined-formulation Mexican OTC products (like Cofepris-approved combination cough or GI preparations) often do not have a single US equivalent; a US clinician will typically split them into their individual components.
  • Herbal or non-pharmaceutical products sold through Mexican pharmacies have no direct US prescription substitute, and DEA warns that medications legal abroad, prescription or over-the-counter, can be confiscated at entry if they are not approved for use in the US.[4] Talk with a clinician before you stop or substitute anything.
  • Certain sustained-release or matrix formulations from Mexican manufacturers may not have a US bioequivalent; the US clinician will substitute the closest FDA-approved formulation, which may be immediate-release or a different extended-release product.

Border Crossers vs Interior Visitors: How the Rules Practically Differ

The single distinction that matters most for Mexican visitors is whether you are a frequent border crosser (Baja California to San Diego, Tijuana to San Diego, Ciudad Juarez to El Paso, Nuevo Progreso to South Texas) or an interior US visitor (Mexico City to Los Angeles, Monterrey to Chicago, Guadalajara to Dallas). The rules are the same but the practical situation is different.

Border-crossing visitors often fill prescriptions in Mexico on their regular return trips and only need US care when something goes wrong on the US side. For this group, the primary questions are: (1) can I bring my Mexican prescription across the border for a personal-use quantity (yes, subject to FDA 90-day and DEA 50-dosage-unit limits), and (2) if I run out on the US side and cannot return home immediately, can a US clinician bridge me (often yes for non-controlled medications, if the clinician judges it appropriate; for controlled medications, see the DEA telemedicine rules below[12]).

Interior visitors face a different question: they are typically farther from a US-Mexico border pharmacy than from a US pharmacy, so a US telehealth visit and a US pharmacy fill is usually the fastest path.

The FDA and DEA rules that follow apply to both groups.

The FDA 90-Day Rule at Southwest Border Ports

Under the FDA Personal Importation Policy, foreign nationals visiting the US may bring or ship a 90-day supply of their personal medication into the US.[3] The FDA states: "The FDA will allow foreign nationals to bring or ship a 90-day supply of drug products," and "if the foreign national is staying longer than 90 days, they may have additional medication sent to them." The FDA definition of a foreign national is anyone who is not a US citizen or permanent resident.[3]

If you have medication sent to you by mail or courier, FDA suggests including documentation that it is for your own use while visiting the US, such as:[3]

  • A copy of your visa or passport (a Border Crossing Card is also a B1/B2 visitor visa, issued only to citizens and residents of Mexico[27])
  • A letter from your doctor (the CDC Yellow Book suggests a clinician's letter list your medications with generic names and doses[10])
  • A copy of the prescription, in English

For any medication you carry across the border, DEA advises keeping it in its original container and declaring all medications to US Customs and Border Protection. Medications that are legal abroad, prescription or over-the-counter, can still be confiscated if they are not approved for use in the US.[4]

DEA-Controlled Mexican Brands and Non-FDA-Approved Medications

Several medications routinely prescribed in Mexico are Schedule IV controlled substances in the US.[14] Separately, FDA says a drug approved in another country but unapproved in the US is illegal to import.[3] For controlled substances, DEA rules apply.[4] Foreign visitors may bring controlled substances into the US if:

  1. The medication is in its original labeled container.
  2. The label includes the drug's chemical or trade name and schedule symbol, or, if the name is not on the label, the pharmacy or practitioner information and a prescription number.
  3. The quantity does not exceed 50 dosage units of controlled substances for personal use.
  4. The medication is declared to Customs and Border Protection at entry.

What US Pharmacies Actually Cost: Cash Prices Vary

US pharmacy prices are not fixed. GoodRx, a price-comparison service, says drug prices vary by pharmacy and that its free coupons can help you pay less than the cash price for brand and generic drugs; GoodRx is not insurance.[6] Compare the price at more than one pharmacy before you fill a prescription.

If you take a brand-name inhaler, an injectable diabetes medication such as semaglutide (Ozempic) or dulaglutide, or a specialty biologic, plan to bring your FDA 90-day supply, and if you stay longer than 90 days, ask your Mexican clinician about having more medication sent to you.[3]

For a broader review of what a US pharmacy can and cannot fill, see the sister satellite, Running Out of Medication in the US.

Diabetes in the Mexican-American Population: More Common Than in Non-Hispanic White Adults

Diabetes is common among Mexican-American adults. In the CDC National Diabetes Statistics Report (Table 2, National Health Interview Survey 2019-2021), crude prevalence of diagnosed diabetes among Mexican or Mexican American adults aged 18 or older was 11.1 percent (95% CI 9.9-12.3), compared with 13.3 percent among Puerto Rican, 9.0 percent among Cuban, 7.3 percent among Central American, and 5.0 percent among South American adults, and 8.5 percent among non-Hispanic White adults.[7] These are crude figures, not adjusted for age. In an earlier national survey (NHANES 2005-2006), age- and sex-standardized prevalence of diagnosed diabetes was approximately twice as high in Mexican Americans as in non-Hispanic whites.[15] In an analysis of 3,165 Latino adults in NHANES 2007-2010, O'Brien and colleagues found that 12.4 percent had diabetes (diagnosed, or HbA1c of 6.5 percent or higher), and that greater acculturation was associated with a higher risk of diabetes.[8]

The clinical implications for a visiting Mexican adult, particularly an older one:

  • Diabetes is common, so tell any US clinician about a known diabetes diagnosis and bring your glucose readings and medication list.
  • Symptoms attributable to poorly controlled glucose (fatigue, blurred vision, frequent urination) should not be dismissed as travel-related.
  • For visitors on basal insulin, a review of basal insulin practice advises that for short trips (5 or fewer time zones, or under 3 days) people keep their watch on home time and continue basal insulin as usual, and it describes a 4 percent dose adjustment per time zone for trips across 5 or more time zones with stays longer than 3 days. It also advises discussing travel plans with the diabetes team well in advance.[18] See also Chronic Conditions While Your Parents Visit the US.

In 2020, among adults aged 65 or older, the age-adjusted death rate with diabetes as the underlying or a contributing cause was 778.5 per 100,000 in Hispanic adults, compared with 493.3 in non-Hispanic White adults, and lower than in non-Hispanic American Indian or Alaska Native and non-Hispanic Black adults.[13] The threshold for evaluating new chest pain, exertional shortness of breath, or unexplained fatigue in an older Mexican visitor should be the same as for any adult with metabolic risk factors: prompt evaluation, not wait-and-see. For heart attack symptoms such as chest pain or discomfort or shortness of breath, CDC says to call 911 immediately.[19]

Your Section 1557 Language Rights in US Healthcare

Under Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act, covered entities, including health programs and activities that receive federal financial assistance, must take reasonable steps to provide meaningful access to people with limited English proficiency, and language assistance services, including qualified interpreters, must be free of charge, accurate, and timely.[9][16] The final rule was published May 6, 2024 (89 Fed. Reg. 37522). In a December 5, 2024 letter, Melanie Fontes Rainer, Director of the HHS Office for Civil Rights, said the language-access requirements must be fully implemented by July 5, 2025; the letter also notes that certain provisions of the rule are stayed or enjoined.[9]

What Section 1557 requires, in plain terms:[9]

  • Free of charge. Language assistance must be provided free of charge, and a covered entity cannot require you to bring your own interpreter or pay for one.[16]
  • Qualified interpreters. A qualified interpreter has demonstrated proficiency in English and at least one other spoken language, such as Spanish, can interpret effectively, accurately, and impartially, and follows interpreter ethics, including confidentiality. A covered entity must not rely on an adult who is not a qualified interpreter, such as a family member, except in limited situations: an emergency involving an imminent threat, or when you specifically ask for it in private.[9][16]
  • Minors cannot be used as interpreters except as a temporary measure, while a qualified interpreter is found, in an emergency involving an imminent threat to safety or welfare.[9]
  • Written translations. If a covered entity uses machine translation for critical documents, a qualified human translator must review the translation for accuracy.[9]
  • Posted notices. Covered entities must provide a notice that language assistance is available, in English and in at least the 15 languages most commonly spoken by people with limited English proficiency in the relevant state or states.[9]

The practical implication: if you go to a US emergency department, urgent care, or clinic that is a covered entity, are not offered a Spanish interpreter, and would like one, you can ask for one. The facility cannot require you to bring your own interpreter.[9][16]

What US Telehealth Can Bridge for a Mexican Visitor

For non-controlled chronic medications, a physician-led US telehealth visit can result in a US prescription if the clinician judges it appropriate. Cash prices for the medication itself vary by pharmacy.[6]

Common chronic medications a US telehealth clinician can typically bridge for a stable Mexican visitor:

  • Blood pressure: amlodipine (Norvas), losartan (Cozaar, Losacor), telmisartan (Micardis), enalapril (Enaladil, Renitec).
  • Thyroid: levothyroxine (Eutirox).
  • Type 2 diabetes: metformin (Metfor, Dabex), sitagliptin (Januvia), empagliflozin (Jardiance), dapagliflozin.
  • Statins: atorvastatin (Ator), rosuvastatin.
  • SSRIs: sertraline (Altruline), escitalopram, fluoxetine.
  • Asthma: albuterol (Ventolin), budesonide/formoterol (Symbicort).
  • GI: omeprazole (Losec, Inhibitron), pantoprazole.

When to Skip Telehealth and Go In Person

Bridge prescriptions are for stable visitors with no new symptoms. Go to a US urgent care, emergency department, or call 911 if any of the following:

  • New chest pain, pressure, tightness, or exertional shortness of breath. Do not wait to see if it improves. This is a 911 call.[19]
  • New unilateral weakness, facial droop, or slurred speech: FAST protocol, 911.[20]
  • Severe headache, worst of life, sudden and explosive, or with neurological symptoms: ER or 911.[21]
  • Signs of a heart failure exacerbation: shortness of breath when lying down, ankle swelling with weight gain, or shortness of breath at rest. Call 911 for severe shortness of breath.[22][25]
  • Uncontrolled diabetes: if your blood sugar is 250 mg/dL or above, check for ketones; go to the ER or call 911 if your blood sugar stays at 300 mg/dL or above, if you are vomiting and cannot keep food or drinks down, or if your ketones are high.[23] For symptoms of low blood sugar, check your glucose if you can, follow the 15-15 rule, and contact a clinician.[18] This matters given how common diabetes is among Mexican-American adults.
  • Unilateral leg swelling, warmth, redness, or unexplained pain or tenderness after a long trip (CDC notes that travel of more than 4 hours, by air, car, bus, or train, can raise the risk of blood clots): same-day urgent care for DVT evaluation; CDC advises contacting a doctor as soon as possible.[24]
  • Persistent vomiting or dehydration in an older visitor: urgent care; go to an emergency department if vomiting or diarrhea does not stop. Older adults are at higher risk of dehydration.[25][26]
  • Fall with head injury or possible fracture: ER. Call 911 if a head injury comes with passing out, fainting, or confusion.[21][25]

Under Section 1557, you can ask for a Spanish interpreter at an urgent care or ER that is a covered entity, such as a hospital that receives federal financial assistance; language assistance must be free of charge.[9][16]

For the general framework of when to use 911 vs ER vs urgent care vs telehealth for an international visitor, see the parent guide, Medical Care for International Visitors.
