How to Use a US Pharmacy as an International Visitor

A practical playbook for visitors, and the adult children booking care on their behalf: what ID US pharmacies accept, how to actually pick up a prescription, which discount cards work without insurance, and which manufacturer programs are out of reach without US residency.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: August 16, 2026
16 min read

How does an international visitor pick up a prescription at a US pharmacy?

US pharmacies dispense prescription drugs on the prescription of a practitioner licensed by law, so a visitor whose prescription is only from a home-country doctor should plan on a new one from a US-licensed clinician. Which photo ID a pharmacy accepts at pickup depends on the state and the pharmacy. North Carolina's law, for example, requires a driver's license, state ID card, military ID or passport before a Schedule II controlled substance is dispensed, and the state pharmacy board says the passport need not be US-issued; national IDs such as India's Aadhaar or Mexico's INE are not on that list, so carry a passport. Discount services such as GoodRx and SingleCare are free and are not insurance; SingleCare says its coupons cannot be combined with insurance. Federal law requires pharmacists to offer counseling to Medicaid patients, and most states extended counseling to all patients, so ask the pharmacist to explain the medicine before leaving the counter. For visitors booking through TeleDirectMD, the platform accepts a passport or any government-issued photo ID; workplace and student IDs are excluded.

Key Takeaways

  • Which ID a US pharmacy asks for at pickup depends on the state and the pharmacy. North Carolina, for example, requires a driver's license, a state ID card, a military ID or a passport before it dispenses a Schedule II controlled substance,[1] and its pharmacy board says the passport does not have to be US-issued.[2] Carry your passport.
  • Federal law says prescription drugs are dispensed only on the prescription "of a practitioner licensed by law to administer such drug."[3] For controlled substances, the prescriber must also be authorized where licensed and registered with, or exempted by, the DEA.[4] In practice, a visitor with a prescription only from a home-country doctor should plan on a new prescription from a US-licensed clinician.
  • Prescription discount services are not insurance. GoodRx calls itself "a free mobile app and website,"[5] and SingleCare says its free coupons "cannot be combined with insurance."[6] Savings figures are the companies' own: ScriptSave WellRx says savings average 45% per prescription, based on its 2015 program data.[7]
  • Several manufacturer programs require US residency: Lilly Cares requires permanent US residency,[8] and Lilly's $35 insulin program requires US or US-territory residency.[9] A visitor on a short trip should not count on them.
  • Under HIPAA at 45 CFR 164.502(g), an adult child is a parent's personal representative only with legal authority to make health care decisions, such as a health care power of attorney.[10] Without it, a pharmacist may still use professional judgment to let a family member pick up a filled prescription.[11]
  • Federal law (OBRA '90) requires pharmacists to offer to discuss prescriptions with Medicaid patients,[12] and most states extended counseling to all patients.[13] Ask for it before you leave the counter.

1. ID Accepted at US Pharmacies

A pharmacy may ask for photo ID at pickup so the pharmacist can confirm who is receiving the medicine. It is an identity check, not a citizenship check. Which IDs count depends on the state and on the pharmacy's own policy, so the rules differ from place to place.

North Carolina is one example of a state law. Immediately before dispensing a Schedule II controlled substance, a pharmacy there must see one of four "valid, unexpired forms of government-issued photographic identification": a driver's license, a state special identification card, a military identification card or a passport. The pharmacy then records the person's name, the type of ID and the ID number.[1] The state's Board of Pharmacy says the passport does not have to be US-issued and that a foreign-government driver's license does not appear to be excluded, but that employee badges, Mexican consulate IDs and green cards do not meet the rule.[2] Other states, and non-controlled prescriptions, may follow different rules. Of the four documents on that list, a passport is the one every visitor can carry, so it is the ID to bring.

For DEA-controlled substances (the DEA classifies them into five schedules),[14] expect more checks. Under North Carolina's rule, for example, the pharmacy writes down the type and number of the ID shown,[1] so a passport number may be recorded. This is normal.

2. Country-Specific ID: What Aadhaar, INE, and National IDs Do and Don't Do

A home-country ID can prove who you are at a counter that accepts it, but it does not do the things that US residency or a US document would do. Understanding this distinction saves visitors a lot of confusion.

What foreign IDs DO at a US pharmacy

  • Prove identity at prescription pickup, where the pharmacy or the state rule accepts that ID
  • Match the name on the prescription to the person picking it up

What foreign IDs DO NOT do

  • Qualify a visitor for manufacturer programs that require US residency[8][9]
  • Serve as a Social Security number (a foreign ID does not carry one)

The Aadhaar question comes up often, so it is worth addressing directly. UIDAI, India's issuing authority, defines Aadhaar as "a 12 digit individual identification number," and enrolment collects a face photograph, ten fingerprints and iris images.[15] At a US pharmacy it is a foreign national ID card, and as the North Carolina example shows, it may not meet a state's ID rule for controlled substances.[1] Bring the passport too.

The same applies to Mexico's Credencial para Votar, the voter credential issued by the Instituto Nacional Electoral (INE).[16] Carry a passport as well.

3. Booking on Behalf of a Visiting Parent: HIPAA and Proxy Consent

Adult children of visiting parents often manage the logistics: they book the telehealth visit, sit in on the call to interpret, and pick up the medication. All of that is allowed, but the rules are specific.

Under the HIPAA Privacy Rule at 45 CFR 164.502(g), an adult child is a parent's personal representative only if they have legal authority to make health care decisions for the parent, for example a health care power of attorney, a court-appointed guardianship, or a durable power of attorney that covers health care decisions. Without that authority, the Privacy Rule at 45 CFR 164.510(b) separately covers "family members or other persons who are involved in the individual's health care or payment for care," who may receive information relevant to that involvement.[10]

In practice, for a visiting adult parent who is fully competent, the workflow is:

  1. The parent signs a proxy consent form or authorization allowing the adult child to receive health information and communicate with the clinician on the parent's behalf.
  2. The parent is present on the telehealth call, either speaking or listening with the child interpreting.
  3. The prescription is written in the parent's name.
  4. The parent, or a family member the pharmacy agrees to release it to, picks up the medication.

HHS says a pharmacist may use professional judgment to let a relative or friend pick up a filled prescription, and that the patient "does not need to provide the pharmacist with the names of such persons in advance."[11] Pharmacies set their own pickup policies for controlled substances, so call the pharmacy ahead if the parent cannot come in.

Note for family members booking on TeleDirectMD: The parent's proxy consent form is signed as part of the intake process. Bring the parent to the call for the first visit so the clinician can verify identity and consent directly. This is faster than trying to reconstruct proxy authorization after the fact.

4. The Pharmacy Pickup Workflow, Step by Step

This is the workflow at a typical US pharmacy chain (CVS, Walgreens, Walmart, Costco and others) for a new prescription sent electronically from a telehealth clinician:

  1. The clinician sends the prescription electronically. The visitor does not carry a paper Rx; it is transmitted from the clinician's software to the pharmacy.
  2. The pharmacy receives it. Many pharmacies send a text or call when it is ready. Otherwise, call the pharmacy and ask when it will be ready.
  3. Arrive at the pharmacy counter. Not the front cashier; the pharmacy counter, which is usually at the back of the store or a separate drive-through window.
  4. Say "I'm picking up a prescription for [full name] with date of birth [MM/DD/YYYY]." Give the patient's full name as it appears on the passport or ID, not a shortened name.
  5. Show the government-issued ID. The pharmacy staff will compare it against the prescription record.
  6. Give the discount coupon, if using one, before you pay. GoodRx tells users to "Show your coupon when picking up your prescription."[5] Hand it over at the start, before the pharmacy rings up the price.
  7. Pay. Ask ahead if you plan to pay with a card issued outside the US.
  8. Accept the pharmacist counseling. Federal law requires the offer for Medicaid patients,[12] and most states extended counseling to all patients.[13] Take five minutes. Ask what the medicine is for, how to take it, what to avoid, and what side effects to watch for.
  9. Verify the label. Before leaving the counter, check the name on the label, the drug name, dose, and instructions. Mistakes are cheap to catch here.

Wait times vary by pharmacy and by time of day. Ask the pharmacy when the prescription will be ready.

6. Prescription Discount Cards: Which Ones, and How to Use Them

US prescription prices are high compared with other high-income countries: a 2024 RAND report for HHS found that, across all drugs, US prices were 278 percent of prices in 33 other high-income countries, using 2022 data.[20] Discount services lower the cash price at the counter. The savings figures are the companies' own claims: GoodRx and SingleCare say savings can reach up to 80%,[5][6] and ScriptSave WellRx says savings average 45% per prescription, based on its 2015 program data.[7]

None of these services is insurance.[5][6] The main pages read for this review (November 2026) did not mention a US residency, insurance or Social Security number requirement, but read each service's terms before signing up:

The workflow tip that matters most: compare prices before showing up. GoodRx says the price for the same prescription can differ by $100 or more between pharmacies.[5] Do this comparison from a phone, with the exact drug, dose and quantity, before choosing a pharmacy.

7. Manufacturer Patient Assistance for Expensive Prescriptions

For expensive brand medications, manufacturer patient assistance programs (PAPs) can lower the cost for people who qualify. The catch: several PAPs require US residency. Lilly Cares requires that "You are a permanent resident of the United States,"[8] and Novo Nordisk's PAP requires applicants to "Be a US citizen or legal resident."[22] A visitor on a 3-week trip should not expect to qualify.

The important distinction is PAP versus manufacturer savings offer. PAPs are financial-need programs with income limits.[8][22] Savings offers such as the $35 insulin programs are separate, but they can still carry residency terms: Lilly's requires US or US-territory residency.[9] Read the terms before counting on either.

8. Community Options: 340B Health Centers and Sliding-Scale Clinics

HRSA-supported health centers are among the covered entities in the 340B Drug Pricing Program, under which participating manufacturers "agree to provide outpatient drugs to covered entities at significantly reduced prices."[26]

Two features make this relevant for visitors:

  • Health centers provide primary medical and dental care "whether or not they have health insurance."[27]
  • Services are provided on a sliding fee scale, based on ability to pay.[27] Visitors typically don't have US income documentation, so ask the health center what it needs.

For a visitor already using telehealth, a health center pharmacy is not usually where the prescription is filled. But for a visitor who develops a more complex issue and needs in-person care, a health center is a good place to look.

Find a health center via HRSA's Find a Health Center tool. Enter the ZIP code where the visitor is staying.

9. When Cash Pay at a US Pharmacy Is Genuinely Cheaper

Many visitors assume US medicine prices are always higher than at home. For brand-name drugs that is usually right: the 2024 RAND report for HHS found US gross prices for brand-name originator drugs were 422 percent of prices in 33 other high-income countries. Generics are the exception: the same report found "The United States had lower prices for unbranded generics than most countries."[20]

Two limits apply. The comparison countries were high-income countries, so the report does not show how US prices compare with pharmacies in India, Mexico or other middle-income countries. And the figures are averages across many drugs, not the price of any one medicine.

Situations where US cash pay may be cheaper:

  • Generic medications on low-price lists. Walmart says covered generics start at $4 for a 30-day supply.[17]
  • Generics from transparent-price pharmacies. Cost Plus Drugs publishes its 15% markup and fee structure.[21]

Situations where US cash pay is not likely to be cheaper:

  • Brand-name medications, which cost far more in the US than in other high-income countries on average[20]
  • Insulin outside a $35 program, if you do not meet the program's residency terms[9]

The practical answer for visitors: check GoodRx or SingleCare from a phone, for the exact drug, dose and quantity, before assuming either country will be cheaper.

10. Red Flags: Fake Sites, Sketchy Discount Cards, and Common Visitor Scams

FDA warns that some websites that look like pharmacies are unsafe, and visitors searching without local context should be careful.[28] Warning signs:

  • An online pharmacy that does not require a doctor's prescription. FDA says safe online pharmacies "Always require a doctor's prescription,"[28] and federal law limits prescription drugs to dispensing on a licensed practitioner's prescription.[3]
  • A website that is not licensed in the US and by your state board of pharmacy, or that has no licensed pharmacist on staff to answer questions.[28]
  • Deep discounts or prices that seem too good to be true.[28]
  • Discount cards that charge a fee without explaining what you get. GoodRx and SingleCare describe their basic coupon services as free;[5][6] read the terms before paying for any card or membership.
  • Anyone on the phone or online who asks you to pay for a prescription with a gift card. The FTC says "Only scammers will tell you to buy a gift card."[29]
  • "International pharmacy" ads on social media offering "the same medicines cheaper." FDA warns that unsafe sites may make you think their medicines come from countries with high safety standards, when the medicines "could have been made anywhere."[28]

The Safe.Pharmacy website lets you "Enter a website URL to verify before you buy."[30] If in doubt, check the pharmacy there.

11. Get the Free Counseling: It's Required by Law

The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1990 (OBRA '90) brought in a federal rule that "The pharmacist must offer to discuss" prescriptions with each person receiving Medicaid benefits.[12] OBRA 1990 was implemented in 1993, and most states acted quickly to extend counseling to all patients receiving prescription drugs. In a 2003 study of pharmacies filling one test prescription, 69% offered counseling.[13] The pharmacist makes the offer; accepting it is up to the patient.

Under the federal rule, the matters the pharmacist may discuss include:[12]

  • Name and description of the medication
  • Route, dosage form, dosage, and duration of drug therapy
  • Special directions and precautions for preparation, administration, and use
  • Common severe side effects or interactions, and therapeutic contraindications
  • Techniques for self-monitoring drug therapy
  • Proper storage
  • Prescription refill information
  • Action to be taken in the event of a missed dose

For a visitor unfamiliar with US medication systems, ask specifically:

  • "What is this medicine for?"
  • "How and when should I take it?"
  • "What foods, drinks, or other medicines should I avoid?"
  • "What side effects should I watch for, and when should I stop the medicine and get help?"
  • "Is it safe to take with the medicines I brought from home?" (Bring a list of home medicines, with generic names.)

Ask whether there is a private area to talk. If the visitor prefers Spanish, Mandarin, Hindi, or another language, ask whether interpreter help is available.

12. The Visitor Pharmacy Checklist

Before the pharmacy visit:

  • Confirm the prescription was sent by the US clinician (get confirmation from the telehealth visit).
  • Have the patient's passport, plus any other government-issued photo ID.
  • Compare prices at 2 or 3 nearby pharmacies using GoodRx or SingleCare.
  • Print or save the coupon on a phone.
  • Have a list of the patient's current home-country medications with generic names.

At the pharmacy counter:

  • Give the patient's full name and date of birth as they appear on the passport.
  • Show the government-issued ID.
  • Show the discount coupon before you pay.
  • Ask for the pharmacist counseling.
  • Verify the label at the counter before leaving.

If problems arise:

  • If the pharmacy refuses a foreign ID, politely ask which ID it accepts, ask for the pharmacy manager, or call another location. Show the passport.
  • If quoted an unusually high price, ask "what's the price with a GoodRx coupon?" and show the coupon.
  • If the prescription is not on file, call the telehealth clinician; e-prescriptions occasionally route to the wrong pharmacy branch.
