Tooth Infection Antibiotics: What to Take and When (2026 Guide)

Amoxicillin 500 mg three times a day (or penicillin VK) is first-line for a dental infection with systemic involvement, per the ADA 2019 guideline. Antibiotics do not fix a diseased tooth. They buy time until you can see a dentist.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: August 27, 2026
Read time: 14 min

What is the best antibiotic for a tooth infection?

For an adult with a tooth abscess that is causing systemic involvement (such as fever, malaise, or spread into the fascial spaces or local lymph nodes), the ADA 2019 clinical practice guideline (Lockhart et al., JADA) suggests amoxicillin 500 mg by mouth three times a day, or penicillin VK 500 mg four times a day, for 3 to 7 days, together with urgent definitive dental treatment or an urgent dental referral. For a penicillin allergy without a history of anaphylaxis, angioedema, or hives, it suggests cephalexin; with that history, it lists azithromycin or clindamycin 300 mg four times a day. Clindamycin carries an FDA Black Box warning for C. difficile infection, and the guideline notes that about 10% of people report a penicillin allergy while less than 1% are truly allergic. Antibiotics are not a substitute for dental care: the guideline says immediate definitive dental treatment should be prioritized in all cases.

🗂 Key Takeaways

  • The ADA 2019 guideline recommends against antibiotics in most scenarios for pulpal and periapical dental pain and intraoral swelling in immunocompetent adults. It suggests antibiotics when systemic involvement is present or the risk of progression to systemic involvement is high, and says immediate definitive dental treatment (such as a root canal procedure or incision and drainage) should be prioritized in all cases.[1]
  • When antibiotics are indicated, the guideline suggests amoxicillin 500 mg three times a day or penicillin VK 500 mg four times a day, for 3 to 7 days.[1]
  • If first-line treatment does not work promptly, the guideline says clinicians may add metronidazole (500 mg three times a day, 7 days) or switch to amoxicillin-clavulanate (500/125 mg three times a day, 7 days).[1]
  • For a penicillin allergy without anaphylaxis, angioedema, or hives, the guideline suggests cephalexin 500 mg four times a day for 3 to 7 days. With that history, it lists azithromycin (500 mg on day 1, then 250 mg for four more days) or clindamycin 300 mg four times a day for 3 to 7 days.[1]
  • A Cochrane review found insufficient evidence to determine whether antibiotics reduce the pain of irreversible pulpitis; it rested on a single trial of 40 people. The tooth needs definitive treatment.[2]
  • In a national 2019 Veterans Affairs study, only 17.5% of dental visits with an antibiotic met a guideline-based definition of appropriate use, and 64.3% met a broader literature-based definition.[3]
  • Trouble breathing or swallowing, drooling, swelling under the tongue or in the neck, a swollen jaw, or eyelid swelling with fever means emergency care, not a dentist or a telehealth visit.[4][5][6][7]

When Antibiotics Are (and Are Not) Indicated

The 2019 American Dental Association (ADA) evidence-based clinical practice guideline by Lockhart and colleagues is the operative document in the United States. Based on a systematic review, its panel recommended against antibiotics in most clinical scenarios for pulpal- and periapical-related dental pain and intraoral swelling, and recommended them for patients with systemic involvement (for example, malaise or fever) or when the risk of progression to systemic involvement is high. Its scope is immunocompetent adults; people with cellulitis or a compromised immune system are outside it.[1]

The guideline describes an acute apical abscess with systemic involvement as one with evidence of fascial space or local lymph node involvement, fever, and/or malaise. Below that line, it is specific. For symptomatic irreversible pulpitis, it recommends no antibiotics. For pulp necrosis with symptomatic apical periodontitis, it suggests no antibiotics, with a delayed prescription only if definitive dental treatment is not feasible. For pulp necrosis with a localized acute apical abscess when dental treatment is not immediately available, it suggests amoxicillin or penicillin VK plus urgent referral. When definitive dental treatment is available right away, it recommends against adding antibiotics.[1]

The reason for the restraint is the balance of benefit and harm. The ADA panel judged the benefits of antibiotics for these conditions as likely negligible and the harms as potentially large, and noted that oral antibiotics may not reach the affected tooth because of its lack of vascular supply.[1] The 2019 Cochrane review by Agnihotry and colleagues found insufficient evidence to determine whether antibiotics reduce the pain of irreversible pulpitis.[2] The 2018 Cochrane review by Cope and colleagues on symptomatic apical periodontitis and acute apical abscess included two trials (62 participants) of penicillin VK versus placebo given with pulpectomy and pain relievers; the review found no statistically significant differences in pain or swelling at any time point, and the authors rated the evidence as very low quality and insufficient to determine an effect.[8]

The framing that matters: "Antibiotics don't fix teeth" is the sentence to remember. If you have a hot pulp or a walled-off abscess, you need a dentist for drainage, a root canal, or an extraction. Antibiotics are how we keep the infection from spreading during the interval before that definitive care.

First-Line: Amoxicillin

The ADA 2019 guideline names oral amoxicillin or oral penicillin VK as the antibiotics to use when a tooth infection meets its threshold. The panel ordered its list of antibiotics by balancing the desirable and undesirable consequences of each one, including the risk of allergic reactions and C. difficile infection.[1]

Adult dose

Amoxicillin 500 mg by mouth three times a day for 3 to 7 days, or penicillin VK 500 mg by mouth four times a day for 3 to 7 days. These are the regimens in the guideline's recommendations and good practice statements.[1]

Why 3 to 7 days and not the classic 10

The ADA regimens run 3 to 7 days. The guideline tells clinicians to reevaluate the patient within 3 days, in person or by phone, and to instruct patients to stop the antibiotic 24 hours after their symptoms resolve. It also notes there is currently little to no evidence supporting the common belief that a shortened course of antibiotics contributes to antimicrobial resistance.[1]

What to check before prescribing

Ask about penicillin allergy history and press for details (see the penicillin allergy section below). Ask about pregnancy: the amoxicillin label says it should be used during pregnancy only if clearly needed. The label warns that serious and occasionally fatal anaphylactic reactions have been reported with penicillin therapy, and that Clostridioides difficile-associated diarrhea, from mild diarrhea to fatal colitis, can occur. It also lists reduced efficacy of combined oral estrogen/progesterone contraceptives as a possible interaction, so ask your clinician whether you need a backup method.[9]

Amoxicillin-Clavulanate (Augmentin) When First-Line Treatment Fails

In the ADA guideline, amoxicillin-clavulanate is not a first-line drug. It is one of two options for a case that does not respond promptly to first-line antibiotics, chosen to enhance activity against Gram-negative anaerobic organisms. The guideline's framework for these situations:[1]

  • No prompt response to amoxicillin or penicillin VK: add oral metronidazole to the first-line drug, or stop it and switch to oral amoxicillin-clavulanate
  • Reevaluate within 3 days, in person or by phone
  • Worsening condition, concern for deeper space infection, or an immediate threat to life: refer for urgent evaluation
  • Cellulitis or a compromised immune system: outside the guideline's scope, so treatment is individualized by the treating clinician

Adult dose: amoxicillin-clavulanate 500/125 mg three times a day for 7 days. The metronidazole add-on dose is 500 mg three times a day for 7 days.[1]

Penicillin Allergy: Clindamycin and Alternatives

Penicillin allergy is the highest-yield conversation in dental prescribing. The ADA guideline notes that approximately 10% of the population self-reports a penicillin allergy, but less than 1% of the entire population is truly allergic. It also says clinicians should proceed with non-penicillin antibiotics until a true penicillin allergy is confirmed.[1]

The problem: mislabeling of penicillin allergies is widespread and may push prescribers toward higher-risk alternatives such as clindamycin.[10] The ADA guideline warns that clindamycin substantially increases the risk of C. difficile infection, even after a single dose.[1] Ask the following before jumping to clindamycin:

  • What happened when you took penicillin or amoxicillin? A rash, hives, swelling of face or throat, trouble breathing, or anaphylaxis?
  • When did this happen?
  • Have you tolerated a cephalosporin (like cephalexin) or amoxicillin since?

The answers decide the alternative. For a penicillin allergy without a history of anaphylaxis, angioedema, or hives, the guideline suggests cephalexin. For a history of anaphylaxis, angioedema, or hives, it lists azithromycin or clindamycin instead.[1]

Clindamycin (severe allergy option)

Clindamycin 300 mg by mouth four times a day for 3 to 7 days. The guideline notes that clindamycin has an FDA Black Box warning for C. difficile infection, and that patients should call their primary care provider if they develop fever, abdominal cramping, or 3 or more loose bowel movements per day.[1]

Azithromycin (severe allergy option)

Azithromycin is the other ADA option for a penicillin allergy with a history of anaphylaxis, angioedema, or hives. Adult dose: a loading dose of 500 mg on day 1, then 250 mg once daily for four more days. The guideline notes that bacterial resistance rates for azithromycin are higher than for other antibiotics.[1]

A note on cephalexin

Cephalexin (a first-generation cephalosporin) is the option the ADA guideline suggests for a penicillin allergy without a history of anaphylaxis, angioedema, or hives: cephalexin 500 mg four times a day for 3 to 7 days. It is not the guideline's option when that history is present.[1]

Duration: Why Shorter Is Now Preferred

The ADA guideline's regimens run 3 to 7 days (7 days for the second-line options), and they are meant to bridge the patient to definitive dental treatment, which should not be delayed.[1]

The guideline's stopping rule: patients should stop the antibiotic 24 hours after their symptoms resolve, and the clinician should reevaluate within 3 days. If the condition worsens, or there is concern for a deeper space infection or an immediate threat to life, the next step is urgent evaluation, not a longer course.[1]

If Antibiotics Are Not Working

A dental infection that has not responded by the 3-day reevaluation the ADA guideline calls for needs a fresh look, not just a longer or stronger antibiotic.[1] The three questions to ask:

  1. Is there an undrained abscess? A pus collection needs to be opened. The guideline counts incision and drainage, like a root canal procedure, as definitive dental treatment, and prioritizes it in all cases.[1]
  2. Was the antibiotic actually taken? Adherence, GI intolerance, and prescription-fill delays are common failure points.
  3. Is the infection now beyond the local space? Odontogenic infection is the most common source of deep neck infection, which can cause airway obstruction, sepsis, and other life-threatening complications. Treatment rests on securing the airway, drainage, and appropriate antibiotics.[4]

Escalation options in the ADA guideline: for a case that does not respond promptly, add metronidazole 500 mg three times a day to the first-line drug, or switch to amoxicillin-clavulanate 500/125 mg three times a day, each for 7 days. If the condition worsens, or there is concern for a deeper space infection or an immediate threat to life, the guideline says to refer for urgent evaluation.[1]

Pain Management: NSAIDs Beat Opioids

Antibiotics are not a pain treatment. The ADA guideline says that for pain, NSAIDs and acetaminophen should be considered instead. An overview of systematic reviews found that ibuprofen combined with acetaminophen had the highest association with treatment benefit in adults, while regimens that included opioids were among those most often linked to acute adverse events.[1][11]

The regimen the ADA guideline names for pain is ibuprofen 400 to 600 mg plus acetaminophen 1,000 mg.[1] Follow the package directions or your clinician's instructions for timing and the daily maximum. Even a brief NSAID course can affect kidney function, blood pressure, and the stomach lining in susceptible people, and cumulative acetaminophen dosing may increase the risk of liver injury.[10] Ibuprofen's Drug Facts label says it is especially important not to use it at 20 weeks or later in pregnancy unless a doctor directs it.[13]

Do not prescribe opioids as a first-line agent for acute dental pain: they offer no advantage over non-opioid analgesics and contribute to opioid misuse in the community.[10] In a 2020 JADA analysis of US emergency department visits for dental conditions from 2012 through 2014, about 55% of patients filled an antibiotic prescription within 7 days, and 39.6% of Medicaid and 42.0% of commercially insured patients filled an opioid prescription; the authors describe this as potentially avoidable use.[14]

The Stewardship Problem

The 2024 Murphy and colleagues analysis in Infection Control and Hospital Epidemiology looked at 92,224 antibiotic prescriptions written by dentists at 88,539 Veterans Affairs dental visits in 2019. Only 17.5% of visits met a guideline-based definition of appropriate antibiotic use, and 64.3% met a broader literature-based definition. The authors concluded that more than half of antibiotics prescribed by dentists do not have guidelines supporting their use.[3]

Why this matters at the individual level, not just the population level: antibiotics prescribed when they are not clinically indicated may increase the risks of antimicrobial resistance, gut microbiome dysbiosis, and Clostridioides difficile infection.[10] The ADA panel judged the benefits of antibiotics for these dental conditions as likely negligible, and it warns that clindamycin substantially increases C. difficile risk even after a single dose. The most defensible prescribing pattern follows from that: a short course of amoxicillin or penicillin VK for a clearly indicated infection, prompt definitive dental treatment, and no antibiotic for the many presentations where the answer is dental treatment alone.[1]

Red Flags: When to Go to the ER

A tooth infection can become life-threatening: odontogenic infection is the most common source of deep neck infection, and untreated abscesses may get worse and lead to life-threatening complications.[4][6] Go to the emergency department, not a dentist and not a telehealth visit, if any of the following are present:

  • Breathing difficulty, which is an emergency: go to the emergency room or call 911 right away[5]
  • Difficulty swallowing, drooling, or unusual speech that sounds like a "hot potato" in the mouth[5]
  • Swelling of the tongue or floor of the mouth, or the tongue pushing out of the mouth (Ludwig angina)[5]
  • Neck swelling, redness, or pain[5]
  • A swollen area of the upper or lower jaw, which MedlinePlus calls a very serious symptom[6]
  • Eyelid swelling, especially with fever, a bulging eye, decreased or double vision, or pain when moving the eye (orbital cellulitis risk)[7]
  • Feeling very ill with fever as swelling spreads, which can signal sepsis[4][5]
Ludwig angina and orbital cellulitis are two dangerous complications of a spreading dental infection. Ludwig angina is an infection of the floor of the mouth under the tongue, often due to a bacterial infection of the teeth or jaw, and can cause airway blockage, sepsis, and septic shock.[5] Orbital cellulitis is an infection of the fat and muscles around the eye; prompt treatment of a dental infection may prevent it, and it is a medical emergency.[7] Either of the above red-flag features should trigger an ER visit that day.

Getting Antibiotics Online: When It Is Appropriate

A telehealth visit is appropriate for a dental infection when three things are true: a clear dental source, signs that meet the ADA 2019 threshold for antibiotics (such as fever or malaise from the dental infection), and a dental appointment already scheduled within 24 to 72 hours. The role of the telehealth clinician is to bridge the gap between symptom onset and definitive dental care.[1]

Telehealth is not appropriate when any red-flag feature is present, when there is no dental follow-up plan, when the patient has already had a failed antibiotic course, or when the presentation could plausibly be non-dental (sinusitis, salivary gland infection, TMJ disorder). In those cases the answer is in-person evaluation, not a virtual prescription.

Bridge to Dental Care

Antibiotics buy time. The tooth still has to be treated. The three definitive options are root canal therapy, extraction, or, in rare cases where drainage alone is sufficient (a small periodontal abscess that has already opened), incision and drainage with periodontal follow-up. None of these is a physician's job. The role of a medical clinician here is to control systemic infection and pain in the interval before dental care and to know when the situation exceeds that role.

If you do not have a dentist, options include community dental clinics, dental schools, federally qualified health centers (which often have dental services on a sliding fee scale), and university-affiliated urgent dental services. Waiting while a dental infection recurs carries real risk, because untreated abscesses can lead to life-threatening complications such as Ludwig angina.[5][6]
