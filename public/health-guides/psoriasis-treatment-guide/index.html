Psoriasis: Types, Topical Therapy, Biologics & Clinical Guidelines

Types, topical therapy, phototherapy, biologics, psoriatic arthritis screening, and comorbidity management, based on AAD-NPF joint guidelines.

Written by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: January 2026 · Updated: Jul 2026
16 min read

How is psoriasis treated according to current clinical guidelines?

Psoriasis treatment follows the AAD-NPF joint guidelines, which recommend topical corticosteroids for plaque psoriasis outside skin folds, note that off-label topical calcineurin inhibitors work on the face and genitals, and cover phototherapy, oral systemic drugs, and biologics for more extensive disease. FDA labels approve IL-17 and IL-23 biologics such as risankizumab for adults with moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis who are candidates for systemic therapy or phototherapy; in its label trials, 75% of patients reached PASI 90 at week 16. The guidelines recommend PASI for clinical trials rather than routine practice. Rheumatologists diagnosed psoriatic arthritis in 30% of dermatology patients with plaque psoriasis in a large study, so joint screening matters. A licensed clinician, through telehealth or in-person evaluation, can confirm the diagnosis and recommend next steps.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated July 14, 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • Plaque psoriasis is the most common form; guttate, inverse, pustular, and erythrodermic psoriasis each have different triggers and treatment considerations.
  • The AAD and NPF have published six joint guideline sections covering topical therapy and severity measures, systemic nonbiologic therapy, pediatric patients, phototherapy, biologics, and comorbidities.
  • Topicals come first for limited disease. Phototherapy, oral systemic drugs, and biologics are options for people with more extensive disease who are candidates for systemic therapy or phototherapy.
  • In the trials summarized in their FDA labels, IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors brought roughly 54% to 75% of patients to PASI 90 within 12 to 16 weeks.
  • In a large study of dermatology patients, rheumatologists diagnosed psoriatic arthritis in 30% of people with plaque psoriasis; brief questionnaires such as PEST help catch it early.
  • Psoriasis is linked with heart disease, metabolic syndrome, and depression, so care extends beyond the skin.

Psoriasis is a chronic, inflammatory, multisystem disease that affects up to 3.2% of the U.S. population.[1] It is not a purely cosmetic condition. The AAD-NPF guidelines list arthritis, heart disease, metabolic syndrome, smoking, excessive alcohol intake, inflammatory bowel disease, and psychiatric disorders among the comorbidities that come with it.[2]

Many patients arrive frustrated after years of treatment that did not work well. The field has changed. In the placebo-controlled trials described in their FDA labels, newer biologics brought most participants to PASI 90, meaning at least a 90% improvement in their psoriasis score, within 12 to 16 weeks.[3][4][5] The challenge is identifying who qualifies, matching them to the right therapy, and making sure nothing is missed in the bigger clinical picture.

This guide walks through what matters clinically: the types of psoriasis, how they're graded, what the AAD-NPF guidelines actually say, how to think about treatment selection, and why this condition demands attention to more than just what's on the surface.

Types of Psoriasis

Psoriasis is not a single disease. The term encompasses several distinct clinical subtypes, each with different presentations, triggers, and treatment implications. Knowing the subtype matters because treatment response and risk of complications vary meaningfully.

Plaque Psoriasis (Psoriasis Vulgaris)

This is the form most people picture when they hear "psoriasis," and it is the most common type. Plaque psoriasis presents as well-demarcated, raised, erythematous plaques covered by thick silvery-white scale. Classic locations include the elbows, knees, scalp, lower back, and sacral area. The Koebner phenomenon, in which new plaques appear at sites of skin trauma, is characteristic. Pruritus is common.

Guttate Psoriasis

Guttate psoriasis appears as small (less than 1 cm), drop-shaped, salmon-colored papules scattered across the trunk and extremities. It most commonly affects children and young adults and is strongly associated with preceding streptococcal pharyngitis. Many patients experience a single episode that resolves; some go on to develop chronic plaque psoriasis.

Inverse (Intertriginous) Psoriasis

Rather than the typical thick scale of plaque disease, inverse psoriasis presents as smooth, shiny, well-defined red plaques in skin folds such as the axillae, groin, under the breasts, and the perianal area. The moist environment reduces the scale but also makes the diagnosis easy to confuse with fungal or bacterial infections, contact dermatitis, or intertrigo. The AAD-NPF guidelines recommend topical corticosteroids for plaque psoriasis that does not involve intertriginous areas.[2] For skin folds, a 2024 review describes low-to-mid-potency corticosteroids for short courses (a maximum of 4 weeks) and topical calcineurin inhibitors, which can be used long term.[6]

Pustular Psoriasis

Pustular psoriasis is characterized by sterile (non-infectious) pustules on erythematous skin. It has two major forms: localized palmoplantar pustulosis, which is confined to the palms and soles, and generalized pustular psoriasis (GPP), which is a serious and potentially life-threatening variant with widespread pustules, fever, and systemic illness. GPP requires urgent medical attention. Spesolimab (Spevigo), an interleukin-36 receptor antagonist first approved in the U.S. in 2022, is indicated for GPP in adults and in children 12 and older who weigh at least 40 kg.[7]

Erythrodermic Psoriasis

Erythrodermic psoriasis covers most of the body surface with diffuse, fiery redness. It is rare but medically serious. The disrupted skin barrier can lead to temperature dysregulation, fluid and electrolyte imbalance, and secondary infection. This is a dermatologic emergency.

AAD-NPF Joint Guidelines: What They Say

The American Academy of Dermatology and the National Psoriasis Foundation have published six joint guideline sections: topical therapy and alternative medicine with severity measures, systemic nonbiologic therapies, pediatric patients, phototherapy, biologics, and awareness and attention to comorbidities.[2] They appear in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology, including the biologics section and the topical therapy section, which also covers severity assessment in adults.[1][8]

The Severity Framework

The guidelines describe several severity tools. Body Surface Area (BSA), the Physician Global Assessment (PGA), and BSA multiplied by PGA can be used in clinical practice. PASI (Psoriasis Area and Severity Index) is recommended for clinical trials rather than clinical practice. Quality-of-life tools such as the DLQI, PSI, and pruritus assessment add useful information.[2] Location matters as well: psoriasis of the scalp, nails, palms, soles, and genitals is considered more severe even when the lesions are not extensive.[6]

One of the more practically useful points from the guidelines: PASI is recommended for clinical trials, not routine practice. For day-to-day decisions, BSA and PGA, together with a quality-of-life instrument such as the DLQI (Dermatology Life Quality Index), are the tools meant for the clinic.[2]

PASI Scoring: The Basics

Even though PASI is most relevant to clinical trials, you will encounter it in any discussion of biologic therapy. Understanding the scoring helps patients and clinicians communicate about treatment goals.

PASI divides the body into four regions: head, upper extremities, trunk, and lower extremities, which account for 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40% of total body surface area. Each region is rated for erythema (redness), induration (thickness), and scaling, each on a scale of 0 (none) to 4 (very severe), and the extent of involvement in each region is graded from 0 to 6. The result is a total score from 0 to 72.[9]

Topical Therapy: The Corticosteroid Potency Ladder and Steroid-Sparing Options

For limited psoriasis, or as an add-on to systemic therapy, topical treatment is usually the first step. The AAD-NPF topical guideline reviews topical agents and severity assessment in adults.[8]

Corticosteroids: The Potency Ladder

The AAD-NPF guidelines recommend topical corticosteroids for plaque psoriasis that does not involve intertriginous areas.[2] They work by suppressing local inflammation. Potency is the key variable in selection, and using the wrong potency for a given body site is a common error that leads to either treatment failure or skin damage.

The guidelines say topical corticosteroid use beyond 12 weeks can be considered only under careful physician supervision.[2] Labels for super-high-potency products such as clobetasol warn that absorption through the skin can cause reversible suppression of the body's own steroid (HPA axis) production.[13]

Vitamin D Analogues: Calcipotriene

Calcipotriene (calcipotriol) is a synthetic vitamin D analogue that is often combined with betamethasone dipropionate. In the 4-week trial described in the Enstilar foam label, more patients reached clear or almost clear skin with the combination (45.0%) than with betamethasone dipropionate alone (30.7%) or calcipotriene alone (14.9%).[14] The label limits the foam to once daily for up to 4 weeks and no more than 60 grams every 4 days, advises avoiding the face, groin, and axillae, and notes that high calcium levels in blood and urine have been observed.[14]

Tazarotene

Tazarotene is a topical retinoid. The AAD-NPF guidelines describe topical tazarotene used in combination with topical corticosteroids for skin or nail psoriasis.[2] It can cause excessive irritation in sensitive skin. The Tazorac gel label contraindicates it during pregnancy, calls for a negative pregnancy test within 2 weeks before starting, and advises effective contraception during treatment.[15]

Calcineurin Inhibitors for Special Sites

Tacrolimus and pimecrolimus are not FDA-approved for psoriasis, but the AAD-NPF guidelines state that psoriasis of the face and genitalia responds to off-label topical calcineurin inhibitors.[2] A 2024 review notes that they suit the thinner skin of skin folds and can be used as long-term therapy, where steroid-related thinning is a concern.[6]

Phototherapy: Narrowband UVB and Excimer Laser

Phototherapy sits between topical and systemic therapy in the treatment algorithm. Narrowband UVB is an effective treatment for people whose psoriasis has not responded to topical regimens or who want to avoid systemic treatment.[16]

Narrowband UVB (NB-UVB)

Narrowband UVB (NB-UVB) is widely used for psoriasis. In a 2025 meta-analysis of patients with skin of color, 70.5% reached PASI 75, with treatment courses ranging from twice weekly for 8 weeks to three times weekly for 12 weeks; NB-UVB produced complete clearance more often than broadband UVB.[16]

Office-based phototherapy is cost-effective but difficult to access. In the 2024 LITE randomized clinical trial of 783 patients, a 12-week course of home NB-UVB was noninferior to office-based NB-UVB for clear or almost clear skin and for quality of life, overall and across all skin phototypes. Home treatment brought better adherence and lower indirect costs, along with more episodes of persistent redness.[17] This matters for patients who live far from a phototherapy center.

The AAD-NPF phototherapy guideline reviews NB-UVB and other light therapies used alone or together with topical or systemic psoriasis treatments.[18]

Excimer Laser

The excimer laser delivers targeted UVB to individual plaques without exposing uninvolved skin, which makes it useful for localized plaques that have not responded to topicals. The AAD-NPF phototherapy guideline covers targeted UVB treatments such as the excimer laser.[18] A 2024 review lists the 308-nanometer excimer laser among effective options for scalp psoriasis.[6]

Systemic Therapy: Methotrexate, Cyclosporine, and Apremilast

When topicals and phototherapy are insufficient, or when phototherapy is impractical, oral systemic agents become the next consideration. The AAD-NPF systemic nonbiologic guideline focuses on methotrexate, cyclosporine, and acitretin, and also discusses newer therapies including apremilast and tofacitinib.[19]

Methotrexate

Methotrexate (MTX) is FDA-approved for adults with severe psoriasis. The label dose is 10 to 25 mg by mouth once weekly until an adequate response, never more than 30 mg per week, with folic acid or folinic acid to reduce the risk of side effects.[20] Its boxed warning covers embryo-fetal toxicity including fetal death, severe hypersensitivity reactions, serious adverse reactions including death, and deaths from accidentally taking it once daily instead of once weekly.[20]

The major long-term concern is liver toxicity. The label warns that methotrexate can cause fibrosis and cirrhosis, that in psoriasis these may occur without symptoms or abnormal liver tests, and that the risk rises with heavy alcohol use and with cumulative dose.[20] The AAD-NPF systemic guideline revised its hepatotoxicity monitoring guidance and recommends newer non-invasive tests for liver fibrosis.[2]

Cyclosporine

Cyclosporine (Neoral) is approved for nonimmunocompromised adults with severe, recalcitrant plaque psoriasis that has not responded to at least one systemic therapy, or when other systemic therapies cannot be used. The label starts at 2.5 mg/kg/day and caps the dose at 4.0 mg/kg/day.[21] Its boxed warning covers high blood pressure and kidney toxicity, and a higher risk of skin cancers in psoriasis patients previously treated with PUVA and, to a lesser extent, methotrexate. Blood pressure, serum creatinine, and BUN are checked every 2 weeks during the first 3 months, and most patients relapse when treatment stops.[21]

Apremilast

Apremilast (Otezla) is an oral phosphodiesterase-4 (PDE4) inhibitor approved for adults with plaque psoriasis who are candidates for phototherapy or systemic therapy. After a short starting titration, the maintenance dose is 30 mg twice daily (or 75 mg once daily for the extended-release form). The label carries no boxed warning.[22] Efficacy is more modest than with biologics: in the two 16-week label trials, 33.1% and 28.8% of patients reached PASI 75, compared with 5.3% and 5.8% on placebo.[22] The label warns about diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting, depression, and weight decrease; the most common side effects are diarrhea, nausea, upper respiratory tract infection, and headache.[22]

Deucravacitinib (Sotyktu), an oral tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2) inhibitor, is approved for adults with moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis who are candidates for systemic therapy or phototherapy, at 6 mg once daily. In its two label trials, 58% and 53% of patients reached PASI 75 at week 16, compared with 13% and 9% on placebo. Its label has no boxed warning and does not recommend combining it with other potent immunosuppressants.[23]

Biologics: TNF-Alpha, IL-17, and IL-23 Inhibitors

Biologics are injectable or infused agents that target specific proteins in the inflammatory cascade driving psoriasis. The FDA labels for IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors such as secukinumab and risankizumab cover moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis in patients who are candidates for systemic therapy or phototherapy; they do not require that other systemic drugs have failed first.[11][5] The AAD-NPF biologics guidance covers biologics used alone or together with other psoriasis therapies.[2]

TNF-Alpha Inhibitors

The TNF-alpha inhibitors include etanercept, adalimumab, infliximab, and certolizumab pegol. They block tumor necrosis factor-alpha, a pro-inflammatory cytokine. In the phase III REVEAL trial, 71% of patients on adalimumab reached PASI 75 at week 16, compared with 7% on placebo.[10] Adalimumab's label dose for plaque psoriasis is 80 mg once, then 40 mg every other week starting one week later. Its boxed warning covers serious infections and malignancy, and patients are tested for latent tuberculosis before and during therapy. The label also warns about heart failure and neurologic reactions, including demyelinating disease.[24]

IL-17 Inhibitors

The IL-17 pathway is central to psoriatic inflammation. IL-17A, produced largely by Th17 cells, acts on keratinocytes to drive the rapid skin cell turnover and inflammation that characterize plaque psoriasis.

Secukinumab (Cosentyx) is an IL-17A inhibitor.[25] In its two pivotal label trials, 59% and 54% of patients on 300 mg reached PASI 90 at week 12, compared with 1% and 2% on placebo. It is given as 300 mg at weeks 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4, then every 4 weeks.[11] Ixekizumab (Taltz) also binds IL-17A; in three label trials, 68% to 71% of patients reached PASI 90 at week 12. It starts at 160 mg, then 80 mg every 2 weeks through week 12, then 80 mg every 4 weeks.[3] Brodalumab (Siliq) binds the IL-17 receptor A instead of the cytokine. It carries a boxed warning for suicidal ideation and behavior, including completed suicides, and is available only through a restricted REMS program.[26] For nail psoriasis, a 2024 review reports that IL-17 inhibitors generally outperform the IL-12/23 inhibitor ustekinumab.[6]

IL-23 Inhibitors

IL-23 sits upstream of IL-17 and drives the differentiation and proliferation of Th17 cells. The p19 subunit-specific IL-23 inhibitors are guselkumab (Tremfya), risankizumab (Skyrizi), and tildrakizumab (Ilumya).[4][25][27]

In risankizumab's two label trials, 75% of patients reached PASI 90 at week 16 and 36% to 51% reached PASI 100, compared with 5% or less on placebo.[5] Guselkumab brought 70% to 73% of patients to PASI 90 at week 16 in its label trials.[4] In a British registry cohort of more than 19,000 treatment courses, guselkumab and risankizumab had the most favourable drug survival for effectiveness, meaning patients tended to stay on them without stopping for loss of effect.[25] Dosing intervals are long: risankizumab 150 mg at weeks 0 and 4, then every 12 weeks;[5] guselkumab 100 mg at weeks 0 and 4, then every 8 weeks;[4] and tildrakizumab 100 mg at weeks 0 and 4, then every 12 weeks.[27]

Psoriatic Arthritis Screening

In a study of 949 patients with plaque psoriasis seen in dermatology centers in Europe and North America, rheumatologists diagnosed psoriatic arthritis (PsA) in 30%, and 41% of those cases had not been diagnosed before.[29] Undiagnosed PsA, or even a 6-month delay in diagnosis, may lead to physical disability and joint erosion.[30] This is why regular joint screening during psoriasis care matters clinically, not just as a checkbox.

The AAD-NPF guidelines address psoriatic arthritis among the major comorbid conditions and highlight the dermatologist's role in identifying comorbidities by screening for them or alerting the patient's primary care physician.[2] A definitive PsA diagnosis comes from a rheumatologist.[30]

What to Ask

In my clinical practice, joint assessment starts with five direct questions:

  • Do you have pain, stiffness, or swelling in any joints?
  • Is the stiffness worse in the morning, and does it last more than 30 minutes?
  • Do you have pain at the heel, the bottom of the foot, or where tendons attach to bone?
  • Has anyone ever told you that your fingers or toes look swollen like "sausages" (dactylitis)?
  • Do you have nail pitting or separation of the nail from the nail bed?

Validated Screening Tools

Two validated questionnaires are widely used in non-rheumatology settings:

The PEST (Psoriasis Epidemiology Screening Tool) is five yes-or-no questions. Each yes scores 1 point, and a score of 3 or more indicates risk of PsA and that a rheumatology referral may be needed.[30] In the study that developed it, sensitivity was 0.92 and specificity 0.78.[31] It is brief enough for routine clinic use and, like ToPAS, includes a visual aid so patients can mark painful joints.[30]

The ToPAS (Toronto Psoriatic Arthritis Screen) uses a simplified score based on skin, joint, and nail domains, with an overall sensitivity of 86.8% and specificity of 93.1% at a single cut point.[32]

When PsA is suspected, the workup typically includes inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP), rheumatoid factor (to distinguish it from rheumatoid arthritis, though PsA is usually seronegative), and plain radiographs of affected joints. Observational studies have shown better outcomes in patients with PsA who are treated soon after diagnosis.[30]

Special Site Management: Scalp, Nail, and Genital Psoriasis

These three locations require distinct approaches. Standard topical formulations designed for body plaques are often poorly tolerated or impractical at these sites, and the disease can persist despite adequate clearance elsewhere.

Scalp Psoriasis

Scalp involvement occurs in 45% to 56% of people with psoriasis and can cause pain, itching, bleeding, and embarrassment.[6] The hair impedes topical application, so vehicle matters as much as the active ingredient. A 2024 review advises using topical steroids on the scalp as a foam, gel, solution, shampoo, or spray, notes that steroids combined with vitamin D analogues have shown long-term results, and describes keratolytic and tar-based shampoos as add-on options that many patients find hard to keep using.[6]

For scalp psoriasis with moderate-to-severe psoriasis elsewhere on the body, the same review lists systemic agents as first-line. It finds that guselkumab, infliximab, ixekizumab, and brodalumab appear to have the highest scalp clearance across studies, while noting the lack of head-to-head trials. In one large observational study, patients with scalp psoriasis had a 10% to 20% higher response rate at week 12 with ixekizumab than with the comparison biologics.[6]

Nail Psoriasis

Nail involvement affects 10% to 55% of people with psoriasis, and nail psoriasis is frequently associated with psoriatic arthritis.[6] Nail disease shows up as pitting (small depressions in the nail surface), onycholysis (separation of the nail plate from the nail bed), oil-drop spots (yellow-brown discoloration under the nail), and subungual hyperkeratosis (thickened nail bed). These changes are not merely cosmetic; they can be painful and functionally limiting.

For mild nail disease, the 2024 review lists topical calcipotriol with a high-potency topical corticosteroid as first-line, with topical tacrolimus and tazarotene as second-line. Intralesional triamcinolone injections into the nail folds are another second-line option. For moderate-to-severe nail psoriasis, biologics are typically first-line. Apremilast can be used as second-line therapy; in its ESTEEM trials, up to 55.4% of patients reached a 50% improvement in nail severity (NAPSI 50) at week 32. Brodalumab has shown substantial nail improvement in phase III trials.[6]

Genital Psoriasis

Genital involvement is the only skin manifestation of psoriasis in 2% to 5% of patients. It is often underdiagnosed by both clinicians and patients, and it has a significant negative impact on quality of life, psychosexual well-being, and sexual health.[6] Because patients rarely volunteer it, it is worth asking about directly.

Genital skin is thinner and frequently occluded, so it absorbs topical treatments more and is prone to atrophy, telangiectasia, and striae. The 2024 review describes low-potency topical steroids for short courses of 2 to 4 weeks, vitamin D analogues combined with steroids, and topical calcineurin inhibitors. It also cites a randomized, vehicle-controlled trial showing crisaborole 2% ointment (approved for atopic dermatitis) to be effective and safe for intertriginous, genital, and facial psoriasis.[6] For systemic therapy, ixekizumab improved genital psoriasis in a phase III trial: at week 12, 73% of patients reached clear or minimal genital disease, compared with 8% on placebo.[3] Asking about genital involvement routinely, rather than waiting for patients to raise it, is part of complete psoriasis care.

Comorbidities: Cardiovascular Risk, Metabolic Syndrome, and Depression

The AAD-NPF guidelines describe psoriasis as a "chronic, multisystem, inflammatory disease" and list arthritis, heart disease, metabolic syndrome, smoking, excessive alcohol intake, inflammatory bowel disease, and psychiatric disorders among the comorbidities to address.[2] This matters clinically because psoriasis care is not only skin care.

Cardiovascular Risk

Cardiovascular disease is more common in people with psoriasis than in healthy individuals, the severity of skin disease correlates with subclinical atherosclerosis, and psoriasis is an independent risk factor for myocardial infarction.[33] Inflammatory cytokines and immune dysregulation are thought to drive atherosclerosis and endothelial dysfunction in psoriatic disease. A 2024 review also reports that standard risk calculators such as the Framingham Risk Score can underestimate cardiovascular risk, and that the underestimation is worse with chronic inflammatory conditions such as psoriatic arthritis.[33]

In practice, dermatologists can help by screening for cardiovascular risk factors or alerting the patient's primary care physician, as the AAD-NPF guidelines describe.[2]

Metabolic Syndrome

Metabolic syndrome, the cluster of obesity, high blood pressure, abnormal lipids, and insulin resistance, is one of the comorbidities the AAD-NPF guidelines ask clinicians to address in psoriasis.[2] Weight, blood pressure, lipids, and blood sugar are worth reviewing with patients and their primary care physicians.

Depression and Mental Health

In a 2023 meta-analysis of 56 studies, the prevalence of depression and anxiety in adults with psoriasis was 20% and 21%.[34] In a systematic review, depression was generally more common in people with more severe psoriasis.[35]

The psychological burden extends to suicidal ideation, with prevalence ranging from 0.4% to 21.0% across studies. The review's authors conclude that psoriasis care should incorporate psychological interventions.[35] Routine mental health screening with a validated tool (PHQ-2 or PHQ-9), and referral to behavioral health without stigma when indicated, is a practical way to do that.

Telehealth in Psoriasis: Monitoring, Refills, and What Works Remotely

Psoriasis follow-up adapts well to telehealth. In a 24-week randomized trial of 64 patients published in the Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology, asynchronous online follow-up with a dermatologist produced the same improvement in PASI as in-office care (a mean improvement of 3.4 points in both groups), with no significant differences in physician global assessment or DLQI.[36] A 2025 study of a remote monitoring program found significant quality-of-life improvement when specialists managed care but not when primary care managed it alone; collaboration between primary care physicians and specialists was associated with better results.[37]

What telehealth handles well in psoriasis:

  • Treatment response monitoring: photo-based assessment of plaque clearance, BSA estimation, and quality-of-life scoring through secure video or asynchronous photo submission.
  • Medication refill management: patients on stable topical regimens, phototherapy maintenance, or established biologics can avoid unnecessary in-office visits.
  • Lab review: for patients on methotrexate or cyclosporine, laboratory and blood pressure results can be reviewed and dose adjustments communicated remotely.
  • Comorbidity check-ins: PEST screening, depression screening, and cardiovascular risk discussion can all be conducted by video visit.
  • Flare triage: patients who know their disease well can often describe flare patterns accurately enough for remote decisions, with in-person visits reserved for new or diagnostically uncertain presentations.

What requires in-person evaluation: suspected new diagnosis or diagnostic uncertainty, skin biopsy, joint examination for PsA assessment, initiation of biologic therapy (which typically requires in-person work-up and education), and any clinical picture suggesting erythrodermic or pustular psoriasis.

For established patients, remote follow-up can maintain continuity between in-person visits, so that gaps in care do not let psoriasis rebound unnoticed.

A new or uncertain diagnosis, joint examination, and suspected erythrodermic or pustular psoriasis need in-person care.

These "special sites" involve thinner or more sensitive skin, hair that gets in the way, or a nail plate that blocks topicals. Scalp psoriasis is treated with foams, gels, solutions, shampoos, or sprays rather than creams. Mild nail psoriasis is treated first with topical calcipotriol plus a strong corticosteroid, and moderate-to-severe nail disease typically with biologics. Genital psoriasis calls for gentle options such as short courses of low-potency steroids or topical calcineurin inhibitors, because the skin there thins easily. Ixekizumab has shown benefit for genital psoriasis in a phase III trial.
