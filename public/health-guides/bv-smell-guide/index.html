BV Smell

A physician's evidence-based guide to why bacterial vaginosis smells fishy, the chemistry behind the odor, and how to actually make it stop.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: September 8, 2026
13 min read

Why does bacterial vaginosis cause a fishy smell?

The fishy odor of bacterial vaginosis comes from amines. In BV, the normal lactobacilli are replaced by an overgrowth of anaerobic bacteria, and vaginal fluid contains amines such as putrescine, cadaverine and trimethylamine. Trimethylamine, which bacteria make from trimethylamine oxide, is the compound linked to the fishy smell, and adding an alkali releases it. That is the basis of the whiff test, one of the four Amsel criteria in the CDC 2021 STI guidelines. Semen neutralizes vaginal acidity, which helps explain why CDC describes the odor as strong especially after sex. The odor is not a hygiene failure, and douching does not fix it: CDC says no data support douching for treatment or symptom relief and that it might increase the risk of relapse. The odor resolves when BV itself is treated with a prescription antibiotic regimen, such as oral metronidazole, metronidazole gel or clindamycin cream, not with deodorizing products.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated September 8, 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • The fishy odor of BV is linked to amines. Vaginal fluid in BV contains putrescine and cadaverine, thought to be made by the anaerobic bacteria that overgrow, and trimethylamine, the compound behind the fishy smell.[10][11]
  • CDC lists a strong fish-like odor, especially after sex, as a BV symptom. Semen neutralizes vaginal acidity, and alkaline conditions release trimethylamine, which is the likely explanation for that pattern.[15][13][11]
  • The whiff test is a formal diagnostic step: a fishy odor of discharge before or after adding 10% potassium hydroxide (KOH) is one of the four Amsel criteria, and at least three are needed for the diagnosis.[1]
  • Vaginal odor is not a hygiene failure, and douching does not fix it. CDC says no data support douching for treatment or symptom relief and that it might increase the risk of relapse.[1]
  • Lactobacilli keep a healthy vagina acidic, at a pH of 3.6 to 4.5 in most women. In BV, lactobacilli are lost, other bacteria overgrow, and the pH rises above 4.5.[7]
  • The odor clears when BV itself is treated. CDC's recommended regimens are oral metronidazole, metronidazole gel or clindamycin cream, with secnidazole, tinidazole and other clindamycin forms as alternatives; deodorizing washes and yeast treatments are not on the list.[1]
  • In a national U.S. survey, 84 percent of women with BV did not report symptoms, so a fishy odor is a useful clue when present, but its absence does not rule BV out.[6]

Vaginal odor is one of the most distressing and least discussed symptoms in primary care, and when it has a fishy quality, it is a strong pointer toward bacterial vaginosis.[15] This guide explains the chemistry behind the smell, why it tends to be stronger at certain times, how clinicians test for it, and why washing or douching it away does not work.

Where the Odor Comes From

The fishy smell is not the bacteria themselves. It is linked to amines, small nitrogen containing molecules found in vaginal fluid in BV. A 1979 study of vaginal washings identified seven amines, with putrescine and cadaverine the most abundant, and suggested they may be partly responsible for the fishy odor; the authors proposed that the anaerobic bacteria increased in BV produce them.[10] Later work traced the fishy odor itself to trimethylamine, which bacteria make from trimethylamine oxide in vaginal fluid.[11] In a healthy vagina, lactobacilli keep the environment acidic, at a pH of 3.6 to 4.5 in most women. When lactobacilli are lost and other bacteria overgrow, the pH rises above 4.5.[7] In one small study of 37 women, putrescine, cadaverine and tyramine were higher in vaginal communities depleted of lactobacilli.[12]

This is why the odor tracks the infection and not the surface. The amines are a product of the bacterial imbalance, which is why surface remedies do not reach the cause.

BV is not a single germ. CDC describes it as the replacement of the normal hydrogen peroxide and lactic acid producing lactobacilli with high concentrations of anaerobic bacteria, with a polymicrobial biofilm on vaginal cells.[1] Gardnerella vaginalis and Prevotella bivia are among the BV-associated bacteria,[7] and a molecular study found 9 to 17 bacterial species per sample in women with BV, compared with 1 to 6, mostly lactobacilli, in women without it.[3] Gardnerella on its own does not mean BV: in the study that produced the Amsel criteria, it was isolated from 51.3 percent of all the women tested.[2] BV is also the most common cause of vaginal discharge worldwide, so the odor is rarely an isolated symptom.[1]

What BV Smells Like, In Practice

CDC describes the BV odor as a strong fish-like odor, especially after sex, often with a thin white or gray discharge.[15] A foul smell can point to other causes: trichomoniasis can cause a malodorous discharge,[1] and a forgotten tampon or other object in the vagina can cause foul-smelling discharge,[16] so a genuinely foul odor should broaden the list beyond BV.

It is also worth separating the fishy odor of BV from a yeast infection. A German national guideline lists odorless discharge among the typical yeast symptoms, along with itching, burning and redness,[8] and CDC notes that none of the yeast symptoms is specific.[1] When odor is the main complaint and itching is minimal, BV is the better working hypothesis, but testing settles it.

Odor vs a Normal Vaginal Scent

Before chasing an odor, it is worth knowing that normal is not fixed. In healthy women the vaginal bacteria are dominated by lactobacilli, but their levels can change dramatically over a month; in one daily-sampling study they fell with the onset of menstruation while Gardnerella rose.[14] Normal vaginal pH also differs between women: in a study of 396 women without symptoms, average pH ranged from 4.2 to 5.0 across ethnic groups.[4] So a change from your own usual pattern matters more than a fixed standard.

The line that matters is the fishy quality and its timing. A new fishy odor that is especially noticeable after sex, especially with thin white or gray discharge, is the classic BV pattern and is worth confirming.[15] An exam and a swab settle it, because CDC notes that a history alone is not enough for an accurate diagnosis of vaginitis and can lead to the wrong medication.[1]

The Whiff Test: How Clinicians Confirm the Odor

The odor is not just a patient report; it is part of the formal diagnostic criteria. In the Amsel criteria, one of the four findings is a fishy odor of vaginal discharge before or after adding 10% potassium hydroxide (KOH), the whiff test.[1] Adding an alkali releases trimethylamine from the fluid, which is why the test works.[11] CDC also notes that an amine odor right after KOH suggests BV or trichomoniasis, so a positive whiff test is not specific to BV.[1]

The whiff test is only one of the four Amsel criteria. It joins a thin, homogeneous discharge that coats the vaginal walls, a pH above 4.5, and clue cells on microscopy, and at least three of the four are needed for a clinical diagnosis, so a clinician is never relying on the smell alone.[1] The criteria trace back to a 1983 study of 397 university students that proposed practical diagnostic criteria for clinic use.[2]

Why the Odor Worsens After Sex and During Menstruation

If the odor flares at predictable moments, chemistry is a likely part of the reason. CDC lists a strong fish-like odor, especially after sex, among BV symptoms.[15] Semen neutralizes vaginal acidity: in one study, semen components in vaginal fluid correlated strongly with a higher pH,[13] and lactobacilli then work to reacidify the vagina after sex.[7] Because adding an alkali is what releases trimethylamine in the whiff test,[11] a rise in pH after sex is a plausible explanation for the stronger smell, although none of the studies cited here measured odor directly after sex. Menstruation matters too. CDC notes that BV prevalence increases during menses,[1] and in a daily-sampling study, lactobacilli fell with the onset of menstruation while Gardnerella rose.[14]

The same pH logic is why clinicians check vaginal pH with pH paper. An elevated pH, above 4.5, is common with BV or trichomoniasis, while a yeast infection is associated with a normal pH below 4.5. pH testing is not highly specific, so it is one piece of information, not a diagnosis.[1]

When Odor Is Not BV

Not every vaginal odor is BV, and a few distinctions matter. A yeast infection typically causes an odorless discharge.[8] Trichomoniasis, a sexually transmitted infection, can cause a vaginal discharge that is diffuse, malodorous or yellow-green, with or without vulvar irritation, and CDC recommends testing for it in women seeking care for vaginal discharge.[1]

A forgotten tampon or other object in the vagina can cause bleeding or blood-stained or foul-smelling discharge, and prompt removal and care prevent complications.[16] When the odor is foul rather than fishy, or comes with abnormal bleeding, pain during sex or pelvic pain, evaluation rather than assumption is the right move: CDC notes that pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) often goes unrecognized because signs such as abnormal bleeding, pain with sex and vaginal discharge are mild or nonspecific.[1]

Why Washing and Douching Do Not Fix It

The most common mistake with BV odor is to treat it as a hygiene problem, and the most common tool, douching, does not help. CDC says douching might increase the risk for relapse and that no data support douching for treatment or symptom relief.[1] CDC also lists douching among the things that can upset the normal balance of vaginal bacteria,[15] and in a national U.S. survey, BV was more common with increasing douching frequency.[6] Scented washes, wipes and deodorizing sprays are not BV treatments, and CDC notes that vulvovaginal inflammation without an infection can have chemical or allergic causes.[1]

The fix is not more cleaning; it is treating the imbalance that produces the odor. Restoring that balance, not masking its loss, is what eliminates the smell.

The Real-World Weight of Vaginal Odor

The clinical story understates how much this symptom costs people. In interviews with 35 women living with recurrent BV, women commonly said the symptoms made them feel embarrassed, ashamed and 'dirty', and very concerned that others might detect the odor and discharge. The biggest impact was on self-esteem and sex lives, with women regularly avoiding sex, particularly oral sex.[9] Many people also try over-the-counter products before or alongside a clinician visit, which CDC notes is common for vaginal symptoms.[1]

Naming it helps. The fishy odor is a predictable chemical product of a very common bacterial imbalance, not a reflection of how clean someone is, and CDC notes that treating BV relieves its symptoms.[1] If the odor is affecting your confidence, your relationship, or your willingness to be intimate, that is a legitimate and common reason to seek care, and it is one a good clinician will take seriously rather than minimize.

Treatment for BV Odor

Because the odor is a byproduct of the bacterial overgrowth, the treatment is a BV antibiotic regimen. CDC's recommended regimens are metronidazole 500 mg by mouth twice daily for 7 days, metronidazole 0.75% gel one full applicator (5 g) vaginally once daily for 5 days, or clindamycin 2% cream one full applicator (5 g) vaginally at bedtime for 7 days.[1] Alternatives include secnidazole 2 g oral granules as a single dose, tinidazole, oral clindamycin and clindamycin ovules; CDC lists secnidazole as an alternative because of its higher cost and lack of long-term outcomes compared with the recommended treatments.[1] BV bacteria can fall quickly once treatment starts: in one small study they dropped below detection 1 to 5 days after starting vaginal metronidazole.[14] Even so, CDC advises taking all the medicine prescribed, even if symptoms go away.[15]

If the odor comes back within weeks or months, that is the recurrent BV pattern. CDC notes that retreatment with the same regimen is acceptable after a first recurrence and describes longer suppressive options for multiple recurrences.[1] How to get rid of BV covers the recurrence options in full.

Lowering the Odds the Odor Returns

Because the odor follows the infection, anything that reduces recurrence reduces the return of the smell, but the prevention evidence is limited. CDC says scientists do not completely understand how BV spreads or how best to prevent it, and lists not douching and using condoms the right way every time as basic steps that may help lower the risk.[15] Lack of condom use and douching are both associated with BV in CDC's guideline.[1] Recurrence is common even after a full course: in one Australian cohort treated with 7 days of oral metronidazole, 58 percent had BV again within 12 months.[5]

For women with multiple recurrences, CDC describes twice-weekly metronidazole gel for more than 3 months, which reduces recurrences although the benefit does not persist after stopping, and, based on limited data, an oral nitroimidazole for 7 days followed by vaginal boric acid 600 mg daily for 21 days and then twice-weekly metronidazole gel for 4 to 6 months. CDC does not recommend routine treatment of sex partners.[1] If the odor does come back, it is a signal that the overgrowth may be back too, and CDC advises returning for evaluation when symptoms recur.[1]

When to See a Clinician

Most BV odor is not an emergency, but some accompanying signs call for a prompt visit. Seek care for odor with pelvic or lower abdominal pain, pain during sex, abnormal bleeding or fever, or for a foul rather than fishy smell. CDC notes that PID is often missed because its signs can be mild, treats pelvic or lower abdominal pain in at-risk women as a reason to consider PID, and lists a temperature above 101°F among the findings that support the diagnosis.[1] A forgotten tampon or other object can also cause foul-smelling discharge.[16] In pregnancy, CDC recommends treating all symptomatic BV because it has been linked with preterm birth and other complications, so symptoms during pregnancy are a reason to see a clinician rather than self-treat.[1] And if a completed antibiotic course did not clear the odor, the diagnosis is worth checking again: CDC recommends testing for trichomoniasis in women seeking care for vaginal discharge, and an amine odor on testing can come from trichomoniasis as well as BV.[1]
