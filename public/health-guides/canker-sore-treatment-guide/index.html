Canker Sore Treatment: What Actually Works (2026 Evidence-Based Guide)

Most canker sores heal on their own in 1 to 2 weeks. The right topical steroid, rinse, and toothpaste can cut pain and shorten that window, and a small set of red flags tell you when a mouth ulcer needs a clinician.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD · Last reviewed September 22, 2026 · 14 min read

What is the best treatment for a canker sore in 2026?

For most canker sores, a topical corticosteroid is the first-line treatment: it reduces pain and shortens healing time, per the StatPearls review of recurrent aphthous stomatitis and the 2026 umbrella review by Al-Aizari and colleagues. Generic triamcinolone acetonide 0.1% dental paste is labeled to be pressed onto the sore at bedtime and, if needed, two or three times a day after meals. Over-the-counter numbing gels ease pain, and in a systematic review of small crossover trials, a sodium lauryl sulfate-free toothpaste reduced the number, duration and pain of ulcers. Most canker sores heal on their own in 1 to 2 weeks. See a licensed clinician if an ulcer is larger than 1 cm, has lasted more than two weeks, keeps coming back, or comes with fever, weight loss, diarrhea, eye or genital ulcers, or a rash.

🗃 Key Takeaways

  • Canker sores (aphthous ulcers) affect up to 25% of people worldwide; minor ulcers heal within two weeks without scarring[1].
  • They are not contagious. Cold sores are a herpes simplex (HSV-1) infection that forms outside the mouth around the lips; canker sores form inside the mouth[17].
  • Topical corticosteroids (such as triamcinolone acetonide 0.1% dental paste, or fluocinonide or clobetasol 0.05% ointment mixed with Orabase) are the first-line treatment and reduce pain and healing time[1][10][19].
  • Amlexanox 5% oral paste sped healing and pain relief in randomized trials but is no longer available in the U.S.[8][18].
  • Sodium lauryl sulfate-free toothpaste reduced ulcer number, duration, episodes, and pain in a systematic review of small crossover trials, though one trial saw shorter, less painful ulcers without fewer episodes[7][12].
  • Sublingual vitamin B12 1000 mcg daily reduced outbreak duration, ulcer number, and pain by months 5 and 6 of a placebo-controlled RCT, regardless of baseline B12 levels[6].
  • Ulcers that are larger than 1 cm, last more than 2 weeks, start suddenly in adulthood, or come with lesions elsewhere on the body need evaluation for causes such as Behcet's, IBD, celiac disease, or vitamin deficiency[1][2][15][16].

What Is a Canker Sore (Aphthous Stomatitis)?

A canker sore, known clinically as an aphthous ulcer or recurrent aphthous stomatitis, is a shallow, painful ulcer of the mouth lining. It forms mainly on the soft, non-keratinized lining, especially the inner cheek and inner lip, and also on the tongue[1][17]. It has a distinctive look: round or oval, usually under 1 cm across, with a grayish-white or yellow base and a red border[15][17].

Canker sores are one of the most common oral conditions. Recurrent aphthous stomatitis affects up to 25 percent of people worldwide and usually first appears in childhood or adolescence[1]. It is more common between ages 10 and 40 and in women[15], and less common in tobacco smokers[1].

Two things worth stating up front. First, canker sores are not contagious and not a herpes infection[1][17]. They cannot be passed to a partner, spread by cup-sharing, or caught from a stranger. Second, they are almost always self-limited: they usually go away on their own in 1 to 2 weeks[17].

Minor, Major, and Herpetiform: Sizing Up an Ulcer

Aphthous ulcers are grouped into three clinical patterns based on the size, number, and duration of the ulcers, and the pattern helps predict healing time and how much treatment you need[1][15].

The everyday sore inside your cheek is a minor aphthous ulcer. Herpetiform ulcers are named for their resemblance to herpes, but they have no connection to herpes viruses: up to 100 tiny ulcers can appear at once and may merge[1].

Canker Sore vs Cold Sore vs Other Look-Alikes

The most common source of confusion is the cold sore. They are entirely different problems with different biology, different locations, and different treatments. A canker sore forms inside the mouth and is not contagious. A cold sore (fever blister) is a painful, fluid-filled sore outside the mouth around the lips, is caused by herpes simplex virus type 1, and is very contagious[17]. If you need a walkthrough of that distinction, see the TeleDirectMD guides on cold sore vs pimple and cold sores (HSV-1).

Two other look-alikes are worth naming. A traumatic ulcer can follow an accidental cheek or lip bite[17]. Oral cancer can show up as a sore, lump, or thick patch, or a white or red patch, in the mouth; NIDCR advises seeing a dentist or doctor if any of these lasts more than two weeks[16].

What Actually Causes Canker Sores

There is no single known cause, and the precise cause is still unclear[5]. The leading model is an immune reaction in the lining of the mouth, in which an unknown trigger leads to activation of T lymphocytes, on a background of individual susceptibility; up to 46% of patients report a family history[1][15]. Four families of triggers come up most often.

Local trauma. Biting your cheek, brushing hard, a chip on a tooth, a sharp piece of food, or a rough denture edge sets up an ulcer at the injury site. Local trauma is a recognized trigger, which is why an ulcer on the inside of the cheek or lip often follows a bite you can remember[15][17].

Toothpaste with sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS). SLS is a foaming detergent used in many toothpastes. In a 90-person double-blind crossover trial, an SLS-free toothpaste did not reduce the number of ulcers or episodes, but ulcers healed faster and hurt less[12]. A 2019 systematic review of four crossover trials (124 participants) found SLS-free toothpaste reduced the number of ulcers, ulcer duration, number of episodes, and pain, while calling for better-designed trials[7].

Hematinic deficiencies. Iron, folate (vitamin B9), and vitamin B12 are the classic three; hematinic deficiencies occur about twice as often in people with recurrent aphthous ulcers[1][17]. A simple blood panel can check for them[1].

Systemic disease and drugs. Aphthous-like ulcers appear in celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, Behcet's disease, HIV, PFAPA syndrome, and cyclic neutropenia[1][2][15]. Oral ulceration can also be a side effect of some medicines[2]. Hormonal changes, including the menstrual cycle, and emotional stress are recognized triggers[1][15][17].

Food can matter for some people: reviews list food allergens among predisposing factors and suggest asking whether flares follow particular foods, such as hard, acidic, or spicy ones[15]. And one surprise: canker sores are less common in tobacco smokers, and stopping smoking is a recognized trigger[1][15][17]. That is not a reason to smoke, but it is a reason not to be surprised by mouth ulcers soon after quitting.

How Long Does a Canker Sore Last?

The honest answer: a minor canker sore usually heals within two weeks[1][17]. Nothing you can do at home reliably makes a minor ulcer disappear overnight. What good treatment does is reduce pain, shorten healing, and shorten the window when eating and talking are miserable[1].

Major aphthae are a different situation. These last 5 to 10 weeks and usually leave a scar. Herpetiform ulcers each last one to two weeks, but up to 100 can appear at once[1].

If a single ulcer has lasted more than 2 weeks and is not obviously healing, that is a clinical trigger. It could be a stubborn major aphtha, but it could also be a traumatic ulcer that will not heal because the cause is still there, a drug reaction[2], or, uncommonly, oral cancer. The National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research advises seeing a dentist or doctor for a mouth sore or other oral cancer warning sign that lasts more than two weeks[16].

How to Get Rid of a Canker Sore Fast: The Protocol

The single question people search most is how to get rid of a canker sore fast. Fast in this context means "back to eating normally sooner," not "gone by tomorrow." Here is what the evidence actually supports, in the order to reach for it.

  1. Numb it first. An over-the-counter numbing gel can ease the pain[17]; use it only as the label directs. Prescription viscous lidocaine 2% is labeled for irritated or inflamed mouth and throat lining, and its label says not to eat for 60 minutes after use, because numbness raises the risk of choking and of biting your tongue or cheek[21].
  2. Cover it. A protective paste, film, or dissolving patch over the ulcer reduces contact pain. Use a product labeled for mouth ulcers; a review noted adverse events with cyanoacrylate (tissue glue) formulations, so do not put household or skin glue in your mouth[13].
  3. Add a topical corticosteroid. This is the first-line treatment, and it reduces pain and shortens healing time; it may even stop an episode when started at the first tingling[1][10]. Prescription options include triamcinolone acetonide 0.1% dental paste, whose label says to press a small dab onto the sore until a thin film forms, at bedtime and, if needed, two or three times a day after meals[19]. A review advises not eating or drinking for 20 minutes after applying a topical steroid[15].
  4. Rinse. A chlorhexidine mouth rinse lessens severity and shortens ulcers, though it does not reduce how often outbreaks happen[1]. In the US it is sold as a 0.12% rinse labeled for gingivitis; the label directs 15 mL swished for 30 seconds twice daily after brushing, then spit out[20].
  5. Modify the diet. During a flare, avoid hot, spicy, acidic, and salty foods[1][17], and hard, sharp foods such as toast[15]. Cool or lukewarm foods and softer textures are easier to eat.
  6. Switch to an SLS-free toothpaste. Check the ingredient list for sodium lauryl sulfate. Trials compared toothpastes over weeks (8 weeks per toothpaste in one trial), not days, so the payoff is longer term[7][12].

Benzocaine safety: Use only as directed on the product label. Do not use oral benzocaine in children younger than 2 years. Rarely, it can cause methemoglobinemia, a serious blood-oxygen problem. Stop using it and seek emergency care for blue or gray lips or skin or shortness of breath after use[14].

For a severe episode, a licensed clinician can prescribe a stronger topical steroid such as clobetasol or, in some cases, a short course of oral prednisone[1][15].

A note on amlexanox 5% oral paste (Aphthasol), a prescription product rather than an over-the-counter one[13]. Four vehicle-controlled, randomized, double-blind trials with 1,335 participants, published by Khandwala and colleagues in 1997, found that applying it four times a day sped healing and pain relief compared with the vehicle paste or no treatment[8]. Safety studies in 991 exposed participants found minimal adverse effects[9]. The catch is availability: amlexanox is no longer available in the US[18].

Prescription Options: Triamcinolone, Fluocinonide, Clobetasol, and Kenalog in Orabase

When over-the-counter treatments are not enough, the mainstay is a topical corticosteroid applied directly to the ulcer; topical corticosteroids are the first line of treatment[1]. A 2026 umbrella review of 41 systematic reviews found they consistently reduced pain and shortened healing time, although evidence that they prevent recurrences was limited[10].

On a small screen, swipe across the diagram to read every label.

Triamcinolone acetonide 0.1% dental paste, the product people often know by the older name Kenalog in Orabase, is the classic prescription. Generic triamcinolone acetonide dental paste USP 0.1% is a prescription product with current prescribing information on DailyMed. Its directions: press a small dab (about 1/4 inch) onto the sore until a thin film develops, do not rub it in, apply it at bedtime, and, depending on severity, two or three times a day, preferably after meals. The label adds that if significant repair has not occurred in seven days, further investigation is advisable[19]. A literature review notes that 0.1% is considered the most effective concentration[15].

Fluocinonide 0.05% is another prescription option. StatPearls lists fluocinonide 0.05% or clobetasol 0.05% ointment mixed 1:1 with Orabase, applied three times a day, as an option when ulcers keep recurring in the same area[1].

Clobetasol propionate is the most potent topical corticosteroid used for canker sores. It is reserved for moderate or severe disease and is regarded as an alternative before systemic treatment[15]. Use it only as prescribed.

For patients with many or widespread ulcers that make it hard to place a paste, a topical steroid can be prescribed as a rinse instead. It is used for the prescribed time and is not swallowed[15].

Magic Mouthwash and Chlorhexidine

Two mouth rinses come up in almost every clinician-patient conversation about canker sores: chlorhexidine gluconate 0.12% and magic mouthwash.

Chlorhexidine gluconate 0.12% (Peridex) is an antiseptic rinse whose labeled use is gingivitis. In canker sores, chlorhexidine lessens severity and shortens ulcer duration but does not change how often outbreaks happen[1]. Use it as an adjunct, not a standalone treatment. The label directs 15 mL swished for 30 seconds twice daily after brushing, then spit out, without rinsing with water or eating right afterward. It can stain teeth and the tongue and can change taste[20].

Magic mouthwash is a compounded rinse with no single standard formula; many versions include a numbing medicine such as lidocaine. This review did not find randomized trials of magic mouthwash in canker sores, so think of it as a comfort option, prescribed by a clinician, when several ulcers make single-sore pastes impractical.

A practical point about lidocaine, alone or in a mixed rinse: it numbs the throat as well as the mouth and can impair swallowing, which raises the risk of choking. Its label says to swish it and spit it out when used in the mouth, to space doses at least three hours apart, to take no more than eight doses in 24 hours, and not to eat for 60 minutes afterward[21].

Big Canker Sores and Major Aphthae

A "big canker sore" is either a single, deep, unusually persistent minor aphtha or, more commonly, a major aphthous ulcer. Major aphthae are larger than 1 cm, last 5 to 10 weeks, and usually leave a scar[1]. They tend to appear on the lips, soft palate, and pharynx, which is where "canker sore in throat" fits[15].

Major aphthae warrant a more assertive approach than the drugstore route:

  • A more potent topical steroid, such as clobetasol, as an alternative before systemic treatment[15].
  • A short course of oral prednisone for a severe episode, with the dose and duration set by the prescriber[1].
  • Specialist-prescribed colchicine for severe or refractory recurrences. An umbrella review found benefit in these cases, limited by side effects and low-certainty evidence, and a Cochrane review found too little evidence to support or refute any systemic treatment[4][10]. Consider referral to oral medicine or rheumatology.
  • Systemic evaluation: at this severity, look for underlying celiac disease, IBD, HIV, hematinic deficiency, and Behcet's disease[1][2][15].

For truly refractory cases, specialists may use immune-modulating drugs such as pentoxifylline, dapsone, or thalidomide[1], or apremilast. The clearest evidence for apremilast comes from a 2019 NEJM randomized trial in Behçet's syndrome, in which it reduced the number of oral ulcers over 12 weeks more than placebo, with side effects including diarrhea, nausea, and headache. That trial studied Behçet's syndrome, not ordinary recurrent canker sores, so it should not be treated as evidence for routine canker-sore prescribing[11]. A 2024 meta-analysis of 21 randomized trials (1,668 patients) found thalidomide improved response and healing in recurrent aphthous stomatitis but significantly increased adverse events[3]; its known risks include birth defects and nerve damage[15]. These are specialist decisions, not primary care ones.

Recurrent Canker Sores: Prevention That Works

Recurrent aphthous stomatitis is defined by pattern, not size. If ulcers keep coming back, three months apart, six months apart, or every few weeks, the goal shifts from treating one ulcer to reducing how often they happen.

On a small screen, swipe across the diagram to read every label.

A low-cost option with trial evidence is switching to an SLS-free toothpaste. A 2019 systematic review found SLS-free toothpaste reduced the number of ulcers, ulcer duration, number of episodes, and pain, though it called for better trials[7]. One double-blind crossover trial found shorter, less painful ulcers but no change in the number of ulcers or episodes[12]. Check the ingredient list, and judge the switch over a couple of months rather than days.

Chlorhexidine rinse lessens severity and shortens ulcers but does not reduce how often outbreaks happen[1]. StatPearls pairs it with a short course of topical steroid started as soon as ulcers appear[1], so use it during a flare rather than as a prevention strategy.

Sublingual vitamin B12 1000 mcg daily merits attention. In a 6-month randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial published in the Journal of the American Board of Family Medicine, Volkov and colleagues gave 58 primary care patients with recurrent aphthous stomatitis sublingual B12 1000 mcg or placebo. Outbreak duration, ulcer number, and pain fell significantly at months 5 and 6 in the B12 group, regardless of baseline B12 levels, and the authors called the treatment simple, inexpensive, and low-risk[6]. It is one small trial, so expect benefit, if any, over months.

Specialist-prescribed colchicine is reserved for severe or refractory cases; reviews describe benefit limited by side effects and low-certainty evidence[4][10].

Behavioral basics that support prevention: avoid local trauma, use a soft-bristle brush, avoid hard foods when a flare starts, and notice whether flares follow particular foods[15].

Vitamin B12, Iron, Folate, and Celiac Disease

Nutritional deficiencies are among the treatable causes of recurrent aphthous stomatitis, and checking a blood count, ferritin, folate, and vitamin B12 is common practice in patients with frequent ulcers[1][15]. Hematinic deficiencies occur about twice as often in people with recurrent aphthae, and correcting them may partly or fully resolve the ulcers[1].

If you have frequent, unexplained canker sores, ask about celiac testing, since celiac disease is linked to recurrent aphthae[1]. The American College of Gastroenterology guideline describes a tissue transglutaminase antibody blood test as very helpful for initial screening, with an intestinal biopsy needed in most patients to confirm the diagnosis; treatment is a strict gluten-free diet[22].

What Doesn't Work (and What to Skip)

A short list of treatments and habits that show up on internet lists but do not do what people think.

  • Aciclovir or valacyclovir. These antivirals are used for cold sores, which are a herpes simplex infection[17]. Canker sores have no connection to herpes viruses[1], so antivirals are not a canker sore treatment. If you take antivirals for recurrent cold sores and still get canker sores, the antivirals are not the reason.
  • Alcohol-based mouthwash. NIDCR advises against mouthwashes that contain alcohol because they can irritate the sores[17].
  • A routine daily multivitamin as prevention. A literature review notes that daily multivitamin supplements have not been shown to reduce the number or duration of outbreaks; testing for a specific deficiency is more useful[15].
  • Pills for an ordinary canker sore. Systemic drugs are reserved for severe cases, and a Cochrane review found insufficient evidence to support or refute any systemic treatment for recurrent aphthous stomatitis[4].

Red Flags: When a Mouth Ulcer Is Not Just a Canker Sore

Every year a subset of "canker sore" complaints turn out to be something else. The findings below are indications to see a licensed clinician, in person if you can. They are drawn from the StatPearls review, a gastroenterology review of oral ulceration, a dental literature review, and the NIDCR oral cancer page[1][2][15][16].

See a clinician if any of the following are true:
  • An ulcer larger than 1 cm or a mouth sore that has lasted more than 2 weeks without healing.
  • A lump, thick patch, or white or red patch in the mouth, or numbness of the tongue (concern for oral cancer).
  • Ulcers with fever, weight loss, chronic diarrhea, or blood in the stool (concern for IBD or celiac).
  • Ulcers with recurrent genital ulcers, uveitis or eye pain, skin lesions, or joint pain (concern for Behcet's disease).
  • Sudden outbreaks of ulcers that start in adulthood without an obvious trigger.
  • Unusually severe ulcers, or ulcers with a history of high-risk sexual exposure or HIV risk.
  • Ulcers with unexplained fatigue, pallor, or numbness (concern for B12 or iron deficiency).
  • A sore that is not healing in someone who uses tobacco or drinks heavily, both risk factors for oral cancer. Get seen in person.

A licensed clinician can assess for these causes, order the right tests (CBC, ferritin, B12, folate, a tissue transglutaminase antibody test), and, when needed, refer to oral medicine or another specialty. For most patients the workup is short and the answer is reassuring.
