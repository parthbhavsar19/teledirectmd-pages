Cellulitis (Skin Infection)

Symptoms, causes, MRSA coverage, and when to seek care, based on IDSA guidelines.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: March 2026 · Updated: Jul 2026
15 min read

How is cellulitis treated?

Cellulitis is a bacterial infection of the deep dermis and the tissue just beneath it. The IDSA 2014 skin and soft tissue infection guideline recommends that typical cellulitis without systemic signs of infection be treated with an antibiotic active against streptococci, such as penicillin, cephalexin, dicloxacillin, or clindamycin, for 5 days, extended if the infection has not improved. When MRSA is a concern, oral options that cover both streptococci and MRSA include clindamycin alone, or TMP-SMX or doxycycline combined with a beta-lactam. The guideline recommends outpatient treatment for patients without SIRS (a body-wide inflammatory response), altered mental status, or unstable blood pressure, and hospitalization when a deeper or necrotizing infection is suspected or outpatient treatment is failing. In one hospital study, 30.5% of patients admitted from the emergency department for leg cellulitis were misdiagnosed, so getting the diagnosis right matters. A licensed clinician, through telehealth or an in-person evaluation, can confirm the diagnosis and recommend next steps.

Key Takeaways

  • Cellulitis is a bacterial infection of the skin and underlying soft tissue. Most cases arise from streptococci, often Group A Streptococcus; Staphylococcus aureus causes cellulitis less often.[1]
  • For typical cellulitis without systemic signs of infection, the IDSA 2014 skin and soft tissue infection guidelines recommend an antibiotic active against streptococci; suitable oral choices include penicillin, cephalexin, dicloxacillin, or clindamycin.[1]
  • When MRSA is a concern (for example, penetrating trauma, MRSA infection elsewhere, MRSA in the nose, or injection drug use), the IDSA lists oral options that cover both MRSA and streptococci: clindamycin alone, or TMP-SMX or doxycycline combined with a beta-lactam such as cephalexin.[1]
  • Marking the border of redness at the time of diagnosis is the most practical way to monitor treatment response.
  • Tinea pedis (athlete's foot) is frequently associated with a higher risk of recurrent cellulitis, and the IDSA advises checking between the toes and treating it in leg cellulitis.[3][1]
  • Go to the emergency department for rapidly spreading redness, fever above 101°F (38.3°C), periorbital involvement, or any cellulitis in a patient who is immunocompromised.

Patients often wait too long before seeking care for cellulitis, assuming the redness on a leg is an allergic reaction, a bug bite, or something that will settle down on its own. Cellulitis can spread quickly. When it is caught early, mild cellulitis can usually be treated with oral antibiotics.

Skin and soft tissue infections are common: in a U.S. insurance claims database covering 2010 to 2020, 5.4 million patients had 9.1 million skin and soft tissue infection episodes, and abscesses and cellulitis were the most frequent types.[7] Cellulitis is also frequently misdiagnosed. In one study at a large urban hospital, 30.5% of patients admitted from the emergency department with a diagnosis of lower-extremity cellulitis were misdiagnosed; inflammatory skin conditions of the leg (so-called pseudocellulitis) are often mistaken for it.[11] Getting the diagnosis right matters because the wrong treatment wastes time and delays care for the real problem.

This guide covers everything I discuss with patients in the exam room: what cellulitis is, how to recognize it, how to tell it apart from other conditions that look similar, how we treat it based on current IDSA guidelines, and, critically, how to keep it from coming back.

What Is Cellulitis?

Cellulitis is a bacterial infection of the dermis (the deep layer of skin) and the subcutaneous tissues just beneath it. It is not a surface infection. That distinction is important because it explains both why it looks the way it does and why topical antibiotic creams have no meaningful role in treating it.

When bacteria breach the skin's outer barrier, they trigger an acute inflammatory response in the deeper tissue layers. Your immune system sends white blood cells to the area, producing the four classic signs: redness, warmth, swelling, and pain. The bacteria also release toxins that damage tissue, which is why prompt treatment prevents the infection from burrowing deeper into muscle or bone.

Cellulitis often affects the lower legs, though it can occur anywhere on the body. A population-based study in Olmsted County, Minnesota, found lower-extremity cellulitis at a rate of 199 per 100,000 person-years, and the rate rose with age.[6] The face, periorbital area, and hands are other sites that warrant particular attention, given the anatomical structures at risk if those infections spread.

Symptoms

The typical presentation of cellulitis follows a recognizable pattern. Most patients describe noticing redness that was small at first and then grew over 12–24 hours. By the time they're in my office, the area is warm to the touch, tender, and swollen, and often they can trace where the edge of the redness was yesterday compared to where it is today.

The Core Signs

  • Redness (erythema): The skin appears red or pink, often with poorly defined borders that merge gradually into normal skin. Erysipelas, a more superficial infection, tends to have a sharp, raised border; classic cellulitis does not.
  • Warmth: The infected skin is noticeably warmer than surrounding tissue when you press the back of your hand to it.
  • Swelling (edema): The area feels puffy or tight. In the lower leg, swelling may cause the skin to become tense and shiny.
  • Pain or tenderness: The skin hurts to touch. Severe pain out of proportion to what you can see on the surface is a red flag for a deeper, more serious infection like necrotizing fasciitis.
  • Spreading border: Active cellulitis spreads outward over hours to days. A border that is moving is still active infection, even if you are already on antibiotics. Treatment is working when the border stops advancing, typically within 24–48 hours of starting the right antibiotic.

Systemic Symptoms

Fever, chills, fatigue, and body aches indicate the infection has triggered a systemic response. Their presence moves the infection from the mild to the moderate or severe category in the IDSA framework, and the guideline recommends outpatient treatment only for patients without SIRS (a body-wide inflammatory response), altered mental status, or unstable blood pressure.[1] Some patients also develop enlarged, tender lymph nodes near the affected area (regional lymphadenopathy) and red streaking along the skin that tracks toward lymph nodes (lymphangitis). Both are signs that the immune system is working hard and that the infection is moving, so see a physician the same day.

Causes

Cellulitis is almost always caused by skin bacteria that get underneath the surface. Two organisms account for the overwhelming majority of cases.

Staphylococcus aureus, including both methicillin-sensitive (MSSA) and methicillin-resistant (MRSA) strains, causes a large percentage of skin abscesses. In non-purulent cellulitis (no pus, no abscess), the vast majority of infections arise from streptococci, often Group A Streptococcus (S. pyogenes), and the IDSA notes that MRSA is an unusual cause of typical cellulitis.[1] This distinction matters because it drives antibiotic selection: Streptococcus is reliably sensitive to beta-lactam antibiotics, while MRSA requires different coverage entirely.

How Bacteria Enter the Skin

Bacteria need a way in. They do not penetrate intact, healthy skin. The most common entry points are:

  • Tinea pedis (athlete's foot): The macerated, cracked skin between the toes is the leading portal of entry for lower extremity cellulitis. Many patients have no idea they have a fungal infection until I examine their feet.
  • Minor cuts, abrasions, and insect bites: Small breaks are sufficient. Scratching a mosquito bite, a paper cut, or a minor graze can introduce bacteria.
  • Leg ulcers and venous stasis wounds: Chronic wounds are almost universally colonized with bacteria and provide direct access to deeper tissue.
  • Surgical wounds and IV sites: Any procedure that disrupts the skin creates a potential entry point.
  • Folliculitis: Infected hair follicles can serve as seeding sites for spreading infection.
  • Injection drug use: Skin popping and IV drug use are associated with higher rates of MRSA and polymicrobial infection.
A Note on Blood Cultures

The IDSA 2014 guidelines do not recommend routine cultures of blood or skin for typical cellulitis; blood cultures are generally positive in 5% or fewer of cases.[1] The guideline calls for blood cultures in patients with cancer, severe systemic features such as high fever and low blood pressure, or unusual predisposing factors such as immersion injuries, animal bites, neutropenia, or severe immune deficiency.[1] Routine wound swabs of intact cellulitis are similarly unhelpful; the surface bacteria do not reflect what is causing the deep infection.

Risk Factors

Cellulitis is not random. Certain conditions consistently appear in the background of patients who develop it, especially those who get it repeatedly. Understanding these factors is as important as treating the acute infection, because recurrence rates are high when underlying issues go unaddressed.

  • Diabetes mellitus: Chronic high blood sugar affects immune cell function, and poor glucose control has been linked to a higher risk of cellulitis. Complications of diabetes such as peripheral neuropathy and ulcers also raise the risk of infection.[3]
  • Lymphedema: Lymphatic drainage removes fluid and carries immune cells to sites of infection. When lymphatic channels are damaged by prior surgery, cancer treatment, trauma, or filariasis, fluid pools in the tissue, creating a protein-rich environment ideal for bacterial growth. Patients with lymphedema can develop cellulitis from bacteria with no obvious entry point.
  • Obesity: Excess adipose tissue impairs lymphatic drainage in the legs and creates skin folds that trap moisture and reduce local immune surveillance. Obesity was a common factor in many studies of recurrent cellulitis, and cellulitis in people with obesity is associated with worse outcomes and treatment failure.[3]
  • Venous insufficiency: Chronic venous disease causes tissue congestion, hypoxia, and chronic skin breakdown that makes the lower legs particularly susceptible to infection.
  • Prior cellulitis: Having one episode of cellulitis is one of the strongest predictors of future episodes. Tissue architecture changes after infection, lymphatic channels may be damaged, and underlying risk factors persist if not actively managed. In a large U.S. claims study, a past skin or soft tissue infection was linked to the highest infection rate of any risk factor examined: 113.5 episodes per 1,000 person-years in people whose only listed risk factor was a previous infection, compared with 32.1 in people with no listed risk factors.[7]
  • Tinea pedis: Fungal infection of the feet, particularly between the toes, produces chronic skin fissuring that serves as a persistent bacterial entry point. This is one of the most treatable risk factors and arguably the most overlooked. I examine every cellulitis patient's feet, and a meaningful number have undiagnosed tinea pedis.
  • Immunosuppression: HIV, corticosteroid therapy, chemotherapy, solid organ transplant recipients, and patients receiving biologics all have impaired defenses and may develop atypical or rapidly progressive infections.

Diagnosis

Cellulitis is a clinical diagnosis. There is no blood test or imaging study that definitively confirms it; the diagnosis is made by examining the skin and fitting the picture to the overall clinical context. This is both straightforward and, in practice, frequently wrong.

What I Look For

The characteristic triad is redness, warmth, and tenderness in a patient with a plausible entry point or recognized risk factors. I pay particular attention to:

  • Whether the borders are defined or diffuse: sharp, raised borders suggest erysipelas rather than typical cellulitis
  • The presence of fluctuance (a soft, movable center indicating pus), which means an abscess rather than pure cellulitis
  • Whether there is a central clearing, satellite lesions, or irregular distribution, which suggest an alternative diagnosis
  • Bilateral involvement: true cellulitis is almost always unilateral; bilateral lower leg redness in a patient with known heart failure or venous disease is far more likely to be stasis dermatitis
  • Skin texture: tight, shiny, blistered, or darkened skin can indicate a more severe or necrotizing process

Marking the Border

One of the most useful things I do in the exam room, and one of the most practical things patients can do at home, is to mark the border of the redness with a skin-safe marker or pen. Drawing a line around the leading edge, with the date and time noted, tells you whether the infection is spreading or shrinking when you check 12–24 hours later. If the redness is advancing beyond the line after 24–48 hours of antibiotics, that is a sign the treatment is not working and the patient needs re-evaluation.

When Imaging or Labs Are Needed

Most straightforward cellulitis cases need no additional workup. However, certain situations change that:

  • CT scan or MRI: Ordered when necrotizing fasciitis is suspected. Warning signs include severe pain disproportionate to skin appearance, skin discoloration, crepitus (air in tissue), or rapid systemic deterioration. Imaging helps identify gas in soft tissue planes, though a negative CT does not rule out necrotizing infection in early stages.
  • CBC and CMP: Reasonable for patients with fever, systemic symptoms, or those who are immunocompromised or diabetic. Elevated white cell count confirms inflammatory response. Elevated creatinine or glucose may affect antibiotic selection and disposition decisions.
  • Blood cultures: As noted above, reserved for patients with systemic signs or immunosuppression.
  • Ultrasound: Helpful when there is clinical uncertainty about whether a collection (abscess) is present beneath the cellulitis, particularly in obese patients where palpation is less reliable.

Cellulitis vs. Abscess vs. DVT

Getting the diagnosis right determines everything that follows. Three conditions are frequently confused with cellulitis, and each requires a different approach.

The DVT distinction deserves particular emphasis. DVT can produce unilateral leg swelling, warmth, and tenderness, a presentation that closely mimics early cellulitis. One clinical reference table describes DVT skin as normal or red but blanchable (the color fades when you press on it) with a smooth surface, while cellulitis skin is red and can have an orange-peel texture; red streaking and swollen regional lymph nodes are frequent with cellulitis and uncommon with DVT.[8] If there is diagnostic uncertainty in a patient with risk factors for clotting, such as recent surgery, prolonged immobility, cancer, or oral contraceptive use, testing is needed: American Society of Hematology guidelines note that leg DVT is diagnosed with ultrasonography, and that in people at low risk, starting with a D-dimer blood test reduces the need for imaging.[9]

Stasis dermatitis is perhaps the most common misdiagnosis for bilateral lower extremity cellulitis. Stasis dermatitis is inflammation driven by chronic venous insufficiency. It produces redness, scaling, and itching in both legs, often in a classic "sock" distribution around the ankle and lower calf. It is not infectious and does not require antibiotics. Treating it as cellulitis delays the right treatment (compression, emollients, venous care) and exposes the patient to unnecessary antibiotics.

Treatment

Treatment decisions flow from two questions: Is this purulent (pus-forming) or non-purulent cellulitis? And are there risk factors for MRSA or signs of more severe infection? The IDSA 2014 skin and soft tissue infection guidelines provide a clear evidence-based framework for both.[1]

IDSA 2014 Classification: Mild, Moderate, Severe

Mild: Typical cellulitis with no focus of pus and no systemic signs of infection. The guideline recommends an antibiotic active against streptococci, and many patients can take oral medication from the start.
Moderate: Typical cellulitis with systemic signs of infection, such as fever. Systemic antibiotics are indicated.
Severe: Infection that has failed oral antibiotic treatment, or that comes with SIRS (for example, temperature above 38°C or heart rate above 90), a weakened immune system, or signs of deeper infection such as blisters (bullae), skin sloughing, low blood pressure, or organ dysfunction. The guideline recommends hospitalization when a deeper or necrotizing infection is suspected, when the immune system is severely weakened, or when outpatient treatment is failing.[1]

First-Line Oral Antibiotics: Non-Purulent Cellulitis (No MRSA Risk)

For typical, non-purulent cellulitis in a patient without MRSA risk factors, streptococcal coverage is the target. The IDSA lists penicillin, amoxicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanate, dicloxacillin, cephalexin, or clindamycin as suitable oral antibiotics for most patients; adult doses below are from the guideline's dosing table.[1]

Antibiotic Typical Adult Regimen Notes
Cephalexin (Keflex) 500 mg four times daily × 5 days Listed by IDSA among suitable oral choices.[1] Well-tolerated. Covers streptococci and MSSA.
Dicloxacillin 500 mg four times daily × 5 days Listed by IDSA among suitable oral choices.[1] Covers streptococci and MSSA. The label says it is best absorbed on an empty stomach.[13]
Amoxicillin-clavulanate 875/125 mg twice daily × 5 days Listed by IDSA among suitable oral choices; a broader-spectrum option.[1]
Clindamycin 300–450 mg four times daily × 5 days Listed by IDSA among suitable oral choices.[1] Check local susceptibility. The label warns about C. difficile infection, a cause of severe diarrhea that has been reported with nearly all antibiotics.[14]

The IDSA 2014 guidelines specify a 5-day course of antibiotics as the standard duration, extended if the infection has not clearly improved by that point.[1] The old reflexive 10-day course is no longer supported by evidence for uncomplicated, non-purulent cellulitis. Shorter courses reduce side effects, cost, and contribution to antibiotic resistance without reducing efficacy when the diagnosis is correct.

When MRSA Coverage Is Needed

MRSA is an unusual cause of typical cellulitis, so most cases do not need MRSA coverage. The IDSA recommends an antibiotic that covers MRSA when cellulitis is associated with any of the following:[1]

  • Penetrating trauma as the entry point
  • Evidence of MRSA infection elsewhere
  • Known MRSA nasal colonization
  • Injection drug use
  • Purulent drainage
  • Systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), which places the infection in the IDSA severe category
Antibiotic Typical Adult Regimen Notes
TMP-SMX (Bactrim DS) 1–2 DS tablets twice daily × 5 days An IDSA oral option for MRSA.[1] The label says sulfonamides should not be used to treat group A streptococcal infections, so for cellulitis the IDSA pairs it with a beta-lactam such as cephalexin.[4][1] Contraindicated with a known allergy to sulfonamides or trimethoprim. It can raise potassium, especially with drugs that also raise potassium such as ACE inhibitors, so potassium may need monitoring.[4]
Doxycycline 100 mg twice daily × 5 days An IDSA oral option for MRSA, paired with a beta-lactam for cellulitis.[1] In a retrospective study of MRSA skin infections, mostly abscesses, the authors judged doxycycline or minocycline a reasonable oral option where local MRSA strains are tetracycline-susceptible.[2] If it upsets your stomach, the label says it can be taken with food or milk. The label warns that use during tooth development, including the last half of pregnancy, can permanently discolor teeth.[12]
Clindamycin 300–450 mg four times daily × 5 days Covers both MRSA and streptococci on its own.[1] The label advises screening erythromycin-resistant staphylococci for inducible clindamycin resistance with the D-zone test.[14]

For severe infections requiring hospitalization, the IDSA lists intravenous drugs that cover MRSA, such as vancomycin, daptomycin, linezolid, or telavancin, and calls vancomycin plus either piperacillin-tazobactam or imipenem/meropenem a reasonable empiric regimen for severe infections.[1]

Elevation and Supportive Care

Elevation of the affected limb helps swelling go down. The IDSA guidelines say elevation hastens improvement by promoting gravity drainage of edema and inflammatory substances, and they give a strong recommendation to elevation of the affected area together with treatment of predisposing factors such as edema or underlying skin disorders.[1]

The guidelines also say systemic corticosteroids (for example, prednisone 40 mg daily for 7 days) could be considered in nondiabetic adults with cellulitis, based in part on a randomized trial of 108 nondiabetic adults in which adding a short oral steroid course to antibiotics led to faster clinical resolution. This is a weak recommendation, so steroids are a case-by-case decision with your physician.[1]

When to See a Doctor

Knowing where to go, and how fast, is one of the most practical decisions you face when you notice a red, swollen area on your skin. Here is the framework used in practice when talking with patients.

Scenario Appropriate Level of Care
Small area of redness and warmth, no fever, no systemic symptoms, you have mild risk factors (e.g., minor cut on leg) Telehealth visit: A physician can evaluate photos and your description, confirm the diagnosis, and prescribe the right antibiotic without you needing to leave home. Appropriate for mild, early-stage cellulitis in otherwise healthy patients.
Spreading redness that has grown over 24–48 hours, mild tenderness, low-grade fever under 100.4°F, manageable pain Urgent care or same-day physician visit: In-person evaluation allows border marking, assessment of depth, and closer examination for abscess or other complications. Prescriptions can be issued and a 24-hour follow-up plan established.
Fever above 101°F (38.3°C), rapidly spreading redness that has changed significantly in a few hours, significant pain, red streaking, or you are diabetic/immunocompromised with worsening symptoms Emergency department or urgent care with IV capability: May need IV antibiotics, labs, imaging, and closer monitoring. Do not wait overnight to see if it improves.
Redness involving the eye area (eyelid, around the orbit), any signs of vision change, pain with eye movement, or proptosis (eye appearing to bulge) Emergency department immediately: Periorbital and orbital cellulitis can threaten vision and spread to the brain. These require urgent CT scan and IV antibiotics, and often ophthalmology consultation.
Skin turning dark, purple, or black; excruciating pain out of proportion to appearance; gas or crackling felt under the skin; signs of sepsis (confusion, extremely rapid heart rate, low blood pressure) Call 911 or go to the ER by the fastest means available. These are signs of necrotizing fasciitis or sepsis, both life-threatening emergencies requiring immediate surgical evaluation.

The recommendation for patients: a telehealth visit is genuinely appropriate for many cellulitis presentations. You do not automatically need an emergency room. But if there is any doubt (the redness is moving fast, you feel systemically unwell, or you are in a high-risk group), go in person and go quickly.

Red Flags

Seek Emergency Care Immediately For:
  • Rapidly spreading redness: borders that are visibly advancing over a period of hours, not days
  • Fever above 101°F (38.3°C): signals systemic infection that may require IV antibiotics
  • Severe pain disproportionate to the skin appearance: the hallmark warning sign for necrotizing fasciitis, where deep tissue destruction outpaces what you can see on the surface
  • Skin blistering, darkening, or sloughing: indicates a severe infectious process with tissue death
  • Periorbital or orbital involvement: any cellulitis involving the eye area requires emergency evaluation because orbital cellulitis can cause blindness and meningitis if not treated with IV antibiotics promptly
  • Crepitus (crackling sensation under the skin): indicates gas-producing bacteria and necrotizing infection
  • Signs of sepsis: confusion, rapid heart rate, low blood pressure, or extreme fatigue alongside skin infection
  • Immunocompromised patients: anyone on chemotherapy, biologic therapy, corticosteroids, or with HIV, organ transplant, or poorly controlled diabetes should be evaluated in person for any but the most minor skin infections

Necrotizing fasciitis, sometimes called "flesh-eating bacteria" in the media, is rare but worth understanding because the window for effective surgical intervention is narrow. The bacteria involved (most often Group A Streptococcus, Clostridium species, or mixed organisms) produce enzymes that destroy the fascia and subcutaneous tissue faster than the skin surface shows. Pain that seems completely out of proportion to what the skin looks like is the most important clinical clue. If you or someone else describes the pain as "the worst ever" and the redness seems almost unremarkable in comparison, that is an emergency.

Prevention

Cellulitis often comes back: a 2022 review reports recurrence rates ranging from 16% to 53% within three years, and the risk of recurrence rises with each episode.[3] Prevention is where the most meaningful long-term gains are made.

Treat Tinea Pedis First

Every patient seen in clinical practice with recurrent lower extremity cellulitis gets a careful foot exam, and many have tinea pedis they were either unaware of or not treating consistently. Interdigital tinea pedis, the classic athlete's foot between the toes, causes skin fissuring that lets bacteria in. The IDSA advises examining the toe spaces in leg cellulitis because treating fissuring, scaling, or maceration may eradicate colonization with pathogens and reduce recurrent infection.[1] A 2022 review recommends foot hygiene with attention to the toe webs, and notes that topical or oral antifungal medicines may be needed to control recurrent tinea pedis.[3]

Compression for Lymphedema and Venous Insufficiency

Chronic leg swelling is a major risk factor for cellulitis.[3] In a small, single-center randomized trial of 84 people with chronic leg swelling and recurrent cellulitis, 15% of those using leg compression plus education had another episode, compared with 40% of those given education alone, and the trial was stopped early for efficacy.[5] Ask your clinician which compression level is right for you, since graduated compression stockings are not suitable for everyone, including people with peripheral vascular disease or peripheral neuropathy.[3]

Skin Care and Barrier Maintenance

Dry, cracked skin, particularly on the feet and lower legs, loses its protective barrier function. Regular application of emollients (unscented moisturizers) to the lower extremities reduces fissuring and helps maintain intact skin. Gentle daily washing, particularly between the toes, removes bacteria before they can establish themselves. Patients with venous stasis dermatitis should have this treated actively, since inflamed skin is far more permeable than healthy skin.

Managing Systemic Risk Factors

Blood glucose control in diabetics reduces both the frequency and severity of skin infections. Weight management, though difficult, reduces the lymphatic and venous stasis that drives lower extremity cellulitis. Patients with lymphedema benefit from working with a lymphedema therapist to optimize drainage technique and compression wrapping.

Antibiotic Prophylaxis for Recurrent Cases

For patients who continue to have 3–4 episodes per year despite addressing modifiable risk factors, the IDSA 2014 guidelines say prophylactic antibiotics should be considered: oral penicillin or erythromycin twice daily for 4–52 weeks, or intramuscular benzathine penicillin every 2–4 weeks.[1] In the PATCH I trial of 274 people with two or more episodes of leg cellulitis, penicillin 250 mg twice daily for 12 months reduced recurrences during treatment (22% with penicillin vs 37% with placebo), but the protective effect faded after the drug was stopped.[10] A 2022 review describes non-antibiotic measures as first-line for prevention, with antibiotic prophylaxis limited to non-purulent cellulitis where risk factors are being managed.[3]

Frequently Asked Questions

Cellulitis does not reliably clear on its own. Unlike some superficial skin irritations, cellulitis is a bacterial infection of the deeper skin layers that requires antibiotic treatment. Without antibiotics, the infection is likely to spread and may progress to bacteremia or sepsis. Early treatment with the right antibiotic typically produces clear improvement within 24–48 hours. Do not wait more than 1–2 days if you suspect cellulitis.

The clearest sign of worsening cellulitis is redness that spreads beyond the border you marked at the start of treatment. Also watch for increasing pain or swelling, development of fever or chills, skin that begins to blister or change color (darker, purple, or mottled), or red streaking extending from the affected area. If any of these occur after starting antibiotics, contact your physician the same day. Fever above 101°F (38.3°C) or rapid spread of redness are reasons to go to the emergency department without waiting.

Cellulitis itself is not contagious from person to person. It develops when bacteria enter your own skin through a break or compromised barrier, not through casual contact with another person who has cellulitis. That said, MRSA bacteria can colonize skin surfaces and spread between people through direct contact, so good hand hygiene around open wounds and avoiding sharing towels or personal items with someone who has an active MRSA infection is sensible practice.

An abscess is a localized collection of pus under the skin; you feel it as a tender, soft, fluctuant lump that may have a visible white or yellow center. Cellulitis is a diffuse infection of the tissue itself, without a walled-off pus pocket. The distinction matters for treatment: abscesses primarily need drainage (incision and drainage), while cellulitis is treated with antibiotics. Both can coexist: an abscess surrounded by spreading redness gets both drainage and antibiotics. If you feel a soft, movable center in the middle of the red area, tell your physician; that changes the plan.

Most cellulitis cases do not require an ER visit. A small, early-stage infection without fever in an otherwise healthy person can be evaluated and treated through a telehealth visit or urgent care appointment. Go to the emergency department if you have a fever above 101°F (38.3°C), rapidly expanding redness that is visibly growing over hours, pain out of proportion to the skin changes, skin blistering or darkening, or any cellulitis involving the eye area. Immunocompromised patients and those with poorly controlled diabetes should have a lower threshold for emergency evaluation.

Recurrent cellulitis almost always reflects persistent underlying risk factors rather than antibiotic failure. The most frequent culprits are untreated or undertreated tinea pedis (athlete's foot), chronic leg swelling from lymphedema or venous insufficiency, obesity, and poorly controlled diabetes. Each episode of cellulitis can also damage lymphatic channels, making future episodes more likely. The most effective prevention strategy is to identify and address the specific underlying factors in your case, not simply to repeat antibiotics every time it recurs. For patients with 3–4 episodes per year despite managing risk factors, the IDSA guidelines say prophylactic antibiotics should be considered.[1]

Quick Facts

ICD-10 Code: L03.90

Common Pathogens: Streptococci (often Group A); S. aureus less often

First-Line Tx: Strep-active oral antibiotic (e.g., cephalexin) × 5 days

MRSA Options: Clindamycin, or TMP-SMX or doxycycline plus a beta-lactam

Guidelines: IDSA SSTI 2014
