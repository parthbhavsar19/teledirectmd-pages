Antibiotic Stewardship in Telehealth: What the 2026 Evidence Shows

A review of the published data on antibiotic prescribing in virtual care: where telehealth performs well, where it falls short, and what strong stewardship programs do differently.

Written by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Published: June 17, 2026
8 min read
Last reviewed: June 2026

Key Takeaways

  • In a claims study of a large insurer's direct-to-patient telehealth program, televisits had comparable antibiotic use for upper respiratory infections, bronchitis and sinusitis, but higher use for pharyngitis and uncomplicated UTI, and far less strep testing before antibiotics for pharyngitis.[2]
  • For sinusitis, one primary care network found adults seen by virtual visit were less likely to receive antibiotics than those seen in the office (68.6% vs 94.3%), with similar guideline-concordant antibiotic selection.[3]
  • Two systematic reviews found no uniform telehealth effect. Both found more prescribing for ear infections (otitis media) by telehealth; for sinusitis, one found less prescribing and the other no significant difference. Both called the evidence limited or highly heterogeneous.[4][5]
  • The setting of a virtual visit matters: among children with respiratory infections, telemedicine visits with primary care practices involved antibiotics less often than visits with direct-to-consumer telemedicine companies (28.9% vs 37.2%).[14]
  • Two common stewardship risks in any setting are treating asymptomatic bacteriuria, which IDSA recommends against screening for or treating in healthy non-pregnant women,[9] and prescribing for sinusitis that does not meet the IDSA criteria for bacterial infection.[8]
  • Implementing CDC's Core Elements of Outpatient Antibiotic Stewardship in Veterans Health Administration emergency departments and primary care clinics was associated with lower antibiotic prescribing for respiratory infections; that study was not of telehealth.[7] In 2026, CDC updated the Core Elements and published stewardship strategies for outpatient telemedicine.[16][17]

Stewardship Pressure on Telehealth

Antibiotic resistance is a global public health threat.[1] According to CDC, at least 30% of outpatient antibiotic prescriptions in the United States are unnecessary.[15]

Against that backdrop, telehealth use in primary care has expanded rapidly since the COVID-19 pandemic, and reviewers have noted that this growth is outpacing knowledge about how to practice antibiotic stewardship in virtual care.[1] Whether telehealth prescribes antibiotics appropriately is therefore not an academic question. It carries real public health weight.

Early concerns were understandable. Telehealth brings limited physical examination and changes to the patient-physician relationship.[1] An early claims study of a large direct-to-consumer telemedicine company found overall antibiotic prescribing for adult respiratory infections similar to physician offices (58% vs 55%), but when antibiotics were prescribed, broad-spectrum agents were used far more often (86% vs 56%).[11] Among children with respiratory infections, antibiotics were prescribed at 52% of direct-to-consumer telemedicine visits, compared with 42% of urgent care and 31% of primary care visits.[12] Those findings prompted a closer look at the evidence, and at what separates high-quality telehealth stewardship from low-quality prescribing at a distance.

The picture that has emerged over the past several years is more varied, and more instructive, than either critics or advocates of telehealth predicted.

What the Evidence Shows on Telehealth Antibiotic Prescribing

Three categories of evidence now inform the stewardship conversation in telehealth: direct program evaluations, condition-specific comparisons, and systematic reviews across multiple studies.

Quality of Prescribing in Large Telehealth Programs

Later and larger claims studies found a more mixed picture. Among adults with respiratory infections at 38,839 direct-to-consumer telemedicine visits, matched against primary care and urgent care visits, researchers found clinically similar rates of antibiotic use, broad-spectrum antibiotic use and guideline-concordant antibiotic management, but less appropriate strep testing and more follow-up visits after telemedicine.[13]

A 2022 observational study by Cuellar and colleagues analyzed 2,400,198 patient visits by adults aged 18 to 64 in a large national insurer's direct-to-patient televisit benefit.[2] After adjustment, televisits were associated with comparable rates of antibiotic use for upper respiratory infections, bronchitis and sinusitis relative to most other sites, but higher rates for pharyngitis and uncomplicated UTI. When antibiotics were prescribed, guideline concordance for antibiotic type and duration was comparable to several other settings. The clearest gap was testing: among patients with pharyngitis who received antibiotics, Group A strep testing was done at 2.1% of televisits versus 58.0% of primary care visits.[2]

The practical takeaway: telehealth quality in antibiotic prescribing cannot be generalized across conditions. A program can perform comparably for some respiratory complaints while prescribing more often for others, so condition-specific monitoring matters more than a single program-wide prescribing rate.

Sinusitis: Virtual Visits May Prescribe Less

One of the more striking findings in the stewardship literature comes from a 2019 retrospective cohort study by Johnson and colleagues of 350 adults diagnosed with sinusitis in a single primary care network.[3] Patients seen by virtual visit were more likely to receive a guideline-concordant diagnosis (69.1% vs 45.7%) and less likely to receive antibiotics (68.6% vs 94.3%) than patients seen in the office, and guideline-concordant antibiotic selection was similar (67.5% vs 64.8%). There was a tradeoff: virtual-visit patients were more likely to return for sinusitis within 24 hours (8% vs 1.7%) and within 30 days (14.9% vs 7.4%).[3]

Systematic Reviews: Condition and Context Determine Quality

Two systematic reviews and meta-analyses published in 2021 synthesized the broader literature.[4][5] Bakhit and colleagues identified 13 studies and found no consistent pattern when results were pooled by infection: six studies of sinusitis showed significantly less prescribing in telehealth consultations, two studies of acute otitis media showed a significant increase, and the authors concluded that effects vary by condition, with more increases than reductions, and that the evidence is insufficient for strong conclusions.[4] Suzuki and colleagues included 23 studies and found more frequent prescribing via telemedicine for otitis media (pooled OR 1.26) and pharyngitis (pooled OR 1.16), with no significant difference for sinusitis, upper respiratory infection or UTI, while cautioning that the studies were substantially heterogeneous.[5]

Context also matters. In a pediatric primary care network with active stewardship, including learning collaborative videoconferences and clinician-level prescribing dashboards, guideline-concordant antibiotic management for respiratory infections occurred in 92.5% of telemedicine visits compared with 90.7% of in-person office visits.[6] A 2024 national claims study of children found telemedicine integrated within primary care was associated with lower rates of antibiotic receipt than visits with direct-to-consumer telemedicine companies.[14]

Where Telehealth Stewardship Gets Hard

Acknowledging where telehealth has genuine structural disadvantages is part of honest stewardship. Two specific gaps show up repeatedly in the literature and in clinical practice.

Limited Point-of-Care Testing

In a clinic, a rapid strep test, a urine dipstick, or a nasal swab can redirect a clinical decision within minutes. CDC notes that in telemedicine, laboratory or point-of-care tests may not be available for conditions where diagnostic testing is indicated, and that some conditions, such as otitis media, require a physical examination that may not be available through telemedicine.[17] The strep testing gap in the large insurer study shows what this looks like in practice.[2] For some conditions the gap is smaller: CDC cites a primary care network study of uncomplicated UTI that found more appropriate prescribing among virtual visits than office visits.[17] For others, the absence of testing or examination creates real uncertainty about bacterial versus viral causes.

Risk of Over-Treating Non-Specific Urinary Symptoms

Asymptomatic bacteriuria means bacteria found in the urine of a person without urinary symptoms. The IDSA's 2019 guideline notes that antimicrobial treatment of asymptomatic bacteriuria has been recognized as an important contributor to inappropriate antimicrobial use, which promotes antimicrobial resistance.[9] It recommends against screening for or treating asymptomatic bacteriuria in healthy premenopausal non-pregnant women and healthy postmenopausal women, and recommends screening and treatment in pregnant women and before endoscopic urologic procedures associated with mucosal trauma.[9] The risk in telehealth is that a patient who reports vague urinary discomfort, submits a urine culture showing bacterial growth, and receives an antibiotic prescription may have had asymptomatic bacteriuria rather than a true infection.

The fix is structured history-taking that asks directly about urinary symptoms such as painful urination, frequency and urgency, so that a culture result is read alongside the symptoms rather than on its own. Nonspecific complaints without those features should trigger a more careful diagnostic conversation, not an automatic prescription.

Under-Applying ABRS Criteria for Sinusitis

IDSA's 2012 guideline for acute bacterial rhinosinusitis (ABRS) recommends any of three clinical presentations for identifying bacterial rather than viral rhinosinusitis: persistent symptoms lasting 10 or more days without any evidence of clinical improvement; severe symptoms, meaning high fever (39°C [102°F] or higher) with purulent nasal discharge or facial pain, lasting at least 3 to 4 consecutive days at the beginning of illness; or "double-sickening," in which new fever, headache or increased nasal discharge follows a typical viral upper respiratory infection that lasted 5 to 6 days and was initially improving.[8] Presentations that fit none of these patterns do not meet the guideline's criteria for bacterial sinusitis.

The stewardship failure in telehealth, as in in-person care, is prescribing for patients with 5 or 6 days of nasal congestion and pressure who haven't met any of those thresholds. A well-structured telehealth intake that explicitly asks about symptom duration and pattern can help apply these criteria consistently.

What Effective Telehealth Stewardship Looks Like

The good news from the evidence base is that telehealth stewardship is achievable when programs build the right infrastructure. The published guidance points to four practical pillars.

CDC Core Elements

CDC first published the Core Elements of Outpatient Antibiotic Stewardship in 2016 with four core elements: commitment, action for policy and practice, tracking and reporting, and education and expertise.[15] A 2022 review proposed applying these four elements to primary care delivered by telehealth.[1] In 2026, CDC updated the 2016 Core Elements, placing greater emphasis on the role health systems can play while continuing to highlight stewardship at the clinic and clinician level.[16] CDC's 2026 telemedicine strategies include formalizing a stewardship commitment, establishing standards for tele-diagnosis and treatment, adapting stewardship interventions to the virtual workflow, tracking and reporting prescribing metrics, and providing targeted clinician and patient education.[17]

The clearest outcome data come from in-person care. A quasi-experimental study by Madaras-Kelly and colleagues in Veterans Health Administration emergency departments and primary care clinics, published in Clinical Infectious Diseases in 2021, found antibiotic prescribing for uncomplicated respiratory infections fell from 59.7% to 41.5% at intervention sites, compared with 73.5% to 67.2% at control sites; implementation of the core elements was associated with reduced antibiotic prescribing and a reduction in hospitalizations.[7] That study was not conducted in telehealth.

Delayed Prescribing Strategies

For conditions that usually resolve without treatment, CDC describes delayed prescribing, in which the clinician gives a postdated prescription or instructions to fill it after a predetermined period, and watchful waiting, which means symptomatic relief with a clear plan for follow-up. CDC calls both evidence-based approaches.[15] Its 2026 telemedicine strategies list delayed prescribing or watchful waiting among the evidence-based strategies to embed in telehealth platforms.[17]

Clinical Decision Support and Structured Criteria

Decision support tools, such as symptom-duration checklists and guideline criteria embedded in the visit workflow, are one way to make criteria-based prescribing routine. CDC lists clinical decision support for upper respiratory tract infections among the strategies to embed in telehealth platforms.[17] The trial evidence is mixed. A 2025 systematic review of information technology interventions for UTI prescribing included ten randomized or cluster randomized trials: four reported significant reductions in overall antibiotic prescribing, two showed significant increases in antibiotic appropriateness, and patient outcomes, usually secondary endpoints, showed no statistically significant differences.[10] A telehealth platform that prompts clinicians to confirm IDSA criteria before generating a prescription creates an audit trail and a structural guardrail at the same time.

Lab Pathways and Structured Follow-Up

When testing is indicated but cannot be done during a video visit, CDC recommends incorporating referral pathways and triage systems to in-person evaluation.[17] The key is building that step, and review of any test results, into the telehealth workflow rather than leaving it to the patient to initiate.

What This Means for Patients and Clinicians

The evidence does not support the view that telehealth is inherently reckless with antibiotics, nor the view that it performs as well as any in-person clinic by default. Both channels have strengths and gaps.

For conditions with well-defined clinical criteria that can be assessed by symptom history, such as sinusitis, telehealth can match or improve on in-person prescribing in some settings.[3] The UTI evidence is mixed: one primary care network found more appropriate prescribing among virtual visits for uncomplicated UTI,[17] while the large insurer study found higher antibiotic use for uncomplicated UTI at televisits.[2] Structured intake and guideline-embedded decision support are how a program closes that gap.

For conditions where testing is important to distinguish bacterial from viral causes, in-person care retains an advantage. A patient with 4 days of sore throat who is uncertain whether it is strep or viral benefits from a rapid strep test that a telehealth visit cannot provide, and televisits in one large program rarely included strep testing before antibiotics.[2] The clinically appropriate response in that case may be to direct the patient to an in-person visit or urgent care for testing before any antibiotic decision is made.

Appropriate triage, meaning deciding which conditions are suited to virtual evaluation and which require in-person workup, matters more than the care channel itself. A patient who calls a telehealth service for a possible strep throat and is appropriately directed to in-person testing, then treated based on a confirmed positive result, has received better stewardship care than one who receives an empiric antibiotic from an in-person clinic without confirmatory testing.

Both telehealth and in-person clinicians make antibiotic decisions. The quality of those decisions depends on clinical training, structured criteria, and the willingness to say "we need more information," not on which side of a screen the clinician sits.

Bottom Line

Published evidence through 2026 shows that telehealth antibiotic prescribing quality varies by condition and by setting. It is not a fixed property of the care channel. Strong programs use structured intake, clinical decision support, delayed prescribing where appropriate, referral pathways for testing, and active tracking. Programs that skip established criteria risk more prescribing than patients need. The difference between those two outcomes is not telehealth versus in-person. It is whether effective stewardship infrastructure exists.
