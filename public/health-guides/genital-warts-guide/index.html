Genital Warts

A physician's evidence-based guide to anogenital warts (condyloma acuminata): imiquimod, podofilox, sinecatechins, cryotherapy, HPV vaccination, and what actually works in 2026.

What is the best treatment for genital warts (HPV) in 2026?

Anogenital warts are a common sexually transmitted condition, and about 90% are caused by HPV types 6 or 11, which are nononcogenic (not cancer-causing) types (CDC 2021 STI Treatment Guidelines). CDC lists several recommended treatments and states that no definitive evidence shows any one is superior, so the choice depends on wart size, number and location, patient preference, cost, convenience, side effects and clinician experience. Patient-applied options are imiquimod cream (5% at bedtime three times a week for up to 16 weeks, or 3.75% nightly for up to 8 weeks), podofilox 0.5% solution or gel (twice daily for 3 days, then 4 days off, up to four cycles), and sinecatechins 15% ointment (Veregen) (three times daily for up to 16 weeks). In trials, imiquimod 2% or 5% cream cleared warts in 51% of HIV-negative patients versus 6% with placebo (Moore 2001), and sinecatechins 15% in 57.2% versus 33.7% with vehicle (Tatti 2008). Provider-applied options are cryotherapy, trichloroacetic or bichloroacetic acid (TCA or BCA) 80% to 90%, and surgical removal; in one randomized trial in 130 men, warts resolved in 88% with cryotherapy and 81% with TCA (Godley 1987). Warts often recur after any treatment, especially in the first 3 months. The Gardasil 9 vaccine is FDA-approved for ages 9 through 45 and prevents genital warts caused by HPV 6 and 11 (FDA label). Podofilox and sinecatechins should not be used during pregnancy, and CDC advises avoiding imiquimod in pregnancy until more data are available. A licensed clinician, accessible via telehealth, can prescribe the patient-applied treatments and coordinate provider-applied treatment when needed.

Genital warts, also called anogenital warts or condyloma acuminata, are a common sexually transmitted condition, and also one of the most misunderstood. This guide is a physician's evidence-based look at what causes genital warts, how they are diagnosed, and how the full menu of CDC-recommended treatments, from patient-applied imiquimod and podofilox to provider-applied cryotherapy and TCA, actually compares on clearance and recurrence. It also covers HPV vaccination, partner communication, and pregnancy considerations. Genital warts are common, treatable, and not a reflection of anyone's character or hygiene. What Are Genital Warts?

Genital warts can look quite different from person to person: they may be flat, dome-shaped, cauliflower-shaped, or on a stalk, often beginning as small flesh-colored bumps of 1 to 2 mm, and their color can range from white to pink, purple, red, or brown.[10] In women they commonly appear on the vulva, vagina, cervix, and around the anus; in men, on the penile shaft, scrotum, around the urethral opening, and around the anus.[10] The area around the vaginal opening is another common site.[1] Warts inside the anus are observed mostly in people who have had receptive anal intercourse.[1] Warts are usually painless, but depending on their size and location they can be painful or itchy.[1]

HPV types 6 and 11

About 90 percent of anogenital warts are caused by HPV types 6 or 11, which are nononcogenic (low-risk) types.[1] This is a reassuring distinction: CDC counseling guidance notes that the types of HPV that cause genital warts are different from the types that can cause cancer.[1] HPV types 16, 18, 31, 33, and 35 are only occasionally found in anogenital warts.[1] Women with genital warts do not need Pap tests more often than other women, though routine age-appropriate cervical cancer screening remains important regardless of wart history.[1]

Distinguishing genital warts from other lesions

Some anogenital lesions can resemble genital warts, and part of an accurate diagnosis is ruling them out.[1] One important example is condyloma lata, a manifestation of secondary syphilis, which can be diagnosed with blood (serologic) tests.[1] Normal skin variants and other benign bumps can also be mistaken for warts, which is one reason an examination by a clinician experienced with genital skin is worthwhile.

Prevalence and transmission

Genital HPV infection is very common: the majority of sexually active people get HPV at some time in their lives, and most HPV infections are self-limited and cause no symptoms or go unrecognized.[16] HPV types that cause genital warts can be passed to another person even without visible signs of warts, and sex partners tend to share HPV even when warts appear in only one partner or neither.[1] Condoms used consistently and correctly might lower the chance of passing on genital warts, but HPV can infect areas a condom does not cover.[1] Warts can develop months or years after HPV is acquired, so the time of infection cannot be determined with certainty.[1]

Diagnosis: How Warts Are Identified

Visual inspection is usually enough

Diagnosis of genital warts is usually made by visual inspection, and HPV testing is not recommended to diagnose warts.[1] An experienced clinician can usually identify them from a careful history and examination. Applying a 3 to 5% acetic acid solution (the acetowhite test) may help make warts easier to see.[10]

When to biopsy

Biopsy is not routinely needed. It is indicated when lesions look atypical (for example pigmented, indurated, fixed to underlying tissue, bleeding, or ulcerated), and it might also be needed when the diagnosis is uncertain, the lesions do not respond to standard therapy, or the disease worsens during therapy.[1] Squamous cell carcinomas arising in or resembling anogenital warts might occur more often in people with weakened immune systems, so suspicious lesions in this group need a biopsy to confirm the diagnosis.[1]

STI co-screening

Because genital warts can be sexually transmitted, people with genital warts benefit from testing for other STIs.[1] This is standard, non-judgmental practice, not an assumption about anyone's behavior, and a clinician can tailor which tests make sense.

What's Changed in 2024 to 2026

Genital wart management has been relatively stable. CDC's current treatment recommendations are the 2021 STI Treatment Guidelines,[1] so the points below are less about new treatments and more about vaccination eligibility, off-label options, and access.

HPV vaccination is approved through age 45. In 2018 the FDA expanded the approved age range for Gardasil 9 from 9 through 26 years to 9 through 45 years.[9] ACIP did not make vaccination routine across that whole range: it recommends shared clinical decision-making for some adults aged 27 through 45, because vaccine effectiveness is lower in older age groups because of prior infections.[9]

Off-label options remain second line. CDC lists intralesional interferon, photodynamic therapy, and topical cidofovir as alternative regimens with fewer efficacy data, and podophyllin resin is no longer recommended because safer regimens are available.[1] None of these has replaced the recommended treatments, so be cautious about products marketed for genital warts without that evidence.

Telehealth access. Imiquimod, podofilox, and sinecatechins can be prescribed through a synchronous telehealth visit with a clinician licensed where the patient is located, following a history and, when feasible, photo-assisted visual assessment, lowering the friction for patients who might otherwise delay care due to embarrassment. Provider-applied treatments (cryotherapy, TCA, and surgical options) still require an in-person visit.

Taken together, CDC's patient-applied and provider-applied categories remain the backbone of care, and the first step, a prescription for a patient-applied treatment, can often be taken without an in-person visit.

Patient-Applied Treatments

Imiquimod 5% cream

Imiquimod is a topically active immune enhancer that stimulates production of interferon and other cytokines.[1] A clinical review describes it as activating immune cells through a toll-like receptor,[10] although the FDA label states that its mechanism of action in treating genital warts is unknown.[11] The 5% cream is approved for external genital and perianal warts in immunocompetent patients 12 years of age and older.[11]

A quantitative systematic review of randomized trials in HIV-negative patients found complete clearance at the end of treatment in 51 percent of patients using imiquimod 2% or 5% cream compared with 6 percent with placebo, a number needed to treat of 2.2; imiquimod was not effective in one trial in HIV-positive patients.[2] In a pivotal multicenter trial of the 5% cream, 50 percent of patients cleared all treated warts compared with 11 percent with vehicle cream.[4] In the FDA label's trial results, women cleared more often than men (72 percent versus 20 percent with vehicle in women, and 33 percent versus 5 percent in men).[11] The 5% cream is applied as a thin layer at bedtime three times a week, left on the skin for 6 to 10 hours, then removed with mild soap and water, until warts clear or for a maximum of 16 weeks.[11] CDC also lists a 3.75% cream applied once at bedtime every night for up to 8 weeks.[1] Imiquimod may weaken condoms and vaginal diaphragms, so using them at the same time is not recommended.[11]

Podofilox 0.5% solution or gel

Podofilox (podophyllotoxin) is a patient-applied antimitotic drug that causes wart necrosis.[1] It is applied to anogenital warts twice a day for 3 days, followed by 4 days of no therapy, and the cycle can be repeated until no visible wart tissue remains, for a maximum of four cycles.[1][12] The total wart area treated should not exceed 10 cm2, and the total volume used should be limited to 0.5 mL per day.[1] The label states that it is not indicated for warts on mucous membranes.[12] In the label's two clinical trials of the 0.5% gel, 38.4 percent and 25.6 percent of patients had complete clearing of wart tissue at the end of 4 weeks.[12]

Sinecatechins 15% ointment

Sinecatechins (brand name Veregen) is a patient-applied extract of green tea leaves whose active product is catechins.[1][6] The label describes it as a partially purified fraction of the water extract of green tea leaves, with epigallocatechin gallate making up more than 55 percent, and states that how it clears warts is unknown.[13] In a randomized controlled trial, complete clearance occurred in 57.2 percent of patients using the 15% ointment versus 33.7 percent with vehicle.[3] Recurrence during the 12-week follow-up was 6.5 percent with the 15% ointment, but it was 8.8 percent among vehicle patients, so the trial does not show that the drug itself prevents recurrence.[3] The ointment is applied three times a day, about a 0.5 cm strand to each wart, until all warts clear but for no longer than 16 weeks, and it does not need to be washed off between applications.[1][13] Genital, anal, and oral sexual contact should be avoided while the ointment is on the skin, and CDC does not recommend it for people with HIV, other immunocompromising conditions, or genital herpes.[1] Application site reactions are common: they occurred in 87.7 percent of patients using the 15% ointment in the trial.[3] It is approved for immunocompetent adults 18 and older.[13]

Provider-Applied Treatments

Cryotherapy

Cryotherapy is a provider-administered treatment, usually with liquid nitrogen, that destroys warts by thermal-induced cytolysis.[1] Pain during and after application of the liquid nitrogen, followed by necrosis and sometimes blistering, is common.[1] In a randomized, observer-blind trial of 130 men with penile warts, warts resolved in 88 percent treated with cryotherapy compared with 81 percent treated with TCA, with no significant difference in response, side effects, or recurrence between the two.[5] Persistent lightening or darkening of the skin can occur with ablative treatments.[1] Cryotherapy requires an in-person visit with trained staff and equipment.

Trichloroacetic acid (TCA) or bichloroacetic acid (BCA)

TCA and BCA 80 to 90 percent solutions are provider-applied acids that destroy warts by chemical coagulation of proteins.[1] A small amount is applied only to the warts and allowed to dry, because the acid can spread rapidly and damage adjacent tissue if applied excessively, and treatment can be repeated weekly if necessary.[1] In the trial described above, warts resolved in 81 percent of men treated with TCA.[5] CDC lists TCA or BCA among the options for warts on the external genitals, inside the anus, in the vagina, and on the cervix.[1] A clinical review notes that TCA's low systemic absorption allows safe application during pregnancy.[10]

Surgical options: curettage, electrosurgery, laser

Surgical removal can be done by tangential scissor excision, tangential shave excision, curettage, laser, or electrosurgery.[1] It has the advantage of eliminating the majority of warts at a single visit, although recurrence can occur.[1] These options are generally used for extensive wart burden, warts that have not responded to other treatment, or when a tissue sample is needed for pathology, and they are typically performed by a dermatologist, gynecologist, or surgeon rather than a primary care clinician.

Recurrence: The Persistent Challenge

Why warts recur

Every genital wart treatment, patient-applied or provider-applied, removes visible warts or helps the body clear them, but available therapies might reduce, but probably do not eradicate, HPV infectivity.[1] HPV might remain present and can still be transmitted to partners even after the warts are gone, and genital warts often recur after treatment, especially during the first 3 months.[1] Over time, people who acquire HPV usually clear the infection on their own.[16]

Which treatments have the lowest recurrence

Recurrence figures from individual trials need careful reading. In the trial comparing cryotherapy with TCA, early recurrence occurred in 39 percent of men treated with cryotherapy and 36 percent treated with TCA.[5] In the pivotal imiquimod trial, 13 percent of patients whose warts cleared with the 5% cream had a recurrence, compared with 10 percent of vehicle patients.[4] In the sinecatechins trial, recurrence was 6.5 percent with the 15% ointment and 8.8 percent with vehicle.[3] Because recurrence among vehicle patients was similar in both of those trials, and because the trials used different follow-up periods, these numbers do not show that any one treatment prevents recurrence better than another. A systematic review did find that clearance with no recurrence during follow-up occurred in 37 percent of patients treated with imiquimod 5% cream versus 4 percent with placebo, a measure that reflects both clearing and staying clear.[2]

When to retreat

Recurrent warts after apparently successful treatment are common and are not a sign that something is wrong with the patient's immune system. Warts typically respond within 3 months of therapy, and a new treatment should be selected when no substantial improvement is seen after a complete course.[1] Retreatment with the same or a different agent is standard practice, and many patients need more than one course of therapy over time before warts resolve durably. Combination approaches are sometimes used for patients with frequent recurrence, though comparative trial data on combination regimens specifically is limited.

Recurrence is the norm, not the failure

Because available treatments probably do not eradicate HPV, seeing warts return after successful clearance is common, expected, and not a sign that anything is wrong with a patient's body.[1] Retreatment is standard, routine care, not an escalation of concern.

HPV Vaccination: Prevention Is the Cure

Gardasil 9 coverage

Gardasil 9 protects against nine HPV types (6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58) and is FDA-approved for the prevention of genital warts caused by HPV types 6 and 11.[14] A 2018 CDC review reported that since the end of 2016 it has been the only HPV vaccine sold in the United States.[7] Because types 6 and 11 cause about 90 percent of anogenital warts,[1] the vaccine targets the main causes of genital warts as well as the high-risk types linked to cervical, anal, and other HPV-associated cancers. It prevents new HPV infections but does not treat existing HPV infections or warts.[15]

Adult catch-up vaccination

ACIP recommends HPV vaccination for everyone through age 26 who was not adequately vaccinated when younger, and three doses are recommended for people who start at ages 15 through 26.[15] Vaccination is not recommended for everyone older than 26: for adults 27 through 45, clinicians can consider discussing it with the people most likely to benefit, since it provides less benefit in this range, partly because more people have already been exposed to HPV.[15] No prevaccination testing, such as a Pap or HPV test, is recommended to decide whether vaccination is appropriate.[9] A history of genital warts is not a reason to skip vaccination: CDC states that HPV vaccines can be administered regardless of history of anogenital warts.[16] HPV vaccine is not recommended during pregnancy.[15]

Vaccine impact on genital wart incidence

Population-level surveillance has documented large declines in genital warts after HPV vaccination. In Australia, whose national program started in mid-2007, the proportion of new sexual health clinic patients diagnosed with genital warts fell by 92.6 percent among women under 21 and by 72.6 percent among women aged 21 to 30 by 2011, and by 81.8 percent and 51.1 percent among heterosexual men in the same age groups, declines in men that the authors attribute probably to herd immunity.[8] No significant decline was seen in women or heterosexual men over 30.[8] In the United States, decreases in genital warts were seen at the same time as decreases in vaccine-type HPV during the first decade of the vaccination program.[7]

HPV vaccination through age 45

Gardasil 9 is FDA-approved for ages 9 through 45.[14] HPV vaccine is recommended routinely at 11 or 12 and through 26 for anyone not adequately vaccinated, and adults 27 through 45 can decide with their clinician, since benefit is lower once more people have been exposed to HPV.[15]

Partner Notification and Testing

People with genital warts should inform current partners about the diagnosis.[1] Because sex partners tend to share HPV even when warts appear in only one partner or neither, and because the time of HPV acquisition cannot be definitively determined, partner notification is about shared awareness and shared decision-making rather than establishing blame or a precise transmission timeline.[1]

HPV testing of sex partners of people with genital warts is not recommended, but partners might benefit from a physical examination to detect genital warts and from tests for other STIs.[1] CDC makes no recommendation about informing future sex partners.[1] A useful, non-alarming way to raise the topic is to frame it as routine sexual health information sharing: "I was diagnosed with genital warts, which is caused by a very common virus called HPV. There isn't a recommended HPV test for partners, but a checkup and STI testing could be worthwhile, and any bumps or changes are worth getting looked at." Partners who are not already vaccinated against HPV can discuss vaccination with their own clinician.

Pregnancy and Genital Warts

Anogenital warts can proliferate and become friable (fragile and prone to bleeding) during pregnancy.[1] Removal of warts during pregnancy can be considered, but resolution might be incomplete or poor until pregnancy is complete.[1] Treatment decisions during pregnancy require particular attention to medication safety, since several standard topical treatments should not be used during pregnancy.

Treatments to avoid in pregnancy

Podofilox and sinecatechins should not be used during pregnancy, and imiquimod, which appears to pose low risk, should be avoided until more data are available.[1] When removal is chosen during pregnancy, the remaining options are provider-applied: cryotherapy, TCA or BCA, or surgical removal. A clinical review notes that TCA's low systemic absorption allows safe application during pregnancy and describes cryotherapy as the therapy of choice for pregnant women with multiple warts.[10] Decisions should be made together with the obstetric team.

Cesarean delivery should not be performed solely to prevent transmission of HPV to the newborn. It is indicated for women with anogenital warts if the pelvic outlet is obstructed or if vaginal delivery would result in excessive bleeding.[1] Rarely, HPV types 6 and 11 can cause respiratory papillomatosis (wart-like growths in the airway) in infants and children; the route of transmission is not completely understood, and pregnant women with warts should be counseled about this low risk.[1] Decisions about delivery mode should be individualized with the patient's obstetric team based on wart size, location, and extent.

What Doesn't Work: Home Remedies and OTC Claims

No over-the-counter wart remover, herbal supplement, or "natural" remedy marketed for genital warts has rigorous clinical trial evidence supporting its use on genital or anal skin. This is an area where unproven products circulate widely, in part because genital warts are a condition patients often want to address privately without a clinical visit.

Do not use OTC wart removers on genital skin. Over-the-counter salicylic acid wart removers, sold for common skin warts on hands and feet, are formulated for thicker, non-mucosal skin and are not intended for use on genital or anal skin. They can cause significant chemical burns and tissue damage on this thinner, more sensitive skin, and should never be substituted for the CDC-recommended treatments described in this guide.

A brief evidence summary of commonly marketed non-prescription approaches for genital warts:

  • Duct tape occlusion: has limited evidence for common skin warts on hands and feet but no controlled trial evidence for genital warts, and is not recommended for use on genital or anal skin
  • Apple cider vinegar or tea tree oil applied directly: no controlled trial evidence of efficacy and meaningful risk of chemical irritation or burns on sensitive genital skin
  • Oral immune-boosting supplements: no plausible mechanism specific to HPV clearance and no trial evidence supporting use for genital warts
  • Salicylic acid OTC wart removers: formulated for keratinized skin on hands and feet; risk of significant burns if used on genital or anal skin
  • Waiting without any evaluation: while some genital warts do resolve without treatment, there is no way to know in advance which ones will, and skipping evaluation also means missing the opportunity to rule out atypical or concerning lesions and to discuss STI co-screening and partner considerations

The takeaway: effective genital wart treatment is well established, several options are accessible via telehealth without the delay or cost of unproven alternatives, and self-treatment with products designed for other types of skin warts carries real risk of harm on genital tissue.

Red Flags: When to See a Clinician Urgently

Most genital warts are a benign, common, and manageable condition that does not require urgent care. However, certain features warrant prompt evaluation rather than routine, scheduled care:

  • Bleeding, ulceration, or a lesion that is fixed to underlying tissue, which can indicate a need for biopsy to rule out dysplasia or malignancy rather than a typical wart
  • Rapidly growing or unusually large lesions, particularly in an immunocompromised patient, where atypical growth patterns and malignant transformation are more of a concern
  • Signs of secondary bacterial infection around a wart or treatment site, including increasing pain, spreading redness, warmth, or pus
  • Urinary obstruction or difficulty urinating, which can occur with warts near or within the urethral meatus and warrants prompt urologic evaluation
  • Warts appearing for the first time in a person with known HIV or other significant immunocompromise, since atypical presentations and treatment resistance are more common and closer specialist follow-up is often warranted
  • Any new pigmented, indurated, or unusual-appearing genital lesion that does not fit the classic appearance of a wart, which should be evaluated before assuming a wart diagnosis

None of these red flags are typical of straightforward genital warts, which are usually soft, mobile, non-tender, and slow-growing. Any significant deviation from that pattern is worth a prompt clinical evaluation rather than continuing with standard, at-home wart treatment.

HPV testing is not recommended for partners, but a partner might benefit from an exam and testing for other STIs, and should let a clinician know about any bumps or changes.[1] Because sex partners tend to share HPV and the time of infection cannot be determined, this conversation is best framed as shared health information rather than an attempt to assign blame or pinpoint exactly when or how the infection occurred.[1]

Not necessarily. People who acquire HPV usually clear the infection on their own over time,[16] and warts can resolve even without treatment.[1] Some people have recurrences for a while, especially in the first months after treatment,[1] and there is no way to predict an individual's timeline in advance, but a diagnosis of genital warts is not automatically a lifelong condition.
