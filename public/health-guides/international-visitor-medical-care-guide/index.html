Medical Care for International Visitors

A physician's evidence-based guide to getting medical help in the United States: 911 versus ER versus urgent care versus telehealth, medication access, and what to do when you or your family gets sick during a trip.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: August 14, 2026
22 min read

What should international visitors do if they get sick in the United States?

In 2024 there were 72.4 million international visitor arrivals to the United States (U.S. National Travel and Tourism Office), and some visitors get sick during the trip. The US has no universal healthcare system, so visitors navigate four main access points: 911 for life-threatening emergencies, the emergency department for genuine emergencies, urgent care for same-day non-emergencies, and telehealth for conditions that do not require an in-person exam. Prices are not standardized; uninsured or self-pay patients must be given a good faith estimate when they schedule care or ask for one, and can dispute a bill that is at least $400 above it (CMS). Under Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act, covered entities such as hospitals must provide language assistance free of charge and offer a qualified interpreter when one is requested (HHS Office for Civil Rights). Federal law requires a prescription from a practitioner licensed by law to administer the drug, so plan on a US-licensed clinician writing a new one (21 U.S.C. 353(b)(1)), and foreign visitors may bring up to 50 dosage units of controlled substances for personal use in their original labeled containers (DEA guidance). Travelers’ diarrhea (attack rates 30 to 70 percent of travelers over two weeks, depending on destination and season; CDC Yellow Book), respiratory infections, UTIs, minor injuries, and prescription refills can often be handled outside the ER.

Key Takeaways

  • US care is not free at the point of care, and prices are not standardized. If you are uninsured or paying for yourself, a provider must give you a good faith estimate when you schedule care or ask for one, and a bill at least $400 above that estimate can be disputed through a federal process.[1]
  • Call 911 for life-threatening symptoms: chest pain, sudden severe difficulty breathing, stroke signs (face droop, arm weakness, speech difficulty), major trauma, uncontrolled bleeding, or altered consciousness. The federal No Surprises Act protects insured US patients from balance billing for emergency care regardless of network status.[2]
  • Plan on needing a new US prescription. Federal law says a prescription drug is dispensed only on the prescription of a practitioner licensed by law to administer it, so a US-licensed clinician usually has to write a new one; for chronic non-controlled medications, a synchronous telehealth visit is one route.[3]
  • Foreign visitors may bring up to 50 dosage units of controlled substances into the US for personal use, in the original container with a label that shows the drug's chemical or trade name and schedule symbol; declare all medications to US Customs and Border Protection.[4]
  • Travelers’ diarrhea attack rates run 30 to 70 percent of travelers over a two-week trip, depending on destination and season. Untreated bacterial diarrhea usually lasts 3 to 7 days, and antibiotics shorten it by about 1 to 2 days when the bacterium is susceptible.[5]
  • Under Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act, covered entities such as hospitals must provide language assistance free of charge and offer a qualified interpreter when interpretation is requested; approximately 68 million people in the United States speak a language other than English at home.[6]
  • Heat stroke is a genuine risk for visitors to the American Sunbelt. It is a 911 emergency, and rapid cooling comes first: CDC says an ice bath cools fastest and that cooling should not be delayed while emergency help is called.[7]

In 2024 there were 72.4 million international visitor arrivals to the United States, and Western Europe was the largest source region, with about 13.1 million.[8] Some of those visitors get sick during the trip and end up navigating an unfamiliar, fragmented healthcare system for the first time.

This guide is a physician's attempt to make that navigation less painful. It explains how US care is actually organized, how paying for care works, how to get medication when your foreign prescription does not work here, what your language-access rights are, and what to do for the common illnesses that most often send visitors looking for a doctor. Claims are cited to federal statutes and agencies, CDC, specialty guidelines and peer-reviewed journals. Nothing in this guide is a substitute for evaluation by a US-licensed clinician when your symptoms are serious.

How the US Healthcare System Actually Works

Before getting into what to do when you get sick, understand this: the US does not have a universal healthcare system. Unlike the UK's NHS, Canada's provincial systems, or most European models, care in the US is delivered by a patchwork of private hospitals, physician groups, urgent-care chains, retail clinics, and telehealth companies. If you do not have insurance that covers you in the US, expect to pay for your care yourself.

For an international visitor, this has three important consequences:

  • Emergency care is required; free care is not. Under the Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act (EMTALA), Medicare-participating hospitals that offer emergency services must provide a medical screening examination and stabilizing treatment for an emergency medical condition regardless of an individual's ability to pay.[9] The CMS summary of EMTALA covers screening, stabilization and transfer; it does not say the care is free.
  • Check your travel or visitor insurance before you need it. Read the policy for what it covers in the US and whether it pays the provider directly or reimburses you after you pay.
  • The right level of care can differ from what you would use at home. In the US, urgent care, retail clinics and telehealth handle many minor problems that do not need an emergency department.

Where to Go: 911, ER, Urgent Care, Telehealth, or Retail Clinic

There are five levels of urgent-care access in the US. Choosing correctly is both a clinical decision and a financial one.

911 (emergency medical services). Call for life-threatening or limb-threatening problems: chest pain, sudden severe difficulty breathing, stroke symptoms (face droop, arm weakness, slurred speech, sudden confusion), major trauma, uncontrolled bleeding, seizures, anaphylaxis, or loss of consciousness. 911 is a free call from any US phone.

Emergency department (ER). For genuine emergencies where waiting until morning is unsafe: severe abdominal pain, high fever with confusion, deep lacerations needing repair within hours, suspected fractures with deformity, severe dehydration, severe asthma or COPD flares. Under the No Surprises Act (effective January 1, 2022), insured US patients are protected from balance billing for emergency services regardless of whether the provider or facility is in-network.[2] The CMS page describes these protections for people with health coverage.

Urgent care. Same-day walk-in care for problems that need attention today but are not life-threatening: fevers, minor infections, sprains, minor cuts, UTIs, sore throat, pink eye, mild-to-moderate asthma flares. Hours vary by clinic.

Telehealth (video visit). For conditions where an in-person exam is not required: prescription refills for stable chronic conditions, uncomplicated UTIs in women, upper respiratory infections, sinus infections, seasonal allergies, minor skin rashes, medication questions. Telehealth cannot replace an in-person exam for problems that require palpation, imaging, or lab testing. Controlled substances are a special case: federal DEA telemedicine flexibilities, extended through December 31, 2026, allow DEA-registered practitioners to prescribe Schedule II to V medications by audio-video telemedicine without a prior in-person evaluation, but the prescription must still meet DEA rules and federal and state law, and the clinician decides.[10]

Retail clinic (CVS MinuteClinic, Walgreens, etc.). Nurse-practitioner-led walk-in care at pharmacy chains. Limited scope but useful for vaccinations, strep tests, flu tests, minor infections, and prescription refills.

What Care Actually Costs Without US Insurance

This guide does not list typical cash prices. We could not retrieve a federal or state source that publishes standard cash prices for an ER, urgent care, retail clinic or telehealth visit, and prices differ from one facility to the next. Ask what a visit will cost before any non-emergency care. Federal rules give uninsured and self-pay patients these protections:

Good faith estimates. If you are uninsured or paying for yourself, a provider or facility must give you a good faith estimate once you schedule an item or service, and also when you ask for one. If a provider's bill is at least $400 more than the expected charges on that estimate, you can use the federal patient-provider dispute resolution process.[1]

Hospital financial assistance. Under Internal Revenue Code section 501(r)(4), a hospital organization must establish a written financial assistance policy for each hospital facility it operates, including eligibility criteria, whether assistance includes free or discounted care, and how to apply.[11] Ask the hospital for its policy.

Prescription medications. Prices vary widely between drugs and between pharmacies, so ask the pharmacy for the cash price before it fills a prescription.

No Surprises Act: what it does and does not cover

The federal No Surprises Act, effective January 1, 2022, protects insured US patients from unexpected out-of-network bills for emergency services, air ambulance services, and non-emergency care from out-of-network providers at in-network facilities.[2] CMS describes these protections for people with health coverage. If you are uninsured or self-pay, the separate good faith estimate rules above apply to you.[1]

Language Access: Your Federal Rights

Approximately 68 million people in the United States speak a language other than English at home, and 8.2 percent of that group speak English less than very well.[6] Under Section 1557 of the Affordable Care Act, as implemented by the 2024 HHS final rule, covered entities, including recipients of Federal financial assistance and programs administered by HHS, must take reasonable steps to provide meaningful access to each individual with limited English proficiency (LEP).[6]

Concretely, this means:

  • Interpreters are free. Language assistance services must be provided free of charge, and when interpretation is requested, a covered entity such as a hospital must offer a qualified interpreter.[6]
  • Machine translation has limits. For critical documents, machine translations must be reviewed by a qualified human translator to ensure accuracy.[6]
  • Family and friends are not the default interpreters. Covered entities must not rely on unqualified adults to interpret except in limited situations, and the use of minor children to interpret is prohibited except in narrow emergencies.[6]

If you or a family member has limited English proficiency, ask for a qualified interpreter at any ER, urgent care, or hospital visit. If a covered provider does not provide one, you can file a complaint with the HHS Office for Civil Rights.[6]

Getting a Prescription in the US as a Visitor

For a deeper practical walkthrough (foreign-to-US brand mapping, telehealth bridging workflows, cash pharmacy pricing), see our satellite guide, Running Out of Medication in the US, and Chronic Conditions While Your Parents Visit the US for adult children hosting visiting parents with chronic conditions.

The short version: plan on needing a new US prescription. Federal law says a prescription drug "shall be dispensed only" on the prescription of "a practitioner licensed by law to administer such drug,"[3] so a US-licensed clinician usually needs to write a new prescription, either for the same medication under its US brand or generic name or, if the exact medication is not available in the US, for an FDA-approved alternative.

The FDA is explicit about unapproved products: "If a drug is approved for use in another country but is an unapproved new drug in the U.S. it is illegal to import."[12] Some medications sold abroad, including some combination products and traditional or herbal preparations, may not be approved or legal in the US.

What a US clinician can typically do for a visitor:

  • Write a new prescription for a chronic non-controlled medication (antihypertensive, statin, thyroid hormone, oral diabetes medication, most asthma inhalers, most SSRIs, birth control pills) after reviewing your existing regimen. A synchronous telehealth visit is usually sufficient.
  • Substitute a US-approved equivalent when the exact foreign brand is not sold here. For example, US pharmacies stock levothyroxine (generic), Synthroid, Levoxyl, and Unithroid brands, but not the European brand Eltroxin.
  • Bridge you to a short supply (typically 30 days) until you return home for continuing care.

What a US clinician generally cannot do for a short-term visitor:

  • Promise a controlled substance (opioids, benzodiazepines, stimulants including ADHD medications like Adderall, sleep medications like zolpidem, testosterone). Federal DEA telemedicine flexibilities, extended through December 31, 2026, allow DEA-registered practitioners to prescribe Schedule II to V medications by audio-video telemedicine without a prior in-person evaluation, but the prescription must still meet DEA rules and federal and state law, and the decision is the clinician's.[10]
  • Refill a medication that requires ongoing monitoring (warfarin needing INR checks, complex biologics with prior authorization) without appropriate labs or documentation.
  • Prescribe medications not FDA-approved in the US, even if legal in your home country.

What to bring to a US visit for medication continuity:

  • A written medication list with generic (not brand) names, doses, and frequency. The CDC Yellow Book advises travelers with chronic illnesses to carry a clinician letter on office letterhead that lists "medications prescribed (including generic names and doses)."[13]
  • Your original medication bottles or blister packs with legible labels.
  • A doctor's letter documenting your conditions, allergies, blood type, and medications (helpful but not required).

Bringing Medications Into the US

For non-controlled medicines, the FDA says it "will allow foreign nationals to bring or ship a 90-day supply of drug products."[12] Controlled substances have stricter DEA limits, and the DEA warns that medications can be confiscated and "can result in severe penalties if not approved for use in the U.S."[4]

General rules (all medications):

  • Keep medications in their original containers with legible labels.
  • Bring a copy of your prescription and, ideally, a doctor's letter listing the medication and condition.
  • Declare all medications to Customs and Border Protection at entry.
  • Keep medications in your carry-on. The CDC Yellow Book says all medications "should be carried in hand luggage rather than in checked baggage,"[13] and TSA allows "medically necessary liquids, medications and creams in excess of 3.4 ounces or 100 milliliters" in your carry-on bag, removed for separate screening.[14]

Controlled substances (DEA rules). Per DEA guidance, foreign visitors may bring controlled substances into the US for personal use if all of the following are true:[4]

  1. The medication is in its original container.
  2. The label includes the drug's chemical or trade name and schedule symbol, or (if not) includes the pharmacy or practitioner information and a prescription number.
  3. The quantity is no more than 50 dosage units of controlled substances, for personal use.
  4. The medication is declared to Customs and Border Protection.

Additional state laws may apply.[4]

Managing Chronic Conditions While Visiting

For a practical playbook aimed at adult children hosting visiting parents (pre-travel checklist, time-zone dosing for insulin and daily medications, levothyroxine timing, and jet lag vs warning signs), see the companion satellite guide, Chronic Conditions While Your Parents Visit the US. For visitors from India specifically (Indian brand names, DEA-controlled Indian medications, and the diaspora clinical risk picture), see Medical Care in the US for Visitors From India. For visitors from Mexico (Mexican brand mapping, Southwest border rules, Section 1557 language rights), see Medical Care in the US for Visitors From Mexico.

For visitors with diabetes, hypertension, thyroid disease, asthma, heart disease, or other chronic conditions, three planning steps prevent most trip-disrupting problems:

  1. Bring more medication than you think you need. The CDC Yellow Book advises making sure travelers with chronic illnesses have "medications for the entire trip plus extra in case of unexpected delays," in their original containers.[13]
  2. Bring a written summary. Diagnoses, current medications with generic names and doses, allergies, blood type, and the name and contact information of your regular doctor at home. This is invaluable if you end up in an ER.
  3. Know how to access care if something changes. For most stable chronic conditions, a telehealth visit with a US-licensed physician can resolve the common problems that come up on a two or three-week trip: a lost or damaged prescription bottle, a medication you forgot at home, running out sooner than expected, or a new mild symptom that needs a quick clinical opinion.

Travelers' Diarrhea and Food-Related Illness

The CDC Yellow Book calls travelers' diarrhea (TD) the most predictable travel-related illness, with attack rates of 30 to 70 percent of travelers during a two-week period, depending on the destination and season of travel.[5] Untreated, bacterial diarrhea usually lasts 3 to 7 days, and antibiotics shorten it by about 1 to 2 days when the cause is a bacterium susceptible to the antibiotic.[5] Those attack rates come from travel to higher-risk destinations, but diarrheal illness can also interrupt a US trip. Common causes in US visitors include norovirus outbreaks (cruises, buffets, hotels), foodborne bacterial infections (Salmonella, Campylobacter, occasional E. coli), and cyclosporiasis outbreaks tied to imported produce.

Severity definitions. A 2017 graded expert-panel guideline (Riddle et al., J Travel Med) developed new definitions of TD severity,[15] and the CDC Yellow Book defines severity by the effect on planned activities:[5]

  • Mild TD: diarrhea that is tolerable and does not interfere with planned activities.
  • Moderate TD: diarrhea that is distressing or interferes with planned activities.
  • Severe TD: diarrhea that is incapacitating or prevents planned activities; all dysentery (grossly bloody stools) is considered severe.

First-line management (mild TD): fluids, bland food as tolerated and, if needed, loperamide; the 2017 guideline says that for most mild cases "either increasing intake of fluids only or loperamide or bismuth subsalicylate may suffice."[15] On a typical US over-the-counter loperamide 2 mg label, the adult dose is 2 caplets (4 mg) after the first loose stool and 1 caplet (2 mg) after each later loose stool, with no more than 4 caplets (8 mg) in 24 hours. The label says not to use it with bloody or black stool, to ask a doctor first if you have a fever, and to stop and ask a doctor if diarrhea lasts more than 2 days.[16] No antibiotic.

Moderate to severe TD. The 2017 guideline finds strong evidence that antibiotic treatment works in most cases of moderate to severe TD.[15] The BMJ 2016 review notes that a short course (1 to 3 days) of antibiotics taken at the onset of TD reduces illness duration from 3 days to 1.5 days.[17] The CDC Yellow Book lists azithromycin as first-line empiric treatment in Southeast Asia or other areas where fluoroquinolone-resistant bacteria are suspected, and as the preferred treatment for dysentery or febrile diarrhea.[5] Regimens listed by the CDC Yellow Book:

  • Azithromycin 1,000 mg as a single or divided dose, or 500 mg once daily for 3 days.[5]
  • Ciprofloxacin 750 mg as a single dose, or 500 mg twice daily for 3 days.[5] Fluoroquinolone resistance limits its usefulness in many destinations, particularly South and Southeast Asia, where both Campylobacter infection and fluoroquinolone resistance are prevalent.[5]
  • Rifaximin 200 mg three times daily for 3 days, for TD caused by noninvasive strains of E. coli. CDC advises against it when Campylobacter, Salmonella, Shigella or another cause of invasive diarrhea is suspected.[5]

Combining an antibiotic with loperamide. The CDC Yellow Book notes that antibiotic regimens can be combined with loperamide (4 mg initially, then 2 mg after each loose stool, not to exceed 16 mg in a 24-hour period),[5] a clinician-directed maximum that is higher than the 8 mg limit on the over-the-counter label.[16] CDC also says antimotility agents alone are not recommended for bloody diarrhea or for diarrhea with fever.[5]

Red flags that warrant urgent in-person evaluation:

  • Blood in the stool (dysentery)
  • High fever (temperature at or above 39°C or 102.2°F)
  • Signs of dehydration: dry mouth, minimal urination, dizziness on standing, rapid heart rate
  • Symptoms lasting more than 7 days
  • Immunocompromised state, pregnancy, age under 3 months or over 70

For adults with moderate TD and no red flags, a telehealth visit can be a reasonable first step, with oral rehydration guidance and a follow-up plan; the clinician decides whether an antibiotic is appropriate.

One risk to be aware of. Travelers, in particular travelers to South and Southeast Asia, who develop TD and take antibiotics are at risk for colonization with extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing Enterobacteriaceae (ESBL-PE).[18] Carriage is most often transient,[18] but it can affect later antibiotic choices if you develop a UTI. Reserve antibiotics for moderate to severe cases and mention recent travel to any US clinician who sees you.

Respiratory Infections, Sinus, and Ear Problems

Long-haul flights, hotel HVAC systems, dry cabin air, and dense-crowd exposure at US airports and attractions combine to produce a lot of upper respiratory illness in visitors. Most of it is viral.

What's usually viral (self-limited): sore throat without high fever or exudate; runny nose, congestion, cough lasting up to 10 days; low-grade fever (under 38.3°C or 101°F) for 1 to 3 days; post-flight ear pressure and mild ear discomfort. Management is supportive: fluids, rest, acetaminophen or ibuprofen, saline nasal irrigation, and decongestants if there are no contraindications (uncontrolled hypertension, closed-angle glaucoma). Antibiotics do not help viral URIs.

When to consider a clinician visit (telehealth or urgent care):

  • Symptoms lasting more than 10 days without improvement, or initially improving then worsening (the "double-sickening" pattern), which raises the possibility of a bacterial sinus infection or secondary bacterial pneumonia.
  • Facial pain over the sinuses with fever and purulent nasal discharge for more than 10 days.
  • Ear pain with fever, especially in a child.
  • Cough with green or bloody sputum, or shortness of breath at rest.
  • Wheezing in a person without a prior asthma diagnosis.

Red flags that warrant urgent in-person or ER care: difficulty breathing at rest, blue lips or fingertips, oxygen saturation below 92 percent on a home pulse oximeter, high fever (over 39.5°C or 103°F) not responding to acetaminophen or ibuprofen, chest pain with breathing or cough, confusion or severe headache with stiff neck.

Most visitors with an uncomplicated URI, sinusitis, or minor ear infection can be evaluated by telehealth and, when appropriate, prescribed an antibiotic if a bacterial infection is likely.

Urinary Tract and Yeast Infections

Urinary tract infections and vaginal yeast infections are common trip disruptors, particularly among women. Both are highly treatable and both can typically be handled through telehealth in most US states without an in-person exam.

Uncomplicated UTI in women (cystitis). Typical symptoms are burning with urination (dysuria), urinary frequency, urgency, and lower pelvic discomfort. Per the 2010 IDSA and European Society for Microbiology and Infectious Diseases guidelines by Gupta and colleagues, appropriate first-line antibiotics for acute uncomplicated cystitis in healthy, non-pregnant, premenopausal women are:[19][20]

  • Nitrofurantoin 100 mg twice daily for 5 days
  • Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole 160/800 mg twice daily for 3 days (where local resistance is under 20 percent)
  • Fosfomycin 3 g as a single dose

A 2018 randomized trial in JAMA by Huttner and colleagues (513 women) found that 5 days of nitrofurantoin (100 mg three times a day in the trial) gave clinical resolution through day 28 in 70 percent of patients, compared with 58 percent after a single 3 g dose of fosfomycin.[21] The IDSA/ESCMID guideline also says fosfomycin "appears to have inferior efficacy compared with standard short-course regimens."[19] Fluoroquinolones (such as ciprofloxacin) should be reserved for situations in which other agents are not appropriate.[20][19]

When a UTI needs in-person or ER care: fever above 38°C or 100.4°F, flank pain (suggesting pyelonephritis), nausea and vomiting, visible blood in the urine, symptoms in a man, pregnancy, or symptoms not resolving within 48 hours of starting an antibiotic.

Vaginal yeast infection (vulvovaginal candidiasis). Typical symptoms include itching, vaginal soreness, pain with sex, burning with urination, and abnormal vaginal discharge.[22] Over-the-counter miconazole or clotrimazole vaginal antifungals (1- to 14-day courses, depending on the product) are sold at US pharmacies, and a US clinician can prescribe oral fluconazole 150 mg as a single dose for uncomplicated infection.[22] Severe or recurrent infections need a longer regimen; for severe infection, CDC lists two 150 mg fluconazole doses taken 72 hours apart.[22] Telehealth is appropriate when symptoms are classic and there are no red flags.

Heat Illness in the American Sunbelt

Visitors from cooler climates consistently underestimate US summer heat. This is a genuine cause of hospitalization and death at national parks and popular summer destinations.

A CDC-led retrospective study at Grand Canyon National Park (April through September, 2004 to 2009) documented 474 nonfatal and 6 fatal heat-related illness cases; almost all (90 percent) were hiking, 40 percent required helicopter evacuation, and most (84 percent) were US residents.[23] The highest illness rates were seen in May, and summer temperatures at the bottom of the canyon can exceed 120°F (44°C).[23]

Heat exhaustion (body temperature normal or mildly to moderately elevated[7]): headache, nausea, dizziness, weakness, irritability, thirst, heavy sweating, and decreased urine output.[24] Management: move to a cool environment, remove excess clothing, drink cool water or oral rehydration solution, and cool the skin with cold compresses or cold water on the head, face and neck.[24]

Heat stroke is a 911 emergency. The CDC Yellow Book says a presumptive diagnosis is made in the field when body temperature is 104°F or higher, with marked alteration of mental status, including delirium, convulsions, or coma.[7] Symptoms include confusion, slurred speech, hot dry skin or profuse sweating, seizures, and loss of consciousness, and heat stroke can be fatal if treatment is delayed.[24]

Rapid cooling comes first. CDC advises immersion in cool or cold water where possible ("An ice bath cools fastest") and says to activate emergency medical services "but do not delay cooling measures."[7] A 2026 NEJM Evidence review describes exertional heat illness as a leading yet preventable cause of morbidity and mortality in active populations.[25]

Prevention (CDC guidance):[7]

  • Drink regularly. CDC notes that reliance on thirst alone is not sufficient to prevent mild dehydration, but forcing water when you are not thirsty raises the risk of hyponatremia.
  • Avoid strenuous outdoor activity during the hottest part of the day.
  • Wear lightweight, loose, light-colored clothing that also protects you from the sun.
  • Recognize early warning signs and stop activity immediately.
  • Know that many medications can raise heat-illness risk, including diuretics, beta blockers, anticholinergic drugs, antipsychotics, tricyclic antidepressants, and antihistamines with anticholinergic properties.[26]

Popular summer destinations that regularly produce heat-illness visits from tourists include Grand Canyon, Zion, Death Valley, Las Vegas outdoor tours, Phoenix and Scottsdale in July and August, Big Bend, Joshua Tree, Orlando theme parks in July and August, and any Southern city heat wave. CDC lists infants and children and adults over age 65 among the people who may be more at risk from heat.[27]

High-Altitude Illness in the Rockies

Visitors flying directly into Denver (elevation 5,280 feet), Aspen (7,908 feet), Vail (8,150 feet), Yellowstone (Old Faithful area ~7,300 feet), or similar destinations can develop acute mountain sickness (AMS); CDC reports AMS in 25 percent of all visitors sleeping above 2,450 meters (8,000 feet) in Colorado.[28] AMS is generally mild and self-limited but can escalate to high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE) or high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE), both life-threatening.

CDC Yellow Book guidance puts any unacclimatized traveler going to a sleeping altitude of 2,450 meters (8,000 feet) or higher at risk, and illness can occur at lower altitudes too.[28]

AMS symptoms (onset usually 2 to 12 hours after arrival at high altitude):[28] headache is the cardinal symptom, usually with one or more of loss of appetite, dizziness, fatigue, or nausea.[28]

Management of mild AMS. Stop ascending. Do not go higher until symptoms resolve. Rest, and take acetaminophen or ibuprofen for headache. If symptoms worsen despite rest, descend; AMS improves rapidly with a descent of 300 meters (1,000 feet) or more.[28] For prevention, the CDC Yellow Book acetazolamide dose is 125 mg every 12 hours, beginning the day before ascent; to date, the only dose studied for treatment is 250 mg (2 doses taken 8 hours apart).[28]

Evidence on acetazolamide prophylaxis. A randomized trial in NEJM Evidence by Furian and colleagues gave acetazolamide 375 mg per day, starting 24 hours before a 2-day stay at 3,100 meters. In patients with COPD, altitude-related adverse health effects occurred in 76 percent with placebo vs 49 percent with acetazolamide (number needed to treat, 4). In healthy lowlanders aged 40 or older, acute mountain sickness occurred in 32 percent vs 22 percent (number needed to treat, 10).[29]

High-altitude cerebral edema (HACE): altered mental status, ataxia, confusion, and drowsiness at altitude.[28] HACE is rare, especially below 4,300 meters (14,000 feet).[28] This is a medical emergency: call 911 or get to an emergency department; in remote areas, CDC advises starting descent for anyone suspected of having HACE. The CDC Yellow Book dexamethasone dose for HACE is 8 mg once, then 4 mg every 6 hours.[28]

High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE): early signs include chest congestion, cough, and exaggerated shortness of breath on exertion.[28] HAPE is also a medical emergency. In most circumstances descent is urgent and mandatory, although mild HAPE can be treated with oxygen over 2 to 4 days and bedrest at the current altitude where oxygen is available. The adult nifedipine dose for prevention or treatment is 30 mg of the sustained-release version every 12 hours or 20 mg every 8 hours.[28]

For visitors flying directly to Denver from sea level with plans to ski at 9,000 to 12,000 feet the next day, asking a clinician before the trip about acetazolamide prophylaxis is a reasonable preventive step. The CDC Yellow Book says people allergic to sulfa antibiotics can take acetazolamide, with caution for anyone with a history of anaphylaxis or multiple drug allergies.[28]

When Your Child Gets Sick on a US Trip

Traveling with kids raises the stakes and lowers the sleep. Pediatric urgent care, retail clinics, and telehealth pediatricians are widely available in US cities. TeleDirectMD sees adults 18 and older only. Two specific rules are worth memorizing:

  1. Any infant 3 months of age or younger with a rectal temperature of 100.4°F (38°C) or higher needs a call to a pediatrician immediately, per the American Academy of Pediatrics.[30] If you are traveling without a US pediatrician, seek in-person care promptly. The AAP clinical guideline for well-appearing febrile infants 8 to 60 days old covers evaluation with urine, blood culture, inflammatory marker, and cerebrospinal fluid testing.[31]
  2. Any child with signs of significant dehydration warrants same-day evaluation. Signs include dry mouth, absence of tears when crying, sunken eyes, minimal urine output (dry diapers for more than 6 to 8 hours in an infant), lethargy, or rapid breathing.

Common pediatric travel illnesses and where to go:

  • Fever without other symptoms in a child over 3 months, drinking fluids and behaving reasonably normally: telehealth or watchful waiting for 24 hours; recheck if fever persists more than 3 days or the child becomes lethargic.
  • Ear pain after a flight: often resolves in 24 to 48 hours with acetaminophen. Persistent ear pain with fever suggests otitis media; urgent care or telehealth for evaluation.
  • Vomiting or diarrhea: oral rehydration solution (Pedialyte or equivalent) in small frequent sips is the mainstay. Urgent care or telehealth if the child cannot keep fluids down, has bloody stools, or shows dehydration signs.
  • Rashes with fever: urgent evaluation; some infectious rashes require rapid diagnosis.
  • Head injury with any loss of consciousness, persistent vomiting, unusual sleepiness, or unequal pupils: ER, not urgent care.
  • Any respiratory distress (fast breathing at rest, retractions, blue lips): ER.

Pediatric telehealth is particularly well-suited to fever without red flags, mild URI, minor rashes, and medication questions. Most large pediatric telehealth services can prescribe common pediatric antibiotics, antipyretics, and short courses of asthma medications.

Red Flags: When to Skip Every Other Option and Go to the ER

Some symptoms are 911 or immediate-ER decisions no matter what your insurance situation is. If any of these are present, do not wait, do not try telehealth, and do not delay for logistical reasons:

  • Chest pain, especially with sweating, nausea, or radiation to the arm or jaw
  • Sudden severe difficulty breathing or blue lips
  • Stroke signs: face drooping, arm weakness, speech difficulty (the FAST mnemonic; Time to call 911)
  • Sudden severe headache, especially "the worst headache of my life"
  • Loss of consciousness, seizure, or new-onset confusion
  • Major trauma, uncontrolled bleeding, or suspected fractures with deformity
  • Signs of severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis): facial or tongue swelling, difficulty breathing, widespread rash, low blood pressure
  • Severe abdominal pain, especially with fever, vomiting, or inability to pass gas or stool
  • Signs of sepsis: high fever with confusion, very fast heart rate, low blood pressure, rapid breathing
  • In children: any lethargy that is out of character, any respiratory distress, any age under 3 months with fever, any signs of severe dehydration

The No Surprises Act protects insured US patients from balance billing for emergency care regardless of network status.[2] Medicare-participating hospitals that offer emergency services must screen and stabilize you regardless of ability to pay.[9] Neither of these fully solves the cost problem, but neither should keep you from calling 911 for a life-threatening symptom.
