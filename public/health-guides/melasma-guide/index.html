Melasma

A physician's guide to causes, triggers, sun protection, hydroquinone, triple combination cream, and what actually fades the patches.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: September 8, 2026
13 min read

What is the best treatment for melasma?

The foundation of melasma treatment is daily sun protection: dermatologists often recommend a broad-spectrum sunscreen of SPF 30 or higher containing zinc oxide, titanium dioxide, and iron oxide. On top of that, hydroquinone alone and triple combination cream (hydroquinone, tretinoin, and a corticosteroid) are the most effective, best-studied topicals. Over-the-counter hydroquinone products can no longer be legally sold in the U.S., so hydroquinone is used by prescription. Oral tranexamic acid is an off-label add-on for moderate to severe or recurrent melasma, with meta-analytic evidence of added benefit with triple cream, but its U.S. label rules it out for people with a history of blood clots or who use combined hormonal birth control. Melasma is chronic and tends to return with unprotected sun exposure, so sun protection is lifelong. Visible results usually take 3 to 12 months. A clinician can confirm the diagnosis and build a plan appropriate to your skin tone and triggers.

Key Takeaways

  • Melasma is a common, acquired darkening of facial skin that usually appears on both sides of the face, most often on the cheeks, forehead, chin, and above the upper lip.[2][7]
  • It is much more common in women than men, and risk is higher for women ages 20 to 40 and for people with medium to dark skin tones.[8][7]
  • Sunlight is a major trigger, and hormones from pregnancy and birth control pills, family history, thyroid problems, and some medicines also play a role.[8]

  • Oral tranexamic acid is an off-label add-on for moderate to severe or recurrent melasma, with meta-analytic support. Its U.S. label rules it out for people with a history of blood clots or who use combined hormonal birth control.[1][4][15][12]
  • Melasma is chronic and has no cure; results usually take 3 to 12 months, and it commonly returns with unprotected sun exposure.[3][9][7]

Melasma is a common pigment condition, most often seen in people with skin of colour.[2] It is not dirt, it is not a sign of poor hygiene, and it is not something a harsher cleanser will fix. It is a chronic pigment disorder linked to sunlight and hormones, and it is usually controlled, rather than cured, with a combination of sun protection and medication.[1][8]

This guide explains what melasma is, why it appears, the difference between melasma and other brown spots, and the evidence behind each treatment, from hydroquinone to tranexamic acid. Clinical claims are cited to the numbered sources listed at the end.

What Is Melasma?

Melasma is an acquired skin condition that produces flat, darker patches on sun-exposed skin, usually the face.[13] The classic locations are the cheeks, forehead, chin, and the area above the upper lip.[7] The patches are symmetric, meaning they typically appear on both sides of the face, and they range in color from tan or brown to grayish brown or bluish gray.[7]

The problem is overactive pigment-producing cells. In melasma, melanocytes, the cells that give skin its color, tend to be more active, although exactly why is not well understood.[8] Melasma is not a type of cancer and is not contagious, but its appearance can significantly affect quality of life and self-esteem.[7][1]

What Causes Melasma?

Researchers do not fully understand why the pigment cells become more active, but several triggers and risk factors are known:[8]

  • Ultraviolet light. Sunlight causes the skin to make more pigment, which can darken existing melasma and cause new patches.[9] Tanning beds and sunlamps tend to produce stronger ultraviolet rays than sunlight.[8]
  • Hormones. The rise in estrogen and progesterone during pregnancy is thought to trigger melasma, and birth control pills are another known trigger.[8]
  • Genetics. Having a blood relative with melasma raises your risk. In one global study of 324 people with melasma, 48 percent reported a family member with the condition.[8]
  • Other contributors. Thyroid problems, anti-seizure medicines, and medicines that make skin more sensitive to sunlight (including retinoids, some antibiotics, and some blood pressure medicines) can raise the risk or act as triggers. Some research suggests stress can trigger melasma, though this is controversial.[8]

Who Gets Melasma?

Melasma is much more common in women than men, and the risk is higher for women between ages 20 and 40.[8] Women with medium to dark skin tones are most likely to develop it, and women of Latin, Asian, Black, or Native American heritage are more likely to develop it.[7][8]

That pattern matters for treatment. Some melasma treatments can irritate darker skin, which can worsen melasma and make it darker, and it usually takes longer to see results from treatment in darker skin, so a gentler approach is often needed.[7]

Melasma During Pregnancy

Melasma is so common during pregnancy that it is sometimes called the mask of pregnancy.[7] The rise in estrogen and progesterone during pregnancy is thought to trigger it.[8] Many women first notice melasma during pregnancy, and birth control pills are another common trigger.[7][8]

Two practical points follow. First, melasma triggered by pregnancy or a medication can fade after delivery or after stopping the medication, though it can also last for years.[9][7] Second, the American Academy of Dermatology advises waiting until after the baby is born to treat melasma.[7] The U.S. tranexamic acid label states that it is not indicated for use in pregnant women, and the Tri-Luma label says that cream should be used in pregnancy only if the potential benefit justifies the potential risk to the fetus.[12][11] For a pregnant person, the usual approach is diligent sun protection and waiting. This guide's treatment discussion is for adults who are not pregnant, and pregnancy-related skin changes should be managed with your obstetric team.

Melasma vs Other Brown Spots

Melasma is one of several conditions that darken the skin, and the distinction changes treatment. The broad umbrella term is hyperpigmentation, meaning excess pigment. Melasma, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, sun spots, and freckles are all hyperpigmentation disorders, and melasma is a specific pattern among them.[13]

The practical difference is that melasma is a chronic, hormone-sensitive condition that flares with sun exposure and tends to recur, whereas a sun spot or a post-acne mark is usually a one-time pigment deposit. A dermatologist can usually tell them apart on examination, sometimes using a Wood's lamp or a dermatoscope for a close-up look.[9]

Sun Protection: The Foundation

Every melasma plan starts here. Sunlight causes the skin to make more pigment, which can darken existing melasma and cause new patches, and sun protection can help fade melasma and prevent it from returning.[9]

For melasma specifically, dermatologists often recommend a broad-spectrum sunscreen of SPF 30 or higher that contains zinc oxide, titanium dioxide, and iron oxide.[9] Iron oxide is a pigment that absorbs visible light. In a randomized trial of 68 people with melasma who all used 4 percent hydroquinone, those given a sunscreen with iron oxide improved more over 8 weeks than those given a UV-only sunscreen, which suggests visible light plays a role in melasma.[14] If a sunscreen leaves a white cast, a tinted sunscreen is an option. Add a wide-brimmed hat and shade when you are outdoors.[9] Sun protection is how you first fade melasma and then help keep it from coming back.

Hydroquinone: The Gold Standard Topical

Hydroquinone is a classic first-line medicine for melasma. It works by blocking tyrosinase, an enzyme involved in making melanin, which helps even out skin tone.[13][9]

A regulatory change is worth knowing. As a result of the CARES Act, effective September 23, 2020, the FDA treats over-the-counter skin lightening products containing hydroquinone as new drugs that need FDA approval, so they can no longer be legally sold without a prescription.[6] The FDA has received reports of serious side effects from hydroquinone, including rashes, facial swelling, and ochronosis, a permanent skin discoloration. Hydroquinone is still available by prescription from a health care provider.[10] Ochronosis is a gradual blue-black darkening of the skin, and it is linked to long-term, excessive use.[11][13]

Used under medical supervision and within recommended concentrations, hydroquinone is safe and effective, and side effects in melasma trials have mostly been mild, such as irritation, dryness, burning, and redness.[13][1] It is used for defined courses rather than indefinitely: the FDA-approved triple combination cream is labeled for short-term treatment, not maintenance, and is stopped once control is achieved.[11]

Triple Combination Cream and Other Topicals

Triple combination cream joins three medicines in one formulation: tretinoin (a retinoid), a corticosteroid to reduce inflammation, and hydroquinone to even out skin tone.[9] Along with hydroquinone alone, it is one of the most effective and best-studied treatments for melasma.[1] It has not clearly beaten hydroquinone alone: a 2025 network meta-analysis of recent randomized trials found the fluocinolone-based triple cream comparable to 4 percent hydroquinone alone.[3] In the U.S., Tri-Luma (fluocinolone acetonide 0.01 percent, hydroquinone 4 percent, tretinoin 0.05 percent) is the only FDA-approved drug containing hydroquinone, and it is approved for short-term treatment of moderate to severe melasma of the face.[6][11]

Other options include tretinoin used with a corticosteroid, and medicines that are gentler on the skin, such as azelaic acid, kojic acid, or vitamin C.[9]

Oral Tranexamic Acid

Oral tranexamic acid is FDA-approved for heavy menstrual bleeding, and dermatologists use it off-label for melasma.[15][12] In studies, tranexamic acid has decreased melasma patches when other treatments failed, and an evidence-based review called oral tranexamic acid a promising treatment for moderate and severe recurrent melasma.[9][1]

The evidence is supportive but still limited. A 2024 meta-analysis of four randomized trials with 490 patients found that adding oral tranexamic acid to triple combination cream lowered melasma severity scores more than the cream alone, and lowered recurrence.[4] On safety, the main concern has been blood clots, because tranexamic acid is an antifibrinolytic medicine.[15] A 2025 systematic review reported no thromboembolic events in randomized controlled trials of oral tranexamic acid.[5] Its long-term safety for melasma still needs more study.[1] The U.S. label lists active blood clots, a history of thrombosis or thromboembolism, an intrinsic risk of clotting, and use of combined hormonal contraception as contraindications, and states that it is not indicated in pregnancy.[12] If you have had a blood clot, tell your clinician.[9]

Procedures: Peels, Lasers, and Microneedling

When topicals are not enough, procedures can be added. Options include chemical peels, microneedling, and laser or light treatments, and a few studies have found that adding a laser or light treatment can improve results.[9]

The caution is that in trials, chemical peels and laser- and light-based treatments were equal or inferior to topical medicines while carrying a higher risk of side effects.[1] Some melasma treatments can also irritate darker skin, which can worsen melasma and make it darker.[7] For that reason procedures are usually an addition to, not a replacement for, daily sun protection and topical medication, and are best done by a clinician experienced with melasma.

Can Melasma Be Cured?

Honesty matters here. Melasma is a chronic condition, and there are currently no curative treatments.[1][3] It can fade with good care, but it commonly returns when you spend time outdoors without protecting your skin from the sun.[7] When a specific trigger is removable, like pregnancy or a medication, melasma can fade after delivery or after stopping the medication.[9]

Set expectations accordingly. Visible improvement usually takes 3 to 12 months of following the treatment plan, and it may take longer if you have had melasma for a long time.[9] Those who do best treat sun protection as a lifelong habit, not a short course.

When to See a Doctor

Melasma itself is not a type of cancer, but some brown spots are.[7] A spot that is asymmetric, has an irregular border or varying colors, or is changing in size, shape, or color can be a sign of melanoma, so the two should never be assumed to be the same thing.[16]

See a clinician promptly if

A spot is new, looks different from your other spots, is changing or growing, has a jagged border or more than one color, or itches, bleeds, or is painful.[16] Also tell your clinician if you have had a blood clot and are considering oral tranexamic acid.[9]
