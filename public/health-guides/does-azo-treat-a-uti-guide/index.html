Does AZO Treat a UTI? What Phenazopyridine Can and Cannot Do

AZO relieves urinary pain, but it is not an antibiotic. Here is what phenazopyridine actually does, how long to take it, and when symptoms need real treatment.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed August 30, 2026
9 min read

Does AZO treat or cure a UTI?

No. AZO (phenazopyridine) is a urinary tract analgesic, not an antibiotic. It relieves urinary pain, burning, urgency, and frequency, but it does not cure the infection. Antibiotics are what treat a UTI. Phenazopyridine turns urine reddish-orange and can interfere with laboratory tests, including urine tests, so tell a clinician you have taken it before testing. The over-the-counter label says not to use it for more than 2 days without consulting a doctor, and it is not a substitute for medical care.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated August 30, 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • AZO (phenazopyridine) is a urinary tract analgesic that relieves urinary pain, burning, urgency, and frequency. It is not an antibiotic and does not cure the infection.[1][2]
  • Antibiotics treat UTIs. Phenazopyridine is a comfort measure for the interval before the antibiotic controls the infection, not a treatment.[3][4]
  • Phenazopyridine turns urine reddish-orange. That is not harmful, but it can interfere with laboratory tests, including urine tests. Tell a clinician before testing.[1][2]
  • The recommended duration is short: the over-the-counter label says not to use it for more than 2 days without consulting a doctor. Follow the label and do not extend use on your own.[1][4]
  • Phenazopyridine is not for everyone. The prescription label lists kidney insufficiency and severe liver disease as contraindications, the over-the-counter label says not to use it with G6PD deficiency unless your physician approves, and both labels call for caution in pregnancy and breastfeeding.[4][1]
  • Fever, flank pain, vomiting, pregnancy, or symptoms that do not improve or that last more than 2 days mean you need medical care, not more AZO.[3][5][1]

What does AZO actually do for UTI symptoms?

AZO is a brand of phenazopyridine, a urinary tract analgesic.[1] The active ingredient is a dye.[6] According to the prescription label, it is excreted in the urine, where it has a topical analgesic effect on the lining of the urinary tract; its precise mechanism is unknown.[4] The result is temporary relief of the pain, burning, urgency, and frequency that come with a urinary tract infection.[1][2]

That relief is real, but it is only a mask. Phenazopyridine is not an antibiotic, and it does not cure infections.[2] The prescription label says it should be used for symptomatic relief of pain and not as a substitute for antimicrobial therapy, and that using it for symptom relief should not delay definitive diagnosis and treatment.[4] Phenazopyridine is also not an FDA-approved drug: both the over-the-counter AZO label and the prescription label state that it has not been found by FDA to be safe and effective and that the labeling has not been approved by FDA.[1][4]

For background on how UTIs are diagnosed and treated, see the urinary tract infection guide.

AZO vs antibiotics: what is the difference?

The difference is the difference between comfort and cure. Antibiotics treat UTIs,[3] and first-line choices for uncomplicated bladder infections include nitrofurantoin, fosfomycin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole.[7] Phenazopyridine does not treat the infection. It only eases the symptoms, and even the manufacturer says the only clinically proven cure for a UTI is a prescription antibiotic.[6]

Because phenazopyridine hides symptoms, it can delay the moment a person realizes the infection is not improving; the prescription label warns that it may mask pathological conditions.[4] Using it without an antibiotic does not treat the UTI, and a bladder infection that is not treated or does not go away can spread to one or both kidneys.[5]

How long can you take AZO (phenazopyridine) for urinary pain?

The recommended duration is short. The over-the-counter AZO label says not to use it for more than 2 days (12 tablets) without consulting a doctor,[1] and the prescription label says that when it is used with an antibiotic for a urinary tract infection, it should not exceed 2 days. That label adds that there is no evidence the combination provides greater benefit than the antibiotic alone after 2 days.[4]

AZO Urinary Pain Relief Maximum Strength contains 99.5 mg of phenazopyridine per tablet,[6] and its label directs adults to take 2 tablets 3 times daily with or after meals, as needed, for up to two days.[1] Prescription tablets come in 100 mg and 200 mg strengths.[4] Follow the label exactly, and do not extend the course on your own. If your symptoms last for more than 2 days, the label says to stop use and ask a doctor.[1]

Can you take AZO with antibiotics for a UTI?

Yes. Phenazopyridine is meant to be used alongside an antibiotic, not instead of one. The prescription label describes it as compatible with antimicrobial therapy and able to help relieve pain and discomfort during the interval before the antibiotic controls the infection.[4] People starting an antibiotic for uncomplicated cystitis begin to notice symptom relief within 36 hours on average.[8]

What you should not do is take AZO alone and assume the infection is being handled. The AZO package insert says you need to call your doctor to get diagnosed and obtain a prescription to treat the infection.[1] If a clinician has prescribed an antibiotic, take it exactly as directed and finish the course, even if you feel better.[9] If no antibiotic has been prescribed, AZO is only masking the problem.[4]

Does AZO affect a urine test or urine culture?

Phenazopyridine turns urine reddish-orange. That color change is not harmful, but it can stain clothing and contact lenses.[1][2] It can also interfere with laboratory tests, including urine tests,[1] and the prescription label says it may interfere with test values that use colorimetric, photometric, or fluorometric methods.[4] A review of urinalysis lists phenazopyridine as a cause of false-positive dipstick results for nitrite, bilirubin, protein, and urobilinogen.[10]

Tell the clinician and the laboratory staff that you have taken phenazopyridine before any urine test.[2] Follow the instructions you are given for when and how to collect the sample.

Can men take AZO for urinary symptoms?

Phenazopyridine can relieve urinary burning and urgency regardless of sex, but urinary symptoms in men deserve a different level of attention. Urinary tract infections in men are usually complicated,[8] and clinicians should consider urethritis and prostatitis in men with UTI symptoms. Men with lower UTI symptoms should receive antibiotics, with urine culture results guiding the choice.[7] A man with new urinary symptoms should be evaluated rather than managing the pain alone with AZO.

If a clinician has diagnosed a UTI in a man and prescribed an antibiotic, phenazopyridine may be used for comfort the same way it is in women. The key is that the underlying cause has been assessed first: the prescription label says symptom relief should not delay definitive diagnosis and treatment.[4]

Is AZO safe during pregnancy or breastfeeding?

The prescription label lists phenazopyridine under the older FDA pregnancy category B. Animal reproduction studies showed no evidence of harm to offspring, but one human study showed that the drug crosses the placenta, and there are no adequate, well-controlled studies in pregnant women. The label says it should be used in pregnancy only if the benefit clearly outweighs the risk, and it lists kidney infection in pregnancy as a contraindication.[4] The over-the-counter label says to ask a health professional before use if you are pregnant.[1] Pregnancy falls outside the uncomplicated cystitis covered by the IDSA treatment guidelines,[11] and these guides advise the emergency room for urinary symptoms in pregnancy.

It is not known whether phenazopyridine or its metabolites pass into human milk. The prescription label says a decision should be made either to stop nursing or to stop the drug, taking into account how important the drug is to the mother,[4] and the over-the-counter label says to ask a health professional before use while breastfeeding.[1] Discuss any use with a clinician or pharmacist first.

AZO side effects and urine discoloration

The most noticeable effect is reddish-orange urine, which is expected and not harmful, and which can stain clothing and contact lenses.[1][2] Taking phenazopyridine with or after food can reduce stomach upset. Other reported side effects include headache, rash, itching, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. A yellowish color of the skin or the whites of the eyes may mean the drug is building up because the kidneys are not clearing it, and the label says the drug must then be stopped.[4]

Serious effects can occur. They include methemoglobinemia, a blood condition that reduces oxygen delivery, and hemolytic anemia, the breakdown of red blood cells, and the label notes that kidney and liver toxicity are usually associated with overdose.[4] Published case reports link phenazopyridine to hemolytic anemia, methemoglobinemia, and acute kidney failure, especially in people with preexisting kidney disease.[12] People with G6PD deficiency are more susceptible to oxidative hemolysis,[4] and the over-the-counter label says not to use AZO with G6PD deficiency unless your physician approves.[1] These reactions appear to be rare: a 2023 case report noted that only a few such cases had been reported in 60 years,[13] and a large Israeli health-records study found that hundreds to thousands of G6PD-deficient patients had been prescribed phenazopyridine without major hemolysis.[14] The prescription label lists renal insufficiency, severe liver disease, severe hepatitis, and kidney infection in pregnancy as contraindications, and notes that kidney function commonly declines with age.[4]

Call a doctor right away for yellowing of the skin or eyes, blue to bluish-purple skin discoloration, shortness of breath, confusion, fever, or a sudden decrease in the amount of urine.[2]

When should you stop relying on AZO and get care?

AZO is a bridge, not a destination. The over-the-counter label says to stop use and ask a doctor if your symptoms last for more than 2 days.[1] Masking the pain does not stop the infection: a bladder infection that is not treated or does not go away can spread to one or both kidneys.[5]

Go to the emergency room for visible blood in the urine or any urinary symptoms during pregnancy (Dr. Bhavsar's cautious standard for these guides). Get same-day clinical advice or urgent care for fever, chills, flank or back pain, nausea or vomiting, immune suppression, or symptoms that are getting worse. These can signal a kidney infection or a more serious problem, and phenazopyridine will not help.[3][5][9][7]

Recurrent urinary symptoms also need a plan, not repeated rounds of pain medicine. Urine culture is necessary for people who have recurrent UTIs, treatment failures, or complicated UTIs,[15] and a clinician can identify the organism and discuss prevention options.[7] For more on prevention, see the D-mannose guide.

When Should a UTI Send You to the ER?

Go to the emergency room or call 911 for any of these:

  • Fever with shaking chills and pain in your back or side
  • Vomiting that keeps you from holding down fluids or medicine
  • Confusion, a racing heart, fast breathing, or feeling faint
  • Visible blood in your urine
  • Any urinary symptoms while you are pregnant

Get in-person care the same day for fever on its own, back or side pain on its own, urinary symptoms in a man, symptoms that are not improving 48 hours after starting an antibiotic, or symptoms that come back soon after treatment.

This red-flag list is the same on every UTI guide on this site. Sending visible blood in the urine and urinary symptoms in pregnancy to the emergency room is Dr. Bhavsar's deliberately cautious standard for these guides.
