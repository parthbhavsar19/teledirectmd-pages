How to Get Rid of BV

A physician's evidence-based guide to clearing bacterial vaginosis and, just as important, stopping it from coming back.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: September 8, 2026
14 min read

How do you get rid of bacterial vaginosis for good?

Getting rid of BV is a two-part problem: clearing the current episode and stopping it from coming back. For the first part, the CDC's recommended regimens are metronidazole 500 mg by mouth twice daily for 7 days, metronidazole 0.75% gel once daily for 5 days, or clindamycin 2% cream at bedtime for 7 days, with single-dose secnidazole, tinidazole and other clindamycin forms as alternatives. In one trial of 7-day oral treatment, cure was 76.8 percent at 14 days and 64.5 percent at 1 month. The second part is harder: in one cohort, 58 percent of women had BV again within 12 months of oral metronidazole. For multiple recurrences, twice-weekly metronidazole gel cut recurrence to 25.5 percent versus 59.1 percent on placebo during 16 weeks of use, though the benefit fades after it stops. Treating a male partner reduced recurrence from 63 to 35 percent at 12 weeks in a 2025 trial of monogamous couples, which is newer than CDC guidance that does not recommend routine partner treatment. Boric acid is part of a limited-data CDC option for multiple recurrences, and the Lactin-V probiotic is not yet FDA cleared. A clinician can confirm the diagnosis and build the plan that fits your recurrence pattern.

Key Takeaways

  • Getting rid of BV means solving two problems at once: clearing the current episode with a recommended antibiotic, and preventing a return, which happened in 58 percent of women within 12 months in one cohort.[1][2]
  • CDC's recommended regimens are metronidazole 500 mg by mouth twice daily for 7 days, metronidazole 0.75% gel once daily for 5 days, or clindamycin 2% cream at bedtime for 7 days. In one trial of 7-day oral treatment, cure was 76.8 percent at 14 days and 64.5 percent at 1 month.[1][12]
  • Take the full course as prescribed, and avoid sex or use condoms during treatment. In one trial, women who had sex during the study were more likely to have BV at follow-up.[1][12]
  • For multiple recurrences, twice-weekly metronidazole gel cut recurrence to 25.5 percent versus 59.1 percent on placebo during 16 weeks of use, though the benefit fades after it stops.[1][3]
  • Treating a male partner along with the woman reduced recurrence from 63 to 35 percent at 12 weeks in a 2025 trial of monogamous couples. CDC's 2021 guidance, written before that trial, does not recommend routine partner treatment.[1][6]
  • Boric acid is part of a limited-data CDC option for multiple recurrences, and the Lactin-V probiotic lowered recurrence in a trial but is not yet FDA cleared or commercially available.[1][4][5]
  • Douching is linked to BV and might raise the risk of relapse, and CDC finds no studies that support probiotic products as an add-on or replacement treatment.[1][11]

"How do I get rid of BV" is really two questions folded into one. The first is how to clear the episode you have now, and the answer is well established. The second is how to stop it from coming back, and that is where most of the frustration, and most of the recent evidence, lives. This guide walks through both in order.

Two Problems, One Goal

An antibiotic that clears BV does not necessarily end the story. BV is a shift in the vaginal bacteria in which the normal lactic-acid-producing species are replaced by high concentrations of anaerobic bacteria, and a notable feature is a polymicrobial biofilm on the cells lining the vagina.[1] In a small study of 18 women, a dense Gardnerella biofilm returned on the vaginal lining after a standard week of oral metronidazole, even though all of them recovered.[13] In one Australian cohort treated with oral metronidazole, 58 percent had BV again within 12 months.[2] So the realistic goal is a two-front campaign: treat the episode decisively, and if recurrence proves to be your pattern, layer on a prevention strategy.

The good news is that the two fronts have different levels of difficulty. Clearing a single episode is the more straightforward half. It is the prevention half that is hard, and even that has real evidence behind it now. Knowing which half applies to you, the first episode or the recurrence, is the first move.

Get the Diagnosis Right First

Before any treatment, make sure it is BV. Discharge and odor overlap with yeast and trichomoniasis, and CDC notes that a medical history alone is not enough for an accurate diagnosis and can lead to the wrong medication.[1] Many people with BV have no symptoms at all: in a national U.S. survey, 84 percent of women with BV did not report symptoms.[11] Testing settles it. In the office, vaginal pH, a KOH test and microscopy can often identify the cause, and BV lab tests (NAATs) can be run on clinician-collected or self-collected vaginal swabs from women with symptoms.[1]

The cost of skipping this step is usually measured in weeks. CDC warns that unnecessary or unapproved use of over-the-counter vaginal products is common and can delay treatment of the actual cause.[1] Confirm first, then treat, and the rest of the plan on this page works cleanly. For the full side-by-side, see the BV vs yeast guide.

Clearing a First Episode

For a first or occasional episode, CDC recommends one of three regimens: metronidazole 500 mg by mouth twice daily for 7 days, metronidazole 0.75% vaginal gel once daily for 5 days, or clindamycin 2% cream at bedtime for 7 days.[1] No studies directly compare oral and topical treatment, so the choice usually comes down to preference: pill versus gel versus cream, and whether the oil-based clindamycin cream is acceptable given it can weaken latex condoms and diaphragms for 5 days after use.[1] In a trial of 593 women given 7 days of oral metronidazole or tinidazole, overall cure was 76.8 percent at 14 days and 64.5 percent at 1 month, with no difference between the drugs.[12]

CDC's recommended and alternative regimens are all antibiotics, and CDC states that no studies support probiotic or Lactobacillus products as an add-on to, or replacement for, BV treatment.[1] So a confirmed diagnosis and a clinician's treatment plan are the entry point.

Finish the Course, Even When You Feel Better

The instructions most people skip are the most important. The recommended regimens are fixed courses of 5 or 7 days, so take every dose as prescribed even if symptoms settle sooner.[1] CDC advises avoiding sex or using condoms consistently and correctly during treatment, and notes that douching might increase the risk of relapse.[1] In a trial of 7-day oral treatment, women who had sex during the study were significantly more likely to have BV at follow-up.[12] Follow-up visits are not needed if symptoms resolve, but come back for evaluation if they return.[1]

The Single-Dose Option

If the barrier to a week of pills is adherence or simply the hassle, secnidazole 2 g is a CDC-listed alternative taken once as oral granules sprinkled onto unsweetened applesauce, yogurt, or pudding.[1] It was developed because recommended treatments require extended dosing and are associated with poor adherence, and an industry-authored integrated analysis of its two registration trials found it superior to placebo on all primary and secondary efficacy outcomes.[8] In a phase 3 trial, clinical cure at days 21 to 30 was 53 percent with secnidazole versus 19 percent with placebo. CDC lists it as an alternative rather than a recommended regimen because of its higher cost and lack of long-term outcomes.[1]

When BV Keeps Coming Back

If BV comes back after a first course, CDC says retreating with the same recommended regimen is acceptable, or a different recommended regimen can be considered.[1] Recurrence is tied to biology that a single course may not finish: BV behaves as a polymicrobial biofilm, with Gardnerella vaginalis thought to be the scaffold other species attach to,[9] and that biofilm has been seen returning on the vaginal lining after standard oral metronidazole.[13] For women with multiple recurrences, CDC describes suppression options rather than another single course.

Suppressive metronidazole gel has the strongest trial support. In a randomized trial, women with recurrent BV who responded to 10 days of metronidazole gel then used the gel twice weekly for 16 weeks; recurrence during that time was 25.5 percent versus 59.1 percent on placebo.[3] The gap narrowed after the gel stopped: across the full 28 weeks, recurrence was 51 percent versus 75 percent, and vaginal yeast infections were more common with the gel.[3] CDC likewise notes that the benefit of suppression does not persist once it is stopped.[1] For women who had failed all recommended regimens, one clinic series gave a 7-day oral nitroimidazole together with boric acid 600 mg daily for 30 days, then twice-weekly gel for 5 months. The first phase worked in 92 of 93 women, and 69.6 percent of those who kept using the gel stayed free of symptomatic BV at 6 months, but this was an uncontrolled retrospective review with frequent loss to follow-up.[7]

How the Recurrence Options Compare

Because the recurrence strategies report different follow-up windows, comparing them side by side requires reading the fine print, but the headline numbers are instructive.

Suppressive metronidazole gel reached 25.5 percent at 16 weeks, Lactin-V reached 30 percent at 12 weeks, partner treatment reached 35 percent at 12 weeks, and standard care, treating the woman alone, sat at 63 percent.[3][5][6] The takeaway is not that one strategy is best: each active strategy beat its own comparison group, but they were not compared with each other or tested together.

Treating the Partner

The newest and most discussed lever is partner treatment. In a 2025 New England Journal of Medicine trial of monogamous couples, either the woman alone was treated, or the male partner was also treated with oral metronidazole 400 mg and 2% clindamycin cream on the penile skin, both twice daily for 7 days. Recurrence within 12 weeks was 35 percent with partner treatment versus 63 percent without, and the data and safety monitoring board stopped the trial early because treating the woman alone was inferior.[6] Biofilm-associated Gardnerella has been found in both women with BV and their male partners.[9]

This is newer than the 2021 guidelines, which do not recommend routine treatment of sex partners because earlier trials, including one of 7-day oral metronidazole for male partners, did not reduce recurrence.[1] So it is a discussion with your clinician rather than a standing recommendation, and it applies to women with BV in a monogamous relationship with a male partner.

A few boundaries keep this guidance honest. The trial studied couples in a monogamous relationship, so it does not directly speak to casual or new partnerships, and in both groups the woman herself received first-line treatment. Treated men reported side effects including nausea, headache, and metallic taste.[6] It is also not a reason for a partner to take antibiotics without a clinician's involvement, since the partner regimen is a prescription decision too. For the couples it fits, it is worth discussing.

Support Steps That Help

Alongside medication, a few behavioral steps line up with the risk factors. Stop douching: in a national U.S. survey, BV was more common with more frequent douching, and CDC notes douching might increase the risk of relapse.[11][1] Condom use was associated with lower BV risk (relative risk 0.8) in a meta-analysis of observational studies, while new or multiple partners raised it.[10] Hormonal contraception does not increase BV risk and might protect against it, and oral contraceptive use was linked to less BV in the national survey.[1][11] None of these substitutes for treatment, but they remove conditions linked to BV, and they cost nothing.

Smoking was associated with BV in the same survey, though the link did not hold in its multivariate model, and stopping is reasonable advice that carries beyond BV anyway.[11] None of these lifestyle moves are a cure on their own, and someone who has done all of them and still recurs should not blame themselves.

If You Are Pregnant

BV in pregnancy deserves a separate line of the plan, because the stakes and the choices shift. CDC recommends treating all pregnant women who have symptoms, because symptomatic BV has been linked to premature rupture of membranes, preterm birth, intra-amniotic infection, and postpartum endometritis.[1] Metronidazole and clindamycin remain the drugs used: CDC concludes that metronidazole poses low risk in pregnancy and that vaginal clindamycin is safe, while tinidazole and secnidazole should be avoided.[1]

Two rules apply specifically in pregnancy. Boric acid is not a recurrence tool here, because current guidelines recommend avoiding it in pregnancy.[14] And because symptomatic BV in pregnancy should be treated, symptoms that could be BV are a reason to contact your obstetric clinician rather than wait. Routine BV screening of pregnant women without symptoms is not recommended for preventing preterm birth.[1] With those adjustments, the same diagnosis-first, treat-completely logic from above applies.

What Does Not Help

Be clear about what will not fix BV. CDC's recommended and alternative treatments are antibiotics; over-the-counter yeast products are not among them, and CDC warns that unapproved use of over-the-counter vaginal products is common and can delay treatment of the real cause.[1] There are no data supporting douching for treatment or symptom relief, and it might increase relapse.[1] CDC finds no studies supporting probiotic products as an add-on or replacement for BV treatment; the Lactobacillus crispatus product tested as Lactin-V lowered recurrence in a trial but is not FDA cleared or commercially available.[1][5] Home remedies have thin evidence. In one randomized trial included in a systematic review, a hydrogen peroxide douche was slightly but significantly less effective than a single oral dose of metronidazole, and the reviewers found insufficient evidence to advocate antiseptics for BV.[15] Oral garlic tablets looked similar to metronidazole in one trial of 120 women, but follow-up was only 7 to 10 days after starting treatment, which is not enough to replace an antibiotic.[16] Tea tree oil and yogurt inserts are not part of any CDC regimen.[1]

A Step-by-Step Plan

Putting it together, the plan most clinicians would recognize runs like this. Confirm the diagnosis with testing rather than guessing. Treat with a completed recommended course. If BV returns after that first course, retreating with the same or a different recommended regimen is acceptable. After multiple recurrences, CDC describes suppressive metronidazole gel twice weekly for more than 3 months, or, with limited data, a 7-day oral nitroimidazole followed by boric acid 600 mg daily for 21 days and then twice-weekly gel for 4 to 6 months.[1] Partner treatment in a monogamous relationship is a newer option to discuss.[6] If a plan fails, revisit the diagnosis, because trichomoniasis can also raise vaginal pH and cause similar discharge and odor.[1]

What to expect over the next several weeks helps set the right expectations. The course finishes within a week, follow-up is not needed if symptoms resolve, and you should come back if they return, because persistent or recurrent BV is common.[1] In one cohort, 58 percent of women had BV again within 12 months.[2] If it returns, that is the signal to discuss the next tier with a clinician rather than to re-run the same course indefinitely, and keeping a rough note of episode dates makes that conversation faster. None of this requires white-knuckling it alone; a clinician can map the plan to exactly where you are.
