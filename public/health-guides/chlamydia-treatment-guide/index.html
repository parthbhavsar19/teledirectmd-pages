Chlamydia: Symptoms, Testing & CDC-Recommended Treatment

CDC 2021 treatment guidelines, NAAT testing, doxycycline vs. azithromycin, expedited partner therapy, screening recommendations, and complication prevention, from Dr. Bhavsar.

Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: Mar 2026 · Updated: Jul 2026
15 min read

What is the CDC-recommended treatment for chlamydia in 2025?

The CDC 2021 STI Treatment Guidelines recommend doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for 7 days for adolescents and adults, with azithromycin 1 g as a single dose or levofloxacin 500 mg once daily for 7 days as alternatives. In a randomized trial in men who have sex with men, microbiologic cure of rectal chlamydia was 100% with doxycycline versus 74% with azithromycin. CDC notes that chlamydia was found at the anorectal site in 33% to 83% of women with urogenital infection, regardless of reported anal sex. In pregnancy, azithromycin 1 g as a single dose is recommended, and doxycycline is contraindicated in the second and third trimesters. The USPSTF recommends screening all sexually active women 24 and younger and older women at increased risk; CDC advises annual screening for sexually active women under 25. Abstain from sex for 7 days after single-dose therapy or until a 7-day course is finished, and until all partners have been treated. A licensed clinician, by telehealth or in person, can review symptoms and prescribe treatment.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated July 14, 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • The CDC 2021 STI Treatment Guidelines recommend doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days; azithromycin 1 g as a single dose is an alternative.[1]
  • NAAT is the recommended test. Its sensitivity is usually well above 90% and its specificity usually 99% or higher, and it can be used on urogenital, rectal, and oropharyngeal specimens.[2][7]
  • CDC advises annual screening for all sexually active women under 25; pregnant women need a test of cure about 4 weeks after treatment.[1][2]
  • Expedited partner therapy (EPT) is permissible in most states and the District of Columbia, according to CDC's legal-status map; always check your state's current law.[5]
  • Most infections found after treatment are reinfections from untreated or new partners, not treatment failure, which is why CDC advises retesting about 3 months after treatment.[2]
  • In a large cohort, women who tested positive for chlamydia had about 2.4 times the risk of pelvic inflammatory disease, and about 1.9 times the risk of ectopic pregnancy and of infertility, compared with women who tested negative.[8]

Chlamydial infection is the most frequently reported bacterial infectious disease in the United States.[1] Asymptomatic infection is common in both men and women, so many people who have it do not know.[2] What makes this infection clinically important is not how it presents, but what it can cause when it goes undetected. Among women, the most serious consequences include pelvic inflammatory disease, ectopic pregnancy, and infertility, and treating people with chlamydia prevents these reproductive complications and further sexual transmission.[2]

The CDC's 2021 STI Treatment Guidelines list doxycycline as the recommended regimen and azithromycin as an alternative.[1] This guide explains the evidence behind that recommendation, who it applies to, and what else matters in managing chlamydia, from initial testing to partner treatment.

Why the CDC Changed Its Chlamydia Treatment Recommendation

Azithromycin 1 g as a single oral dose is convenient and can be given as directly observed therapy in the clinic. In the 2021 guidelines, CDC lists it as an alternative regimen, with doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days as the recommended regimen.[1] CDC summarizes the evidence this way: a meta-analysis and a Cochrane review of randomized trials found that microbiologic treatment failure among men with urogenital chlamydia was higher with azithromycin than with doxycycline, and observational studies found doxycycline more efficacious for rectal infection in men and women.[2]

A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial published in Clinical Infectious Diseases compared the two drugs specifically for rectal C. trachomatis infection in men who have sex with men. Microbiologic cure was 100% with doxycycline versus 74% with azithromycin, an absolute difference of 26% (P < .001).[3]

The Rectal Chlamydia Problem in Women

What makes this particularly significant for women: a published review cited by CDC found C. trachomatis at the anorectal site in 33% to 83% of women who had urogenital infection, and its detection was not associated with reported receptive anal sex.[1] CDC notes that azithromycin keeps high efficacy for urogenital infection in women, but there is concern about how well it treats a concurrent rectal infection. Inadequately treated rectal infection can increase transmission and put women at risk of repeat urogenital infection through autoinoculation from the anorectal site.[2] Available evidence supports that doxycycline is efficacious for urogenital, rectal, and oropharyngeal infection.[2]

Current Treatment Regimens

Standard Regimens for Adolescents and Adults (Non-Pregnant)

Regimen Dose Classification Notes
Doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily × 7 days Recommended Efficacious for urogenital, rectal, and oropharyngeal infection. Contraindicated in the second and third trimesters of pregnancy.[1][2]
Azithromycin 1 g orally in a single dose Alternative An option when adherence to a 7-day course is a substantial concern. High efficacy for urogenital infection in women, but lower efficacy for rectal infection, so follow-up testing may be needed.[2]
Levofloxacin 500 mg orally once daily × 7 days Alternative An effective alternative, but more expensive.[2]

The doses in this table are the CDC 2021 regimens.[1] One practical note on doxycycline: a delayed-release 200 mg tablet taken once daily for 7 days is as effective as doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days for urogenital infection. It costs more but causes gastrointestinal side effects less often.[1][2]

Treatment During Pregnancy

Azithromycin 1 g as a single oral dose is the recommended treatment during pregnancy, and amoxicillin 500 mg orally three times daily for 7 days is the alternative.[1] Doxycycline is contraindicated during the second and third trimesters because of the risk of tooth discoloration. For levofloxacin, CDC notes that human data show a low risk to the fetus, but it has potential for toxicity during breastfeeding and animal data raise concern about cartilage damage in newborns.[2] Erythromycin is no longer recommended because its gastrointestinal side effects can lead to nonadherence, and systematic reviews have noted an association between macrolides, especially erythromycin, during pregnancy and adverse child outcomes.[2]

What I tell pregnant patients with chlamydia: treatment is not optional. CDC notes that severe problems can occur for mothers and newborns if the infection persists, and an infected mother can pass chlamydia to her baby during birth, where it most often shows up as conjunctivitis 5 to 12 days after birth and can also cause pneumonia at 1 to 3 months of age.[2]

Pharyngeal and Rectal Chlamydia

Routine oropharyngeal screening for chlamydia is not recommended, and the clinical significance of throat infection is unclear. Still, oropharyngeal chlamydia can be sexually transmitted to genital sites, so if it is found while testing for throat gonorrhea, it should be treated. Evidence is limited, but an observational study indicates doxycycline might be more efficacious than azithromycin for oropharyngeal chlamydia.[1][2]

CDC recommends annual screening for rectal chlamydia in men who report sexual activity at the rectal site. For women, rectal testing can be considered based on reported sexual behaviors and exposure, through shared decision-making between patient and provider. Self-collected rectal swabs are a reasonable alternative to clinician-collected swabs for NAAT screening.[2]

NAAT Testing: The Gold Standard for Diagnosis

Nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT) is the recommended test for chlamydia. CDC's laboratory guidance states that NAATs usually have sensitivity well above 90% while keeping specificity usually at 99% or higher, and that they typically detect 20% to 50% more chlamydial infections than culture or earlier non-culture tests.[7] Older antigen tests (enzyme immunoassays) are not as sensitive or specific as NAATs, and their use is discouraged.[7]

Specimen Collection

  • Women: Vaginal swabs are the preferred specimen type. They are as sensitive as cervical swabs, and first-catch urine may detect up to 10% fewer infections. Self-collected vaginal swabs are equivalent in sensitivity and specificity to clinician-collected swabs.[7]
  • Men: First-catch urine is an optimal specimen for screening men.[2]
  • Extragenital sites: Rectal and oropharyngeal infection can be diagnosed by NAAT at the exposed site, and certain NAAT platforms have been cleared by the FDA for these sites. Certain NAATs detect both C. trachomatis and N. gonorrhoeae from a single specimen.[2]
  • At-home testing: On March 28, 2025, the FDA granted marketing authorization to the Visby Medical Women's Sexual Health Test, the first test for chlamydia, gonorrhea, and trichomoniasis that can be bought without a prescription and performed entirely at home.[14] See our review of the Visby Women's Sexual Health Test for clinical performance data and how it compares to mail-in alternatives.
Important: Do Not NAAT-Test Within 4 Weeks of Treatment

Nonviable organisms can remain after successful treatment, so using a chlamydia NAAT less than 4 weeks after finishing therapy is not recommended because it can give a false-positive result. A test of cure is not advised for nonpregnant people treated with a recommended or alternative regimen, unless adherence is in question, symptoms persist, or reinfection is suspected.[1]

Screening Recommendations

The USPSTF gives a Grade B recommendation for chlamydia screening in all sexually active women 24 years and younger, including pregnant persons, and in women 25 and older who are at increased risk. Risk factors it lists include a new sex partner or more than one sex partner, a sex partner who has other partners at the same time or has an STI, inconsistent condom use outside a mutually monogamous relationship, and a previous or coexisting STI. The USPSTF does not set a screening interval; it suggests rescreening when new or persistent risk factors appear since the last negative test.[6] CDC recommends annual screening of all sexually active women under 25.[2]

Population Recommended Screening
Sexually active women under 25 Annually (CDC); USPSTF Grade B for screening[2][6]
Women 25+ at increased risk Screen (CDC; USPSTF Grade B)[6][10]
All pregnant women under 25 At the first prenatal visit; rescreen in the third trimester[2][10]
Pregnant women 25+ at increased risk At the first prenatal visit; rescreen in the third trimester[2][10]
Men who have sex with men (MSM) At least annually at sites of contact (urethra, rectum); every 3 to 6 months if at increased risk (for example, on PrEP or living with HIV)[10]
All patients diagnosed with chlamydia Retest approximately 3 months after treatment to detect reinfection[1][10]

CDC also states that people diagnosed with chlamydia should be tested for HIV, gonorrhea, and syphilis.[1] MSM who are HIV negative with a rectal chlamydia diagnosis should be offered HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP).[1]

Follow-Up and Special Considerations in Pregnancy

For non-pregnant adults treated with a recommended or alternative regimen, routine test of cure is not advised. The main follow-up action is retesting approximately 3 months after treatment, regardless of whether patients believe their partners were treated, because most infections found after treatment are reinfections rather than treatment failures.[1][2] If retesting at 3 months is not possible, CDC advises retesting whenever the person next seeks care within 12 months of treatment.[2]

Pregnant patients require a different approach. CDC recommends a test of cure, preferably by NAAT, about 4 weeks after finishing therapy, because severe problems can occur for mothers and newborns if the infection persists. All pregnant women diagnosed with chlamydia should also be retested 3 months after treatment, and women under 25 or at increased risk should be rescreened in the third trimester.[2]

Patients should abstain from sexual intercourse for 7 days after single-dose therapy, or until a 7-day regimen is finished and any symptoms have resolved, and until all of their sex partners have been treated.[1][2] The majority of infections found after treatment come from partners who were not treated or from a new infected partner, not from treatment failure.[2]

Expedited Partner Therapy: What It Is and Who Can Receive It

Expedited partner therapy (EPT) is the clinical practice of treating the sex partners of patients diagnosed with chlamydia or gonorrhea by giving the patient prescriptions or medications to take to their partners, without the health care provider first examining the partner.[4] The rationale is simple: treating sex partners can prevent reinfection and infection of other partners.[2]

CDC's 2021 guidelines say that, unless prohibited by law or other regulations, providers should routinely offer EPT to patients with chlamydia when they cannot ensure that all of the patient's sex partners from the previous 60 days will seek timely treatment. For men who have sex with men, data are more limited, so shared clinical decision-making about EPT is recommended.[2] CDC describes EPT as a useful option, particularly for treating male partners of women with chlamydia or gonorrhea, and as an added strategy that does not replace others such as provider-assisted referral when available.[4]

EPT Legal Status by State

CDC's state-by-state legal-status map, last updated July 1, 2026, lists EPT as permissible in most states and in the District of Columbia, and lists a few states, such as South Dakota, as only potentially allowable.[5] CDC notes that the map is not legal advice, and its guidelines state that EPT legality varies by state and by infection.[2][5] Always check your state's current law before using EPT.

EPT Medication for Chlamydia

CDC prefers giving the patient packaged oral medication for the partner rather than a prescription, because the efficacy of EPT by prescription has not been evaluated and many people do not fill prescriptions given to them by a partner. The medication or prescription should come with written materials for the partner, including treatment instructions, warnings about taking the medication (for example, if the partner is pregnant or has an allergy to it), and advice to seek medical evaluation as soon as possible for HIV and any STI symptoms, particularly symptoms of PID.[2]

Partners should abstain from condomless sex until they and their own sex partners have been treated (that is, after finishing a 7-day regimen) and any symptoms have resolved.[2] EPT covers the immediate treatment need, but it does not replace a full clinical evaluation.

Reinfection: A Persistent Clinical Problem

Repeat chlamydial infections are common. In Louisiana surveillance data from 2000 to 2015, 1 in 4 women diagnosed with chlamydia at ages 15 to 34 had a reported repeat infection within 3 years, and risk was higher for younger women. The authors note the true number is likely higher, because surveillance counts only infections that were detected and reported.[9]

The main driver is not treatment failure. CDC states that the majority of infections after treatment come from reinfection, either because sex partners did not receive treatment or because sexual activity began with a new infected partner. This is why partner notification and EPT are central to chlamydia management.[2]

Repeat infections are not merely inconvenient. CDC notes that repeat infections raise the risk of PID and other complications in women.[2] In a large cohort study, PID risk was higher for women with 2 or more positive chlamydia tests than for those with 1 positive test.[8] Retesting about 3 months after treatment is meant to find these new infections early.[1]

What I Tell Patients About Retesting

The recommendation for patients: the 3-month retest is not checking whether the antibiotics worked. It's checking whether you got reinfected. If you took the antibiotics as prescribed, they most likely worked. The retest is about your partners and your exposures after treatment, not the drug. Setting that expectation upfront changes how patients interpret the recommendation.

Complications of Untreated Chlamydia

Most of the harm from chlamydia comes not from the acute infection but from complications when infection goes undetected or untreated. CDC notes that some women diagnosed with uncomplicated cervical infection already have subclinical upper genital tract infection, and that treatment delays have been linked to complications such as PID in a limited proportion of women.[2] Understanding these consequences helps patients grasp why screening and treatment matter even when they feel fine.

Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID)

PID is infection of the upper reproductive tract (the uterus, fallopian tubes, and ovaries). CDC notes that sexually transmitted organisms, especially N. gonorrhoeae and C. trachomatis, are often implicated.[13] In a retrospective cohort of 857,324 primary care patients aged 12 to 25, women who who tested positive for chlamydia had a 2.36-times higher adjusted risk of PID than women who tested negative.[8] Women with PID often have subtle or nonspecific symptoms or none at all, and even mild or asymptomatic PID might carry a risk of infertility.[13]

Ectopic Pregnancy

In the same cohort, women who tested positive for chlamydia had about 87% higher adjusted risk of ectopic pregnancy than women who tested negative.[8] Ectopic pregnancy is implantation of a pregnancy outside the uterus, most commonly in a fallopian tube.

Tubal Factor Infertility

Salpingitis, or inflammation of the fallopian tubes, caused by ascending infection can scar and block the tubes. Women who tested positive for chlamydia had about 85% higher adjusted risk of infertility than women who tested negative.[8] Chlamydia screening programs have been shown to reduce PID rates among women.[2]

Epididymitis in Men

In men, chlamydia can cause epididymitis, inflammation of the coiled tube behind the testicle. Men with acute epididymitis typically have pain and tenderness in one testicle.[11] For acute epididymitis most likely caused by chlamydia or gonorrhea, the CDC recommended regimen is ceftriaxone 500 mg IM in a single dose plus doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for 10 days (ceftriaxone 1 g for people weighing 150 kg or more).[11] CDC's laboratory guidance notes that complications such as epididymitis affect a minority of infected men and rarely result in reproductive health problems.[7]

Lymphogranuloma Venereum (LGV)

LGV is caused by C. trachomatis serovars L1, L2, or L3. Rectal exposure among MSM or women can result in proctocolitis, which is the most common presentation of LGV. The recommended treatment is longer than for standard chlamydia: doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for 21 days.[12]

Telehealth and Chlamydia Management

Chlamydia is among the STIs most amenable to telehealth care. The usual diagnostic and treatment steps (history, test ordering, antibiotic prescribing, EPT where legal, partner notification counseling, and retest scheduling) can often be completed without an in-person visit.

Unlike gonorrhea, whose current treatment involves an injection, the CDC chlamydia regimens are all oral.[1][2] A physician can review your sexual history via a secure video visit, order a NAAT at a local laboratory or through a home collection kit, and send the prescription electronically to your pharmacy. EPT prescriptions for partners can also be issued during the same visit in states where EPT is permissible.

The main limitation of telehealth for STI care is that it cannot replace in-person examination when a clinical finding needs direct assessment, such as suspected PID with abdominal tenderness, epididymitis, or LGV proctitis. For those presentations, an in-person visit or emergency evaluation is appropriate.

Telehealth can also lower practical barriers to STI care, such as distance from a clinic, stigma, time constraints, and privacy concerns, for people who might otherwise delay testing and treatment.

When to Seek Prompt or Emergency Care

Seek Same-Day or Emergency Evaluation For:
  • Acute pelvic pain with fever: possible PID. CDC lists tubo-ovarian abscess, pregnancy, severe illness, and fever above 38.5°C (101°F) among reasons to consider hospital care.[13]
  • Pain and swelling in one testicle: evaluate for epididymitis, and rule out testicular torsion, which is a surgical emergency.[11]
  • Severe rectal pain, bleeding, or discharge with fever: possible LGV proctocolitis or rectal gonorrhea needing further workup.[12]
  • Symptoms during pregnancy: pregnant women suspected of having PID are at high risk for maternal illness and preterm delivery, so any suspected STI in pregnancy needs prompt evaluation.[13]
  • Persistent symptoms after completing treatment: could indicate treatment failure, reinfection, or another infection that was not tested for; CDC's advice against a routine test of cure does not apply when symptoms persist.[1]
  • Eye redness with discharge in a newborn: possible chlamydial conjunctivitis, which most often appears 5 to 12 days after birth; needs prompt evaluation.[2]

Most uncomplicated chlamydial infections do not produce urgent symptoms, and that is precisely what makes them dangerous. The dangerous presentations above indicate that the infection has already progressed beyond the lower genital or urinary tract. Do not wait to seek care for these findings.
