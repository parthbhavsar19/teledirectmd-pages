Sick While Visiting New York City? How Visitors Get Medical Care

For tourists, business travelers and short-program students: when to call 911, how the city's care options compare, and how prescriptions work for someone who does not live here.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MDLast reviewed October 26, 202613 min read

How can a visitor get medical care while sick in New York City?

New York City drew 65 million visitors in 2025, including 12.5 million from outside the US (NYC Tourism + Conventions, March 2026). A visitor who gets sick has the same core options as a resident. For a possible emergency, call 911: under the federal EMTALA law, Medicare-participating hospitals that offer emergency services must provide a medical screening exam and stabilizing treatment regardless of ability to pay, and the city's 11 public hospitals are run by NYC Health + Hospitals, which provides care regardless of ability to pay (NYC311). For non-emergencies, the options are urgent care centers, NYC Health + Hospitals ExpressCare (video and phone visits around the clock, plus walk-in care at its locations), pharmacies, hotel-arranged house calls and telehealth. A prescription written by your doctor at home is not one a New York pharmacy can fill: New York pharmacies dispense prescription drugs only on a prescription from a person legally authorized to issue it (NY Education Law §6810), and New York prescriptions are generally sent electronically (NY Public Health Law §281). A US-licensed clinician who evaluates you while you are physically in New York can decide whether a new prescription is appropriate and send it to a New York pharmacy you choose.

If this might be an emergency, call 911 now. Chest pain or pressure, trouble breathing, sudden weakness on one side, facial droop, slurred speech, sudden confusion or a sudden severe headache need emergency care, not a video visit or an urgent care queue. If you are in a hotel, call the front desk after 911 so staff can meet the responders.[7][8]

Key Takeaways

  • Emergencies come first. Hospitals covered by EMTALA must screen and stabilize anyone with an emergency condition regardless of ability to pay. The city's 11 public hospitals, including Bellevue in Manhattan, are run by NYC Health + Hospitals.[2][3]
  • For non-emergencies, the realistic choices are urgent care, NYC Health + Hospitals ExpressCare, a pharmacy, a hotel or house-call doctor, or a telehealth video visit. They differ in what they can physically do, not just in convenience.[3][4]
  • A prescription from your home doctor cannot simply be filled in New York. A US-licensed clinician has to issue a new one, and New York requires most prescriptions to be sent electronically.[10][11][12]
  • For a telehealth visit, what matters is where you are physically located during the visit, not where you live. A visitor who is in New York at the time of the visit can be seen by a physician licensed in New York.
  • Keep every receipt. Travel insurers that reimburse care generally ask for copies of bills and invoices after the fact.[18]

Who this guide is for

This guide is for adults who are in New York City temporarily and get sick: tourists, people in town for a conference or client meetings, students on a short summer or exchange program, and anyone visiting family. New York City recorded 65 million visitors in 2025. Most, 52.4 million, came from elsewhere in the US; 12.5 million came from other countries.[1]

Those two groups face slightly different problems. A visitor from another US state may have health insurance, but it may treat New York providers as out of network. A visitor from abroad usually has no US coverage at all, may have travel insurance that reimburses after the fact, and carries prescriptions that no New York pharmacy can fill. Both need to know which door to walk through.

If you are visiting from India or Mexico, the country-specific guides cover medication brand names and import rules in more detail: Medical Care in the US for Visitors From India and Medical Care in the US for Visitors From Mexico. The broader international visitors page explains how TeleDirectMD works for people who live outside the US.

This guide is for adults 18 and older. If a child traveling with you is sick, use an in-person option that sees children, or 911 for an emergency.

Emergency first: when to call 911

Start with severity, not cost or convenience. In New York City, 911 is the number for fire, police and medical emergencies.[17] You can dial it from a mobile phone, including a foreign phone roaming in the US.

Call 911 for these

  • Possible heart attack: chest pain or discomfort; pain or discomfort in the jaw, neck, back, or one or both arms or shoulders; shortness of breath; or feeling weak, light-headed or faint.[7]
  • Possible stroke: sudden numbness or weakness in the face, arm or leg, especially on one side; sudden confusion or trouble speaking; sudden trouble seeing; sudden trouble walking or loss of balance; or a sudden severe headache with no known cause. The CDC's B.E. F.A.S.T. check covers balance, eyes, face, arms and speech, and the T stands for time: call 911 right away.[8]
  • Breathing trouble that is severe or getting worse, or swelling of the lips, tongue or throat after a food, medicine or sting.
  • Serious injury: a fall or collision with a head injury, confusion, a possible broken bone, or bleeding that does not stop with pressure.
  • High fever with confusion, a stiff neck or a new rash that does not fade when pressed, or someone who is hard to wake.
  • Thoughts of harming yourself. This is a medical emergency too.

What the hospital must do, and what it does not mean

The federal Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act (EMTALA) applies to Medicare-participating hospitals that offer emergency services. When someone comes to the emergency department and asks for care, the hospital must provide a medical screening exam, regardless of the person's ability to pay, and must stabilize an emergency medical condition or arrange an appropriate transfer.[2]

That protects your access to emergency care. It does not make the care free. You will be billed afterward, so keep your passport details, travel insurance information and a working email address handy for the registration desk.

New York City also has a public hospital system. NYC Health + Hospitals runs the city's 11 public hospitals: Bellevue, Elmhurst, Harlem, Jacobi, Kings County, Lincoln, Metropolitan, North Central Bronx, Queens, South Brooklyn Health and Woodhull. The city's 311 service states that patients can receive care there regardless of ability to pay, and that information, including immigration status, is kept confidential.[3][5] In an emergency, do not delay a 911 call to pick a hospital; the ambulance crew will take you to an appropriate one.

How to read the table

The rows are not ranked. Each one answers a different need. An emergency department is the only right answer for a red-flag symptom. Urgent care centers and ExpressCare walk-in sites can examine you in person, and urgent care centers can often test on site. ExpressCare and telehealth both work by video, which suits problems a clinician can assess by talking with you and looking at what the camera can show.

If you have US insurance from another state, call the number on your card before choosing; your plan's network rules, not this guide, decide what it covers. If you have travel insurance, check whether it requires you to call an assistance line before non-emergency care, because some policies do.

How a video visit works when you are visiting New York

A video visit is an ordinary medical visit held over a secure video connection. For a visitor, four details matter more than they do for a resident.

You must be physically in New York during the visit

For telehealth, the physician must be licensed where the patient is physically located during the visit. TeleDirectMD follows that rule: if you are in New York State when the visit starts, a physician licensed in New York can see you, whether you live in Manhattan, Ohio or Madrid. Your home address does not decide it. Your location at the time of the visit does.

This trips people up near the edges of the city. Newark Liberty International Airport is in New Jersey, not New York. If you plan to do the visit from an airport gate or a day trip, check the states TeleDirectMD serves for where you will actually be. TeleDirectMD sees adults in 40+ states; the New York page covers what is available here.

Book, pay and verify who you are

You choose an open time on the booking page and pay the flat $79 by card at booking. Have a photo ID ready; for most international visitors, that is a passport. In New York, visits are self-pay. If you have a US flexible spending or health savings account, the visit is FSA/HSA eligible.

Choose a New York pharmacy before the visit

Pick a pharmacy close to where you are staying and note its name and street address. If the physician decides a prescription is appropriate, it is sent electronically to that pharmacy. New York law requires prescriptions issued in the state to be made electronically, with narrow exceptions, and the state's mandate covers both controlled and non-controlled medicines.[10][11] You will not receive a paper prescription to carry around. Late at night, confirm the pharmacy is open before you choose it.

Have your information in front of you

  • A list of every medicine you take, with the generic name, strength and how often you take it. Brand names differ between countries, and the FDA advises travelers to know both the brand and generic name, the dosage form and the strength.[13]
  • The original containers or blister packs, if you have them with you, so the physician can see exactly what you take.
  • A letter from your home doctor, if you have one. The CDC recommends a letter on office letterhead listing conditions, medicines with generic names and doses, and allergies.[15]
  • Your allergies, especially to antibiotics, and whether you could be pregnant.
  • Good light and a quiet room. A hotel bathroom mirror light is often the best light for showing a rash, a red eye or the back of the throat.

The physician decides what is appropriate after hearing the full story. Sometimes that is a prescription. Often it is advice on over-the-counter care, a clear list of warning signs, or a recommendation to be seen in person because the problem needs an exam or a test that video cannot provide.

Prescriptions: why your home prescription will not work here

Many visitors do not expect this: a valid prescription from home, even for a common medicine, cannot be handed to a New York pharmacist. The reason is legal, not bureaucratic.

New York's Education Law says a prescription drug may be dispensed only on a prescription written by a person legally authorized to issue it.[12] A physician licensed only in another country is not authorized to prescribe in New York. Separately, New York's Public Health Law requires prescriptions issued in the state to be made electronically, with limited exceptions.[10] A paper prescription from abroad meets neither requirement.

What you can do:

  • Bring enough of your regular medicines. The FDA allows foreign nationals to bring or ship a 90-day supply of drug products for personal use.[14] Keep them in your carry-on, in their original containers where possible.[13][15]
  • If you run out or lose them, a US-licensed clinician can review what you take and decide whether a short bridge prescription is appropriate for a non-controlled medicine. The generic names on your list make that review faster and safer. The guide to running out of medication in the US covers this in detail.
  • Expect the medicine itself to cost extra. The pharmacy charges for the medication separately from any visit fee. Ask the pharmacist for the cash price before it is filled.

Controlled medicines are a different category. TeleDirectMD does not prescribe controlled substances. If you take one, bring your supply and plan for in-person care if you need more.

Common visitor problems that can fit a video visit

These are conditions listed on TeleDirectMD's conditions page that commonly come up during a trip. A video visit is a reasonable first step for each when you have no warning signs. The physician may still direct you to in-person care after hearing the details.

  • Sore throat. Common after flights and crowded venues. A video visit cannot collect a throat swab, so if strep testing is needed, the physician will tell you where to get it. The adult sore throat guide explains when testing makes sense.
  • Common cold and sinus infection. Most colds are viral and need symptom care rather than antibiotics; the physician decides whether a sinus infection needs more.
  • Flu and COVID-19. Some treatments are time-sensitive, so it is better to be assessed early than to wait until you are home.
  • Urinary tract infection, for women only, when it is uncomplicated and the patient is not pregnant. Fever, back or flank pain, or vomiting needs in-person care.
  • Pink eye. Eye pain, light sensitivity, a change in vision, or contact lens wear with a painful red eye needs an in-person eye exam.
  • Traveler's diarrhea, for visitors who arrive unwell after travel. Blood in the stool, high fever or signs of dehydration need in-person care.
  • Stomach bug (viral gastroenteritis), when you can keep fluids down.
  • Motion sickness, for example before a ferry, a long car trip or the flight home.
  • Skin rash from contact with something new (contact dermatitis), such as a different soap, detergent or sunscreen.
  • Cold sores, which can flare with travel stress, wind and sun.
  • Seasonal allergies, which can catch visitors off guard when New York's pollen season differs from home.
  • Ear pain, including pressure that lingers after a flight.

The full list, with what each visit covers, is on the what we treat page.

What does not fit a video visit

Video is a good tool for a narrow set of problems. It is the wrong tool for these:

  • Emergencies. Any symptom in the 911 list above. A video visit adds delay.
  • Anything that needs hands, equipment or a lab. Cuts that may need stitches, possible fractures or sprains that need an X-ray, a throat swab, a pregnancy-related concern, or symptoms that need blood tests today. Urgent care, an ExpressCare walk-in site or an emergency department fits better.
  • Controlled substances. TeleDirectMD does not prescribe them, including sleep, anxiety, ADHD and opioid pain medicines.
  • Children. TeleDirectMD sees adults 18 and older only.
  • Urinary symptoms in men, or a UTI with fever, back pain or pregnancy. These need in-person evaluation.
  • Symptoms that are getting worse after a visit. A plan made by video depends on the illness following the expected course. If it does not, be seen in person.

Practical New York logistics

Pharmacies, including late at night

Pharmacies are spread across all five boroughs, and some are open 24 hours.[16] Hours change, and a store that is open around the clock may close its pharmacy counter overnight. Before you send a prescription late in the evening, check the pharmacy counter's hours in the chain's app or by phone. When you pick up, bring photo ID in case the pharmacy asks for it, and expect to pay at the counter.

311 for city information

NYC311 is the city's line for non-emergency information and services, and it answers 24 hours a day, every day. From outside New York City, or from a phone that cannot dial 311, call 212-NEW-YORK (212-639-9675). The call center can help in over 175 languages when you ask for an interpreter. For a medical emergency, call 911, not 311.[17] The 311 site also explains how to reach NYC Health + Hospitals for non-emergency appointments.[3]

Tell the hotel front desk

  • If you call 911 from your room, call the front desk right after. Staff can meet the responders, hold an elevator and bring them to the right floor.
  • If you are sick but stable, tell the desk anyway. They can let housekeeping know, and many can tell you the nearest pharmacy and its hours.
  • If the hotel offers a doctor who comes to the room, ask who provides the service and what the total fee will be before you agree.

Receipts and travel insurance

CDC guidance for travelers advises being ready to pay at the time of care, then sending the insurer copies of bills and invoices, and keeping all records, charges and receipts.[18] For a TeleDirectMD visit, you can request an itemized receipt by emailing contact@teledirectmd.com. Whether you are reimbursed depends entirely on your policy, so read its terms or call the assistance line before you assume it pays.

Questions about a visit, a receipt or whether your situation fits can go to contact@teledirectmd.com.
