Sick While Visiting New York City? How Visitors Get Medical Care

For tourists, business travelers, students and exchange visitors: when to call 911, what an ambulance and other care cost, where to go for dental and sexual health needs, how New York pharmacies and prescriptions work, and the seasonal risks a visitor should know.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MDLast reviewed November 28, 202627 min read

How can a visitor get medical care while sick in New York City?

New York City drew 65 million visitors in 2025, including 12.5 million from outside the US (NYC Tourism + Conventions, March 2026). For a possible emergency, call 911: under the federal EMTALA law, Medicare-participating hospitals with emergency services must provide a medical screening exam and stabilizing treatment regardless of ability to pay, and the city's 11 public hospitals are run by NYC Health + Hospitals, which provides care regardless of ability to pay (NYC311). Care is still billed afterward; an FDNY basic life support ambulance trip is $1,793 plus $20 per mile at rates effective July 1, 2026 (FDNY). For non-emergencies, visitors can use urgent care centers, NYC Health + Hospitals ExpressCare, the city's Sexual Health Clinics, a dental school urgent care clinic, pharmacies or telehealth. A prescription written at home cannot be filled in New York: pharmacies dispense only on a prescription from a person legally authorized to issue it (NY Education Law §6810), and New York prescriptions are sent electronically (NY Public Health Law §281). Medicine names also differ: paracetamol is sold as acetaminophen in the US (FDA).

If this might be an emergency, call 911 now. Chest pain or pressure, trouble breathing, sudden weakness on one side, facial droop, slurred speech, sudden confusion or a sudden severe headache need emergency care, not a video visit or an urgent care queue. If you are in a hotel, call the front desk after 911 so staff can meet the responders.[7][8]

Key Takeaways

  • Emergencies come first. Hospitals covered by EMTALA must screen and stabilize anyone with an emergency condition regardless of ability to pay. The city's 11 public hospitals, including Bellevue in Manhattan, are run by NYC Health + Hospitals.[2][3]
  • Emergency care is not free. FDNY bills ambulance service afterward, starting at $1,793 for basic life support plus mileage, and runs a Charitable Care Policy for people who qualify by income.[22]
  • For non-emergencies, a visitor can choose urgent care, NYC Health + Hospitals ExpressCare, a city Sexual Health Clinic, NYU Dentistry's walk-in urgent care, a pharmacy or a telehealth video visit. They differ in what they can physically do.[3][30][31]
  • A prescription from your home doctor cannot simply be filled in New York. A US-licensed clinician has to issue a new one, and New York requires most prescriptions to be sent electronically.[10][11][12]
  • Pharmacy names and rules differ from home. Paracetamol is acetaminophen here, pseudoephedrine is sold behind the counter with photo ID, and emergency contraception is sold without a prescription.[26][27][29]
  • Summer heat is the city's deadliest weather. About 500 New Yorkers die from heat-related causes each year, and most of those deaths happen on days that are hot but not extreme.[33]
  • Keep every receipt. Travel insurers that reimburse care generally ask for copies of bills and invoices after the fact.[18]

Who this guide is for

This guide is for adults who are in New York City temporarily and get sick: tourists, people in town for a conference or client meetings, students on a summer or exchange program, and anyone visiting family. New York City recorded 65 million visitors in 2025. Most, 52.4 million, came from elsewhere in the US; 12.5 million came from other countries.[1]

Those two groups face slightly different problems. A visitor from another US state may have health insurance, but it may treat New York providers as out of network. A visitor from abroad usually has no US coverage at all, may have travel insurance that reimburses after the fact, carries prescriptions that no New York pharmacy can fill, and may not recognize the names on pharmacy shelves. Both need to know which door to walk through, and what it will cost.

If you are visiting from India or Mexico, the country-specific guides cover medication brand names and import rules in more detail: Medical Care in the US for Visitors From India and Medical Care in the US for Visitors From Mexico. The US pharmacy guide for international visitors explains how American pharmacies work, and the broader international visitors page explains how TeleDirectMD works for people who live outside the US.

TeleDirectMD treats adults 18 and older. The section on a sick child is educational: it lists the warning signs that mean 911 and where children can be seen in person.

Emergency first: when to call 911

Start with severity, not cost or convenience. In New York City, 911 is the number for fire, police and medical emergencies.[17] You can dial it from a mobile phone, including a foreign phone roaming in the US.

Call 911 for these

  • Possible heart attack: chest pain or discomfort; pain or discomfort in the jaw, neck, back, or one or both arms or shoulders; shortness of breath; or feeling weak, light-headed or faint.[7]
  • Possible stroke: sudden numbness or weakness in the face, arm or leg, especially on one side; sudden confusion or trouble speaking; sudden trouble seeing; sudden trouble walking or loss of balance; or a sudden severe headache with no known cause. The CDC's B.E. F.A.S.T. check covers balance, eyes, face, arms and speech, and the T stands for time: call 911 right away.[8]
  • Breathing problems, choking, or swelling of the face, eyes or tongue.[20]
  • Serious injury: a head or spine injury, a deep or large wound, burns or smoke inhalation, an injury from a traffic collision, or bleeding that will not stop.[20]
  • A change in mental status, such as unusual behavior, confusion or difficulty waking, or fainting.[20]
  • Severe abdominal pain, coughing up or vomiting blood, or severe or persistent vomiting or diarrhea.[20]
  • Thoughts of suicide. This is a medical emergency too. You can also call or text 988, described in the mental health section.[20]

What the hospital must do, and what it does not mean

The federal Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act (EMTALA) applies to Medicare-participating hospitals that offer emergency services. When someone comes to the emergency department and asks for care, the hospital must provide a medical screening exam, regardless of the person's ability to pay, and must stabilize an emergency medical condition or arrange an appropriate transfer.[2]

That protects your access to emergency care. It does not make the care free. You will be billed afterward, so keep your passport details, travel insurance information and a working email address handy for the registration desk. In an emergency, do not delay a 911 call to pick a hospital; the ambulance crew will take you to an appropriate one.

What an ambulance costs in New York City

Calling 911 is free. The ambulance ride is not. The Fire Department of the City of New York (FDNY) runs the city's EMS service and charges for treatment and transport based on the services provided.[22] Its published rates, effective July 1, 2026, are:

The city's 11 public hospitals

New York City has a public hospital system alongside its private hospitals. NYC Health + Hospitals runs 11 hospitals across four boroughs.[3][23] The city's 311 service states that patients can receive care there regardless of ability to pay or immigration status, and that information, including immigration status, is kept confidential.[3] A 2025 open letter from the city's health department, NYC Health + Hospitals and the Mayor's Office of Immigrant Affairs said the same: people have the right to care regardless of immigration status and are not required to share it.[5]

How to read the table

The rows are not ranked. Each one answers a different need. An emergency department is the only right answer for a red-flag symptom. Urgent care centers and ExpressCare walk-in sites can examine you in person, and urgent care centers can often test on site. The Sexual Health Clinics and NYU Dentistry cover two needs that general urgent care often handles poorly. ExpressCare and telehealth both work by video, which suits problems a clinician can assess by talking with you and looking at what the camera can show.

If you have US insurance from another state, call the number on your card before choosing; your plan's network rules, not this guide, decide what it covers. If you have travel insurance, check whether it requires you to call an assistance line before non-emergency care, because some policies do.

What care costs when you are not insured here

There is no single price for a doctor visit in New York City. Each provider sets its own self-pay rates, and few publish them. What a visitor can rely on:

  • Emergency departments must screen and stabilize you regardless of ability to pay, and bill afterward.[2] The bill depends on what was done.
  • Ambulance transport by FDNY has published rates, listed above, starting at $1,793 for basic life support plus mileage.[22]
  • Urgent care centers set their own self-pay prices. CityMD's payment page describes uninsured rates without listing an amount, so ask before you are seen.[9]
  • A good faith estimate is yours to ask for. Under federal rules, if you are not using health insurance, a provider usually must give you a good faith estimate of expected charges if you request one or schedule services at least 3 business days in advance. CMS notes that you need the estimate if you later want to dispute a bill.[24]
  • NYU Dentistry urgent care charges an $85 fee for a limited exam and applicable X-rays, with pain-relief procedures billed separately.[30]
  • NYC Sexual Health Clinics provide low- to no-cost STI and HIV services, and you can still be seen if you cannot pay the sliding scale fee.[31]
  • A TeleDirectMD video visit is a flat $79 paid at booking, with any medication paid for separately at the pharmacy.

NYC Care, the city's program with no membership fees or premiums and charges based on family size and income, is only for people who live in the five boroughs, so visitors do not qualify.[19] Whatever you use, ask for an itemized receipt. Travel insurers that reimburse generally want copies of bills and invoices, and CDC advises keeping all records, charges and receipts.[18]

How a video visit works when you are visiting New York

A video visit is an ordinary medical visit held over a secure video connection. For a visitor, five details matter more than they do for a resident.

You must be physically in New York during the visit

For telehealth, the physician must be licensed where the patient is physically located during the visit. TeleDirectMD follows that rule: if you are in New York State when the visit starts, a physician licensed in New York can see you, whether you live in Manhattan, Ohio or Madrid. Your home address does not decide it. Your location at the time of the visit does. New York's Office of the Professions states that full licensure and current registration are required of any professional who practices in New York State.[21]

This trips people up near the edges of the city. Newark Liberty International Airport is in New Jersey, not New York. If you plan to do the visit from an airport gate or a day trip, check the states TeleDirectMD serves for where you will actually be. TeleDirectMD sees adults in 40+ states; the New York page covers what is available here.

Book, pay and verify who you are

You choose an open time on the booking page and pay the flat $79 by card at booking. Have a photo ID ready; for most international visitors, that is a passport. In New York, visits are self-pay. If you have a US flexible spending or health savings account, the visit is FSA/HSA eligible.

Language

Dr. Bhavsar sees patients in English, Hindi, Gujarati and Urdu. Dr. Bhavsar does not speak Spanish. For Spanish or any of 300+ other languages, a live medical interpreter from Jeenie can join the video visit. Mention the language you need when you book.

Choose a New York pharmacy before the visit

Pick a pharmacy close to where you are staying and note its name and street address. If the physician decides a prescription is appropriate, it is sent electronically to that pharmacy. New York law requires prescriptions issued in the state to be made electronically, with narrow exceptions, and the state's mandate covers both controlled and non-controlled medicines.[10][11] You will not receive a paper prescription to carry around. Late at night, confirm the pharmacy is open before you choose it.

Have your information in front of you

  • A list of every medicine you take, with the generic name, strength and how often you take it. Brand names can differ between countries. CDC travel guidance advises keeping medicines in their original, labeled containers showing the generic and brand name and the exact dosage, and bringing copies of prescriptions that include the generic names.[13]
  • The original containers or blister packs, if you have them with you, so the physician can see exactly what you take.
  • A letter from your home doctor, if you have one. The CDC recommends a letter on office letterhead listing conditions, medicines with generic names and doses, and allergies.[15]
  • Your allergies, especially to antibiotics, and whether you could be pregnant.
  • Good light and a quiet room. A hotel bathroom mirror light is often the best light for showing a rash, a red eye or the back of the throat.

The physician decides what is appropriate after hearing the full story. Sometimes that is a prescription. Often it is advice on over-the-counter care, a clear list of warning signs, or a recommendation to be seen in person because the problem needs an exam or a test that video cannot provide.

Prescriptions: why your home prescription will not work here

Many visitors do not expect this: a valid prescription from home, even for a common medicine, cannot be handed to a New York pharmacist. The reason is legal, not bureaucratic.

New York's Education Law says a prescription drug may be dispensed only on a prescription written by a person legally authorized to issue it.[12] A physician licensed only in another country is not authorized to prescribe in New York.[21] Separately, New York's Public Health Law requires prescriptions issued in the state to be made electronically, with limited exceptions.[10] A paper prescription from abroad meets neither requirement.

Bringing your own medicine into the US

U.S. Customs and Border Protection advises non-US citizens to carry a valid prescription or doctor's note written in English, to keep each medicine in its original container with the doctor's instructions, and, as a rule of thumb, to bring no more than a 90-day supply.[25] The FDA likewise allows foreign nationals to bring or ship a 90-day supply for personal use.[14] Declare all drugs and medicinal products to the CBP officer. Some medicines that are legal elsewhere, including certain drugs with a high potential for abuse, may not enter the country if the FDA has not approved them, and will be confiscated even with a foreign prescription.[25]

What you can do

  • Bring enough of your regular medicines. Keep them in your carry-on, in their original containers where possible.[13][15]
  • If you run out or lose them, a US-licensed clinician can review what you take and decide whether a short bridge prescription is appropriate for a non-controlled medicine. The generic names on your list make that review faster and safer. The guide to running out of medication in the US covers this in detail. If what ran out is a psoriasis cream or ointment, the page on psoriasis refills in New York explains how a video visit can continue an established topical treatment.
  • Expect the medicine itself to cost extra. The pharmacy charges for the medication separately from any visit fee. Ask the pharmacist for the cash price before it is filled.

Controlled medicines are a different category. TeleDirectMD does not prescribe controlled substances. If you take one, bring your supply and plan for in-person care if you need more.

Common visitor problems that can fit a video visit

These are conditions listed on TeleDirectMD's conditions page that commonly come up during a trip. A video visit is a reasonable first step for each when you have no warning signs. The physician may still direct you to in-person care after hearing the details.

  • Sore throat. Common after flights and crowded venues. A video visit cannot collect a throat swab, so if strep testing is needed, the physician will tell you where to get it. The adult sore throat guide explains when testing makes sense.
  • Common cold and sinus infection. Most colds are viral and need symptom care rather than antibiotics; the physician decides whether a sinus infection needs more.
  • Flu and COVID-19. Some treatments are time-sensitive, so it is better to be assessed early than to wait until you are home.
  • Urinary tract infection, for women only, when it is uncomplicated and the patient is not pregnant. Fever, back or flank pain, or vomiting needs in-person care.
  • Pink eye. Eye pain, light sensitivity, a change in vision, or contact lens wear with a painful red eye needs an in-person eye exam.
  • Traveler's diarrhea, for visitors who arrive unwell after travel. Blood in the stool, high fever or signs of dehydration need in-person care.
  • Stomach bug (viral gastroenteritis), when you can keep fluids down.
  • Tooth pain, as a bridge until you can see a dentist. The dental section below covers where to go in person.
  • Doxy PEP for STI prevention, for eligible adults after a possible exposure. The doxy PEP guide explains who it is for.
  • Birth control refills, for adults already on a pill, patch or ring who run short on the trip.
  • Motion sickness, for example before a ferry, a long car trip or the flight home.
  • Skin rash from contact with something new (contact dermatitis), such as a different soap, detergent or sunscreen.
  • Cold sores, which can flare with travel stress, wind and sun.
  • Seasonal allergies, which can catch visitors off guard when New York's pollen season differs from home. The seasonal allergies guide covers treatment options.
  • Ear pain, including pressure that lingers after a flight.

Tooth pain and dental emergencies

A cracked tooth or a throbbing molar is one of the harder problems to solve on a trip, because most dentists book by appointment and general urgent care centers do not treat teeth.

Walk-in dental urgent care

NYU College of Dentistry, at 345 E. 24th Street at the corner of First Avenue in Manhattan, provides urgent care for pain, excessive bleeding, swelling, oral infection or trauma with no appointment, on a first-come, first-served basis.[30] Its regular urgent care hours, starting September 8, 2026, are Monday to Thursday 8:30 AM to 3:00 PM and Friday 8:30 AM to 12:00 PM, with early closings on some holidays, so check the page the day you go.[30]

  • Cost: an $85 fee covers a limited exam and applicable X-rays. Procedures to relieve pain are charged in addition.[30]
  • What to bring: NYU asks new patients for proof of identification, such as a passport, and proof of address. A visitor may not have a US bill in their name, so ask at the desk what they accept before you wait in line.[30]
  • When it is closed: NYU directs patients with pain, excessive bleeding, swelling, oral infection or trauma to the nearest hospital emergency room, and names Bellevue, at 462 First Avenue, as the closest.[30]

When tooth pain is an emergency

Call 911 or go to an emergency department for swelling of the face, eyes or tongue, or for any trouble breathing.[20] A dental infection that spreads into the face or neck is not a problem to manage from a hotel room.

What a video visit can and cannot do

A video visit cannot fix a tooth. It can help with pain control and with deciding whether an antibiotic is appropriate while you arrange a dentist, which the dental pain guide explains in detail. Most tooth pain needs a dentist, not an antibiotic.

Sexual health, emergency contraception and HIV PEP

New York City runs its own Sexual Health Clinics through the health department. They provide low- to no-cost services for sexually transmitted infections, including HIV, regardless of immigration status, and you can still get services if you have no health insurance or cannot pay the sliding scale fee.[31] Most clinic services are walk-in only, and clinics may close early once they reach capacity, so arrive early.[31]

HIV PEP is time-critical

Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) is medication taken after a possible HIV exposure. The city states that it must be started within 72 hours of exposure. If the Sexual Health Clinics are closed, call the NYC 24/7 PEP Hotline at 844-3-PEPNYC (844-373-7692) or go to an emergency room.[31] Do not wait to get home to start it.

Emergency contraception

Levonorgestrel emergency contraception, such as Plan B One-Step, is sold without a prescription and without age restrictions. FDA advises taking it as soon as possible within 72 hours after unprotected sex or contraceptive failure.[29] The Sexual Health Clinics also provide emergency contraception.[31]

Doxy PEP and STI testing

Doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis (doxy PEP), taken after sex, can prevent some bacterial STIs, including chlamydia, gonorrhea and syphilis. The city's clinics offer it by walk-in.[31] A TeleDirectMD physician can also assess whether doxy PEP fits an adult's situation; the doxy PEP guide covers the evidence, and the STI testing guide covers when and how to test. Testing itself needs a lab or clinic.

Seasonal risks: heat, flu and measles

Summer heat

Heat is the weather risk visitors most underestimate in New York. The city health department reports that about 500 New Yorkers die from heat-related causes each year, and about 80% of those deaths occur on hot but not extreme heat days, when temperatures are between 82 and 94 degrees Fahrenheit.[33] A heat index above 95°F is especially dangerous.[32] A visitor who walks the city all day, often in the sun, can get into trouble on a day that does not feel like a heat wave.

  • Heat stroke, call 911: hot, dry skin; confusion, hallucinations or disorientation; loss of consciousness or being unresponsive; nausea or vomiting; trouble breathing; a fast, strong pulse; weakness; or dizziness.[32]
  • Heat exhaustion, cool down now: heavy sweating, cold and clammy skin, muscle cramps, lightheadedness or feeling faint, headache, decreased energy, or loss of appetite or nausea. Get to a cool place, remove extra clothes and drink lots of water.[32]
  • Where to cool off: during a heat wave, call 311 or use the city's online cooling center finder.[32]
  • Smoke or poor air quality during heat: the health department advises that staying cool is most important, because sealing yourself inside a hot room can be more dangerous than being outdoors.[32]

Flu and respiratory season

Flu season in New York usually runs through the winter months. Some flu treatments work best when started early, so a visitor with sudden fever, aches and cough should be assessed rather than wait until the flight home. The flu hub tracks current activity, and the flu treatment guide covers what helps.

Measles

As of November 27, 2026, New York City had eight confirmed measles cases in 2026.[34] Nationally, CDC reported 6,039 confirmed cases in 2026 as of November 24.[35] If you develop fever with a rash and think it could be measles, the city's advice is to call before going to a provider, so the clinic or hospital can keep others from being exposed.[34]

Pollen

Spring tree pollen and late-summer weed pollen can hit visitors whose home season is different. Over-the-counter antihistamines are widely available; the seasonal allergies guide explains the options.

Food, water and bed bugs

Restaurant letter grades

New York City restaurants are inspected and graded by the health department. An inspection score of 0 to 13 is an A, 14 to 27 points is a B, and 28 or more points is a C, and grade cards must be posted where people passing by can easily see them.[36] Lower scores mean fewer violations. A grade is a snapshot of one inspection, not a guarantee, but it is a reasonable quick check.

Tap water

The city's Department of Environmental Protection states that New York City continues to enjoy some of the best tap water in the world, and publishes an annual drinking water quality report.[37] Visitors can generally drink it from the tap. If you have a weakened immune system, ask your own doctor whether you need to take extra precautions with any water supply.

If your stomach turns

Most stomach illness on a trip is a short viral illness or a reaction to unfamiliar food. Sip fluids and watch for the warning signs in the stomach flu guide. Blood in the stool, high fever or signs of dehydration need in-person care.

Bed bugs

Bed bugs cause itchy bites and real distress, but the EPA states that they are not known to transmit or spread disease. Experts believe the recent increase in the US may be due partly to more travel.[38] If you wake with new bites in a line or cluster, tell the hotel, photograph the bites and the mattress seams, and ask for another room. Treat the itch the way you would any bite. Spreading redness, warmth, pus or fever around a bite points to a skin infection and needs a clinician.

Mental health and 988

A trip can bring on panic, low mood or a crisis, especially far from home. The 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline provides free and confidential support 24 hours a day.[20] In New York City, 988 counselors can help in over 200 languages over the phone.[39] Call or text 988. If you or someone with you is in immediate danger, or has attempted suicide, call 911.[20]

For concerns that are not a crisis, NYC Health + Hospitals ExpressCare offers video and phone visits around the clock.[3]

Students, exchange visitors and business travelers

Students and J-1 exchange visitors

If you are in New York on a J-1 exchange visitor program, federal rules require your sponsor to make sure you have insurance covering sickness and accidents for the length of the program. The minimum coverage is $100,000 per accident or illness, $25,000 for repatriation of remains, $50,000 for medical evacuation to your home country, and a deductible of no more than $500 per accident or illness.[40] Before you need care, find your policy card and the insurer's claims or assistance number.

Students on a university program should check the school's student health service first; it may be the fastest and least expensive route for routine problems. The options in this guide cover evenings, weekends and the gaps between.

Business travelers

Employer travel policies often route care through an assistance company. Call it before non-emergency care if your policy requires that, and keep every receipt for the expense report and the claim.[18] A visitor with US insurance from another state should call the number on the card to check network rules before choosing a provider.

If a child traveling with you is sick

TeleDirectMD treats adults 18 and older only, so this section is for education. A child who is sick in New York should be seen by an in-person option that treats children: a pediatric or family urgent care center that confirms it sees children, or a hospital emergency department.

MedlinePlus lists these warning signs of a medical emergency in children. Call 911 for any of them:[20]

  • Bluish or grey color of the skin
  • Difficulty eating or feeding
  • Fever followed by a change in mental status, or stiffness in the neck or back
  • Increased sleepiness, lack of alertness or a diminished response
  • A seizure
  • Severe headache or vomiting, especially after a head injury
  • Trouble breathing or abnormal breathing
  • Being unable to stand or walk
  • Swelling of the face, eyes or tongue
  • Bleeding that will not stop

Babies and children up to 4 years old are more sensitive to heat than adults. Make sure they stay cool and drink enough, watch for signs of heat illness, and never leave a child alone in a vehicle.[32]

The trip home: jet lag, blood clots and flying with medicine

Jet lag

Direction matters. The CDC Yellow Book explains that westward travel is easier to adapt to than eastward travel, with an average adaptation of 1.5 hours a day going west and 1 hour a day going east.[41] A flight from New York to Europe, the Middle East or India crosses time zones eastward, so expect the adjustment at home to take longer than the one on arrival.

  • Shift your sleep before you fly. If possible, move your sleep time closer to your destination's schedule for a few days before the trip.[41]
  • Use light on purpose. Getting, or avoiding, light at the right times of day helps the body clock adjust.[41]
  • Melatonin, if you use it, at a low dose. CDC notes that 0.5 to 1 mg is often sufficient, and advises against high doses above 5 mg.[41]
  • Skip sedating antihistamines and long-acting sleeping pills for jet lag. CDC advises avoiding them because they can worsen thinking, raise fall risk and impair physical performance.[41]

Blood clots on long flights

Anyone traveling more than 4 hours by air, car or bus can be at risk for blood clots, and the longer you are immobile, the higher the risk.[42] Move your legs often, exercise your calf muscles, and get up to stretch when you can.[42]

  • Deep vein thrombosis signs, contact a doctor as soon as possible: swelling of a leg or arm; pain or tenderness you cannot explain; skin that is warm to the touch; or redness or discoloration.[42]
  • Pulmonary embolism signs, seek medical help immediately: difficulty breathing; a faster than normal or irregular heartbeat; chest pain that usually worsens with a deep breath or cough; coughing up blood; or lightheadedness or fainting.[42]

Medicine at airport security

TSA allows larger amounts of medically necessary liquids, gels and aerosols in reasonable quantities for your trip, but you must declare them to TSA officers at the checkpoint.[43] Keep your medicines in your carry-on in case your checked bag is lost or delayed.[13]

What does not fit a video visit

Video is a good tool for a narrow set of problems. It is the wrong tool for these:

  • Emergencies. Any symptom in the 911 list above. A video visit adds delay.
  • Anything that needs hands, equipment or a lab. Cuts that may need stitches, possible fractures or sprains that need an X-ray, a throat swab, a pregnancy-related concern, or symptoms that need blood tests today. Urgent care, an ExpressCare walk-in site or an emergency department fits better.
  • A possible HIV exposure. PEP has to start within 72 hours; go to a Sexual Health Clinic, call the 24/7 PEP Hotline or go to an emergency room.[31]
  • A dental problem that needs a dentist. A broken tooth, an abscess or swelling needs hands-on dental care; see the dental section.
  • Controlled substances. TeleDirectMD does not prescribe them, including sleep, anxiety, ADHD and opioid pain medicines.
  • Children. TeleDirectMD sees adults 18 and older only. See the section on children.
  • Urinary symptoms in men, or a UTI with fever, back pain or pregnancy. These need in-person evaluation.
  • Symptoms that are getting worse after a visit. A plan made by video depends on the illness following the expected course. If it does not, be seen in person.

Practical New York logistics

Pharmacies, including late at night

Pharmacies are spread across all five boroughs, and some are open 24 hours.[16] Hours change, and a store that is open around the clock may close its pharmacy counter overnight. Before you send a prescription late in the evening, check the pharmacy counter's hours in the chain's app or by phone. When you pick up, bring photo ID in case the pharmacy asks for it, and expect to pay at the counter.

311 for city information

NYC311 is the city's line for non-emergency information and services, and it answers 24 hours a day, every day. From outside New York City, or from an internet (VoIP) phone service, call 212-NEW-YORK (212-639-9675). The call center can help in over 175 languages when you ask for an interpreter. For a medical emergency, call 911, not 311.[17] The 311 site also explains how to reach NYC Health + Hospitals for non-emergency appointments.[3]

Tell the hotel front desk

  • If you call 911 from your room, call the front desk right after. Staff can meet the responders, hold an elevator and bring them to the right floor.
  • If you are sick but stable, tell the desk anyway. They can let housekeeping know, and many can tell you the nearest pharmacy and its hours.
  • If the hotel offers a doctor who comes to the room, ask who provides the service and what the total fee will be before you agree.

Receipts and travel insurance

CDC guidance for travelers advises being ready to pay at the time of care, then sending the insurer copies of bills and invoices, and keeping all records, charges and receipts.[18] For a TeleDirectMD visit, you can request an itemized receipt by emailing contact@teledirectmd.com. Whether you are reimbursed depends entirely on your policy, so read its terms or call the assistance line before you assume it pays.

Questions about a visit, a receipt or whether your situation fits can go to contact@teledirectmd.com.
