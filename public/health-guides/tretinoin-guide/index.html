Tretinoin

A physician's evidence-based guide to prescription tretinoin for photoaging and acne, retinoid dermatitis management, and what actually works in 2026.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: July 18, 2026 · Fully rewritten Jul 2026
22 min read

What is the best treatment for photoaging and wrinkles in 2026?

For adults seeking evidence-based prevention and treatment of photoaging (sun-induced wrinkles, uneven pigmentation, and rough texture), two well-supported steps are daily broad-spectrum SPF 30+ sunscreen and prescription tretinoin. The FDA approved topical tretinoin for acne in 1971 and for the palliative treatment of photoaging in 1995; tazarotene 0.1% cream is also FDA-labeled for fine facial wrinkling. A 2022 systematic review of 7 randomized controlled trials found tretinoin improved wrinkling, mottled hyperpigmentation, sallowness, and lentigines, with improvement significant after 4 months and continuing over 24 months. In a 48-week trial, tretinoin 0.025% and 0.1% produced similar improvement, with significantly more irritation at 0.1%. Adapalene 0.3% gel was non-inferior to tretinoin 0.05% cream for photoaging (Bagatin 2018). The tretinoin label for photoaging says it should not be used during pregnancy. A licensed clinician, accessible via telehealth or in-person care, can prescribe tretinoin after reviewing skin type, medical history, and photoprotection habits.

Key Takeaways

  • Tretinoin is a gold-standard prescription retinoid for photoaging, approved by the FDA for acne in 1971 and for the palliative treatment of photoaging in 1995.[9] It is the most widely investigated retinoid for photoaging.[11]
  • Daily broad-spectrum SPF 30+ sunscreen is an essential partner. The AAD describes UVA rays as "aging rays" that can prematurely age skin, causing wrinkles and age spots.[10]
  • Higher strength is not automatically better. In a 48-week trial, tretinoin 0.025% and 0.1% produced similar improvement in photoaging, with significantly more irritation at 0.1%.[15]
  • Adapalene 0.3% gel was non-inferior to tretinoin 0.05% cream for photoaging, with a similar safety profile (Bagatin 2018), an option for mild or moderate photoaging.[5]
  • The AAD 2024 acne guidelines make a strong recommendation for topical retinoids, alongside benzoyl peroxide, topical antibiotics, and oral doxycycline.[6]
  • Early irritation is expected, not a failure. Dryness, redness, and peeling are common early on; a lower starting concentration, two or three nights a week at first, and moisturizer can help.[9]
  • Do not use tretinoin during pregnancy. The tretinoin label for photoaging says not to use it if pregnant or attempting to become pregnant; stop and contact a clinician if pregnancy occurs.[12]

Tretinoin, prescription-strength all-trans retinoic acid, is the most widely investigated retinoid for photoaging,[11] with randomized controlled trial evidence going back to 1988.[1] This guide is a physician's evidence-based look at how tretinoin works for photoaging and acne, how it compares to adapalene, tazarotene, and trifarotene, how to start it without unnecessary irritation, and what the evidence says about results. Material claims are cited to the trials, reviews, guidelines, and FDA labels listed in the references.

What Is Photoaging?

Photoaging is skin damage caused by long-term exposure to ultraviolet (UV) radiation from sunlight, distinct from intrinsic (chronological) aging. Long-term UV exposure causes premature skin aging, characterized in part by wrinkles, altered pigmentation, and loss of skin tone.[8] The American Academy of Dermatology describes UVA rays as "aging rays" that can prematurely age skin, causing wrinkles and age spots.[10]

At the cellular level, repeated UV exposure leads to sustained elevations of matrix metalloproteinases, enzymes that degrade skin collagen.[8] UVA penetrates deeper into the skin than UVB and damages the dermal extracellular matrix and blood vessels. Over years of exposure, this produces the clinical hallmarks of photoaging:[4]

  • Fine and coarse wrinkling
  • Mottled hyperpigmentation and solar lentigines ("age spots" or "sun spots")
  • Sallowness, a dull, yellowish skin tone
  • Skin roughness and textural irregularity
  • Telangiectasias (visible small blood vessels)

Unlike intrinsic aging, photoaging is partly reversible: biopsy studies show that collagen formation is reduced in photodamaged skin and partly restored by tretinoin.[2] This is a central reason tretinoin and sunscreen remain foundational for most people. Work by Fisher and colleagues in the New England Journal of Medicine showed that UV light induces collagen-degrading enzymes in human skin and that pretreatment with topical tretinoin inhibited that induction.[8]

How Tretinoin Works

Cellular mechanism

Tretinoin is all-trans retinoic acid, the biologically active form of vitamin A. Unlike over-the-counter retinol or retinyl esters, tretinoin requires no enzymatic conversion, it binds directly to nuclear retinoic acid receptors (RARs) and retinoid X receptors (RXRs) in keratinocytes and fibroblasts, directly regulating gene transcription. This produces several measurable effects:

  • Increased keratinocyte turnover, which thins the stratum corneum, smooths surface texture, and helps clear comedones
  • Increased collagen formation: in photodamaged skin, tretinoin produced an 80 percent increase in collagen I formation, compared with a 14 percent decrease on vehicle[2]
  • Inhibition of UV-induced matrix metalloproteinases, slowing further collagen breakdown[8]
  • Reduced epidermal melanin and pigmentation, likely through faster keratinocyte turnover and reduced tyrosinase activity, which improves mottled hyperpigmentation over months of use[4]
  • Action against comedones and microcomedones, plus direct anti-inflammatory effects, the basis for its acne indication[13]

The 1993 Griffiths study in the New England Journal of Medicine used skin biopsies to show that collagen I formation was 56 percent lower in photodamaged skin than in sun-protected skin, and that 10 to 12 months of daily 0.1 percent tretinoin cream increased collagen I formation by 80 percent, compared with a 14 percent decrease on vehicle. The authors concluded that collagen formation "is partly restored by treatment with tretinoin."[2]

What tretinoin does not do

Tretinoin does not tighten skin, remove deep static wrinkles caused by volume loss, or replace lost subcutaneous fat. It is a treatment for the epidermal and superficial dermal changes of photoaging and for follicular changes in acne, not a substitute for procedures that address structural volume loss (fillers) or significant skin laxity (energy-based devices, surgical lifting). Setting this expectation correctly is one of the most important parts of a good tretinoin conversation. Patients who expect tretinoin to produce facelift-level change are likely to be disappointed and may stop a genuinely beneficial medication prematurely; patients who understand it as a slow, cumulative improvement in skin quality, tone, and fine lines tend to stay adherent long enough to see the real benefit.

Tretinoin also does not treat active sunburn, does not function as a moisturizer (and in fact tends to be mildly drying, particularly during the adjustment period), and does not substitute for basic photoprotection. It works best as one component of a broader photoaging-prevention regimen rather than as a stand-alone fix.

The retinoid family

"Retinoid" is an umbrella term for vitamin A derivatives. They differ in receptor selectivity, potency, and how much biotransformation is required before they become active:

  • Retinol (OTC): must be converted to retinaldehyde, then to retinoic acid, inside the skin, a two-step process that is inefficient and produces a much weaker, gentler effect
  • Retinaldehyde (OTC): one conversion step closer to active acid; intermediate potency
  • Tretinoin (Rx): the active acid itself; no conversion needed; possibly the most potent and the most widely investigated retinoid for photoaging[11]
  • Adapalene (OTC 0.1% / Rx 0.3%): a synthetic retinoid that selectively binds RAR-β and RAR-γ; different receptor profile than tretinoin but clinically effective, and in acne trials better tolerated than several tretinoin formulations[13][19][20]
  • Tazarotene (Rx): a synthetic retinoid prodrug, converted to its active form (tazarotenic acid) in skin; in acne, tazarotene 0.1% was more effective than tretinoin 0.025% or adapalene 0.1%[13]
  • Trifarotene (Rx): first approved in the U.S. in 2019 and labeled for acne of the face and/or trunk in patients 9 years of age and older[18]
The purge phase is temporary, do not stop

Dryness, redness, or peeling in the early weeks is common and expected, and usually not a sign the medication is failing. For acne, the tretinoin label notes that an apparent flare of inflammatory lesions may occur during the early weeks of therapy and "should not be considered a reason to discontinue therapy."[16]

What's Changed in 2024 to 2026

Several recent developments are relevant to a modern tretinoin conversation, spanning clinical guidance, labeling, and how patients now typically access a prescription.

The AAD published updated acne guidelines in 2024. The American Academy of Dermatology's 2024 guidelines of care for acne vulgaris, built on a systematic review using the GRADE approach, make strong recommendations for benzoyl peroxide, topical retinoids, topical antibiotics, and oral doxycycline.[6] They also list combining topical therapies with multiple mechanisms of action as a good practice statement.[6]

Trifarotene is labeled for acne of the face and trunk. AKLIEF (trifarotene) 0.005% cream is indicated for acne vulgaris in patients 9 years of age and older and is applied to the affected areas of the face and/or trunk.[18]

Tretinoin can be prescribed through telehealth. Tretinoin is a non-controlled prescription medication, and licensed clinicians can evaluate skin type, acne severity or photoaging signs, pregnancy status, and photoprotection habits via a synchronous telehealth visit and prescribe an appropriate strength without an in-person exam in most straightforward cases.

Tretinoin for Photoaging: The Evidence

The landmark trials

Tretinoin's photoaging indication rests on a long evidence base in topical dermatology. The 1988 Weiss study, published in JAMA, was a 16-week randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled trial: all 30 patients who completed it improved on the tretinoin-treated forearm but not the vehicle-treated forearm, and 14 of the 15 patients who applied tretinoin to the face improved, versus none of the vehicle-treated faces.[1] The FDA approved tretinoin for the palliative treatment of photoaging in 1995.[9]

Five years later, the 1993 Griffiths study in the New England Journal of Medicine used skin biopsies to show that tretinoin partly restored collagen I formation in photodamaged skin, histologic evidence of an effect beyond surface appearance.[2]

A two-year, randomized, placebo-controlled trial of 204 subjects (Kang et al., 2005) found that tretinoin emollient cream 0.05% produced significantly greater improvement than placebo in fine and coarse wrinkling, mottled hyperpigmentation, lentigines, and sallowness, with no increase in keratinocytic or melanocytic atypia on biopsy, and concluded that long-term treatment is safe and effective.[3]

Dose-response and duration

A 2022 systematic review in the International Journal of Women's Dermatology identified 7 randomized controlled trials of topical tretinoin for photoaging; because the studies were heterogeneous, a meta-analysis was not performed.[4] The included studies consistently reported that tretinoin improved wrinkling, mottled hyperpigmentation, sallowness, and lentigines, in some studies as early as 1 month, with improvement significant after 4 months and continuing over a 24-month period.[4] The time course below combines that review with the trials and FDA labels:

Over the next 24 weeks, the improvement was sustained with three-times-weekly applications and to a lesser extent with once-weekly dosing, while effects tended to regress in subjects who stopped.[7]

Fine vs coarse wrinkles

Tretinoin's labeled photoaging indication is fine facial wrinkles.[12] Evidence on coarse (deep, static) wrinkles is mixed: a two-year trial of tretinoin emollient cream 0.05% reported greater improvement than placebo in coarse wrinkling,[3] while the label for tretinoin cream 0.02% states it has not demonstrated a mitigating effect on coarse or deep wrinkling.[12] Deep wrinkles often involve structural changes (volume loss, deep dermal collagen architecture) that a topical medication cannot fully reverse, so tretinoin is best viewed as a treatment for early-to-moderate photoaging and as an adjunct to procedural treatment for advanced, deeply wrinkled skin.

Hyperpigmentation and lentigines

Tretinoin's effect on mottled hyperpigmentation and solar lentigines is one of its most patient-visible benefits. In the two-year Kang trial, tretinoin 0.05% improved mottled hyperpigmentation and lentigines more than placebo,[3] and the 2022 review notes that tretinoin may reduce epidermal melanin by increasing keratinocyte turnover and reducing tyrosinase activity.[4] This makes it a rational first choice for patients whose primary photoaging complaint is uneven pigmentation rather than wrinkling.

Clinically, this often means two patients starting tretinoin for the same reason, "sun damage", see somewhat different first results: one may notice pigmentation evening out first, while another notices texture and fine lines improving first, before pigmentation catches up. Both patterns are normal and both continue to improve with consistent use over the following months.

Tretinoin for Acne: The Evidence

AAD 2024 recommendations

The American Academy of Dermatology's 2024 guidelines of care for the management of acne vulgaris make a strong recommendation for topical retinoids, a class that includes tretinoin, adapalene, tazarotene, and trifarotene.[6]

The mechanism for acne differs slightly in emphasis from the photoaging mechanism: retinoids normalize the abnormal desquamation (shedding) of follicular keratinocytes that initiates comedone formation, and they have direct anti-inflammatory effects that reduce inflammatory lesion counts.[13]

Comparative efficacy

Comparative trials show all topical retinoids are effective as single agents in mild to moderate acne but differ in efficacy and tolerability:[13]

  • Tretinoin 0.025% and adapalene 0.1% were equally effective in head-to-head comparisons[13]
  • Adapalene 0.1% gel was significantly better tolerated than tazarotene 0.1% gel and several tretinoin formulations[13]
  • Tazarotene 0.1% was more effective than tretinoin 0.025% or 0.1% microsphere gel and adapalene 0.1%[13]
  • Trifarotene is labeled for acne of the face and/or trunk in patients 9 years of age and older[18]

Combination therapy

The AAD 2024 guidelines include a good practice statement recommending combining topical therapies with multiple mechanisms of action, and they make strong recommendations for benzoyl peroxide, topical retinoids, and topical antibiotics.[6] This reflects acne's multifactorial pathophysiology (abnormal keratinization, Cutibacterium acnes proliferation, inflammation, and sebum production): no single mechanism of action addresses all four drivers.

The pivotal adapalene head-to-head trial is Bagatin and colleagues' 2018 randomized, investigator-blinded study comparing adapalene 0.3% gel with tretinoin 0.05% cream once daily for 24 weeks: the two did not differ significantly on global photoaging, periorbital and forehead wrinkles, or pigmentation, and adapalene was non-inferior with a similar safety profile.[5] For tazarotene, a multicenter trial by Kang and colleagues comparing 0.01% to 0.1% tazarotene creams with tretinoin 0.05% emollient cream found week-24 treatment success of 67% with tazarotene 0.1% and 55% with tretinoin 0.05%, with local adverse events more frequent at higher tazarotene concentrations.[14]

How to Use Tretinoin: A Practical Protocol

Starting protocol

Irritant reactions such as burning, scaling, or dermatitis limit many patients' acceptance of retinoids, a problem more prominent with tretinoin and tazarotene.[11] A conservative start reduces irritation while still reaching the labeled once-daily schedule:

  • Starting: begin with a lower concentration, applied two or three nights a week, with moisturizer and broad-spectrum SPF 30+ sunscreen[9]
  • Building up: increase toward the labeled schedule of once a day before bedtime, using enough to cover the area lightly, as tolerated[16]
  • If irritation flares: the label directs using it less frequently, pausing temporarily, or stopping, depending on severity[16]
  • Maintenance: after 48 weeks of once-daily use, three-times-weekly application maintained photoaging improvement (Olsen et al., 1997)[7]

Application technique

Wash the face gently with a mild soap, pat dry, and wait 20 to 30 minutes before applying, as the tretinoin labels direct.[12][16] Apply a thin layer and keep it away from the eyes, the mouth, the angles of the nose, and mucous membranes.[16]

The pea-sized rule

Use a small amount. The tretinoin photoaging label calls for "a small pearl-sized (about ¼ inch or 5 mm diameter) amount of cream" for the face,[12] and the acne label warns that too much may irritate the skin and "won't give faster or better results."[16]

Timing and sun sensitivity

Tretinoin should be applied in the evening: the labels direct once-daily use in the evening or before retiring.[12][16] Because tretinoin can make skin more susceptible to sunburn, daytime sun protection matters throughout treatment.[16]

Managing Retinoid Dermatitis

The purge phase (early weeks)

Retinoid dermatitis (dryness, redness, peeling, itching, and burning or stinging) is the most commonly reported side effect of tretinoin and is typically mild to moderate and transient.[4][9] For acne, an apparent flare of inflammatory lesions may occur during the early weeks of therapy; the label says this "should not be considered a reason to discontinue therapy."[16] The label adds that results should be noticed after 2 to 3 weeks, but more than 6 weeks of therapy may be required before definite beneficial effects are seen.[16]

Buffering strategies

A 2025 review lists several ways to reduce irritation: introducing therapy with lower concentrations, applying two or three nights a week at first, and using moisturizers, together with broad-spectrum SPF 30+ sunscreen.[9]

  • Moisturizer sandwich: apply a bland, fragrance-free moisturizer, wait several minutes, apply tretinoin, then apply moisturizer again on top once the tretinoin has absorbed.
  • Two or three nights a week until tolerance builds (see starting protocol above)[9]
  • A small amount only; the label says using more "won't give faster or better results"[16]
  • Mild, bland cleanser; avoid medicated or abrasive soaps and cleansers during treatment[16]
  • Bland, fragrance-free moisturizer applied liberally on non-tretinoin mornings and nights

When to reduce frequency

If irritation is significant, the label directs using the medication less frequently, stopping temporarily, or stopping altogether, depending on severity.[16] Skin that becomes excessively red, swollen, blistered, or crusted, or a reaction suggesting sensitivity, calls for stopping tretinoin and contacting a clinician.[16]

Products to avoid concurrently

The tretinoin label urges caution when combining it with other products that can dry or irritate the skin:[16]

  • Products containing sulfur, resorcinol, or salicylic acid
  • Medicated or abrasive soaps and cleansers
  • Soaps and cosmetics with a strong drying effect
  • Products with high concentrations of alcohol, astringents, spices, or lime
Do not use during pregnancy

The tretinoin label for photoaging says it "should not be used during pregnancy" and not to use it if pregnant or attempting to become pregnant.[12] Stop tretinoin and contact a clinician if pregnancy is confirmed or suspected, and ask about alternatives.

Sunscreen: The Non-Negotiable Companion

Of every recommendation in this guide, this is the one clinicians repeat most often, and the one patients most often skip. Sunscreen is a core part of tretinoin therapy for two independent reasons. First, tretinoin may make skin more susceptible to sunburn and other adverse effects of the sun.[16] Second, UV exposure drives the photoaging that tretinoin is used to treat: the AAD describes UVA rays as "aging rays" that can prematurely age skin, causing wrinkles and age spots.[10] Using tretinoin without consistent sunscreen works against the treatment's own purpose.

The AAD recommends sunscreen that offers broad-spectrum (UVA and UVB) protection, has an SPF of 30 or higher, and is water resistant. It advises applying sunscreen every day on skin not covered by clothing if you will be outside, and reapplying approximately every two hours when outdoors.[10] The tretinoin photoaging label also advises avoiding or minimizing sun exposure, including sunlamps, and using protective clothing.[12]

Sunscreen is non-negotiable with tretinoin

If a patient will only reliably do one thing beyond taking the medication itself, make it daily sunscreen. It protects the investment tretinoin is making in the skin and prevents new photodamage from accumulating faster than tretinoin can repair the old damage.

Contraindications and Safety

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Tretinoin should not be used during pregnancy. The photoaging label states that tretinoin cream 0.02% "should not be used during pregnancy" and not to use it if pregnant or attempting to become pregnant;[12] the acne label notes there are no adequate and well-controlled studies in pregnant women and that oral tretinoin is teratogenic in animals.[16] Absorption through the skin is minimal (about 1 to 2 percent), and a 2025 review found that prospective and retrospective studies did not show an association between topical tretinoin exposure in pregnancy and congenital malformations, while still advising against use.[9] Whether tretinoin passes into breast milk is not known, and the acne label advises caution when it is used by a nursing woman.[16] Effective contraception should be discussed before starting tretinoin in patients who could become pregnant, and tretinoin should be stopped if pregnancy is confirmed.

Photosensitivity

Tretinoin may make skin more susceptible to sunburn and other adverse effects of the sun, and some people have heightened susceptibility to sunlight during treatment.[16] Weather extremes such as wind or cold may also be irritating.[16] This is a second, independent reason (beyond preventing photoaging itself) that daily broad-spectrum sunscreen matters during tretinoin therapy.

Concurrent medications and procedures

Patients should tell their clinicians about tretinoin use before starting other topical treatments or skin procedures. The acne label advises not using other medications with tretinoin unless a doctor recommends them.[16] Relevant considerations:

  • Chemical peels, laser resurfacing, waxing, and electrolysis: tell the person performing the procedure that you use tretinoin and ask whether to pause it beforehand; a 2025 review notes that published pre-laser tretinoin protocols rest mainly on anecdotal evidence and personal experience[9]
  • Other topical retinoids or exfoliants: do not combine tretinoin with other medications your doctor has not recommended[16]
  • Isotretinoin (oral): ask the prescribing clinician before using topical tretinoin during oral isotretinoin therapy
  • Sulfur-, resorcinol-, or salicylic acid-containing products: the label advises particular caution when using them with tretinoin[16]

None of these interactions are absolute contraindications to tretinoin therapy, they are considerations that call for spacing, sequencing, or a brief pause around a specific procedure rather than permanent discontinuation. A clinician familiar with a patient's full skincare and procedural history is best positioned to sequence these appropriately.

What Doesn't Work: Retinol Myths, Copper Peptides, and "Alternatives"

Because tretinoin requires a prescription and carries a real, if usually mild, side-effect burden, the skincare market is full of alternatives marketed as equivalent without the downsides. The evidence for most of these is thin.

"Retinol is basically the same as tretinoin, just gentler." This oversimplifies real pharmacology. Retinol must undergo two sequential enzymatic conversions in the skin (to retinaldehyde, then to retinoic acid) before it becomes biologically active, and this conversion is inefficient. The practical result is a meaningfully weaker effect at any given concentration, useful for very sensitive skin or as a gentle introduction to retinoids, but not a substitute for prescription tretinoin when a robust photoaging or acne effect is the goal. Retinol and retinaldehyde are considerably less irritating than tretinoin and tazarotene.[11]

Copper peptides. Marketed heavily for "collagen stimulation." A PubMed search run for this review in November 2026 found no randomized trial comparing copper peptides with tretinoin for photoaging. They should not be positioned as a tretinoin equivalent.

Bakuchiol. A plant-derived compound marketed as a "natural retinol alternative." In a 12-week randomized, double-blind trial of 44 patients, bakuchiol 0.5% cream twice daily and retinol 0.5% cream daily both decreased wrinkle surface area and hyperpigmentation with no statistical difference, and retinol users reported more scaling and stinging.[21] That trial compared bakuchiol with over-the-counter retinol, not prescription tretinoin, so it should be considered a gentler option rather than a tretinoin replacement.

"Retinol creams that work overnight" and similarly implausible marketing claims. No topical ingredient, tretinoin included, produces meaningful photoaging reversal in days. The landmark tretinoin trial measured improvement at 16 weeks,[1] and the photoaging label notes that up to 6 months of therapy may be required before effects are seen.[12] Any product claiming faster dramatic results should be viewed skeptically.

Extremely high-concentration OTC retinol serums. A higher percentage on the label does not by itself establish efficacy or safety; look for published trial data before assuming a high-percentage serum works better or is safe to use.

The broader pattern across all of these alternatives is the same: none of them has been studied with the depth, duration, or methodological rigor of tretinoin's evidence base, which spans double-blind vehicle-controlled trials, histologic biopsy-confirmed mechanism studies, and multi-year randomized follow-up.[11] That does not mean gentler alternatives have no role, for genuinely retinoid-intolerant skin, a well-formulated retinol or adapalene product used consistently is far better than no retinoid at all. But patients should choose alternatives with realistic expectations about the evidence gap, not marketing claims that imply equivalence to prescription tretinoin.

Red Flags: When to Contact a Clinician

Contact a clinician promptly if you experience any of the following while using tretinoin:

  • Severe swelling, blistering, crusting, or signs of a true allergic contact reaction, which is distinct from the expected mild dryness and redness of retinoid dermatitis and warrants stopping tretinoin and prompt evaluation[16]
  • Positive pregnancy test or suspected pregnancy: stop tretinoin and contact a clinician[12]
  • Severe sunburn or unusual sun sensitivity that occurs despite consistent sunscreen use
  • Signs of secondary skin infection, increasing warmth, spreading redness, pus, or fever, particularly if acne lesions have been manipulated or picked
  • Persistent, worsening irritation that does not respond to less frequent use or buffering strategies
  • Acne that is not improving after the treatment period your clinician expected (the acne label notes that more than 6 weeks may be needed before definite benefit)[16], which may indicate the need for a different regimen or combination therapy

For acne, the tretinoin label says results should be noticed after 2 to 3 weeks, but more than 6 weeks may be required before definite benefit.[16] For photoaging, the label says some effects may be noticed in 3 to 4 months and up to 6 months of therapy may be required;[12] in trials, improvement continued over 24 months of use.[4] Photoaging is a slow process to develop and a slow process to reverse; tretinoin is not a quick fix.

Tretinoin is prescription-strength all-trans retinoic acid that binds directly to retinoic acid receptors, requiring no conversion in the skin. Retinol is an over-the-counter precursor that the skin must convert to become active; retinol and retinaldehyde are considerably less irritating than tretinoin.[11] Adapalene is a synthetic retinoid, available over the counter at 0.1% and by prescription at 0.3%, both labeled for acne;[19][20] in a head-to-head photoaging trial, adapalene 0.3% gel was non-inferior to tretinoin 0.05% cream with a similar safety profile.[5]

The labeled schedule for tretinoin is once a day, in the evening or before retiring.[12][16] To limit irritation, a 2025 review suggests starting with a lower concentration two or three nights a week and building up.[9] For maintenance, one trial found that after 48 weeks of once-daily use, three-times-weekly application maintained photoaging improvement.[7]

For acne, the tretinoin label notes that an apparent flare of inflammatory lesions may occur during the early weeks of therapy and that this should not be considered a reason to stop.[16] It usually settles as the skin adapts; buffering strategies can reduce discomfort in the meantime. Severe redness, swelling, blistering, or crusting is different and needs a clinician's review.[16]

No. The tretinoin label for photoaging says it should not be used during pregnancy or by anyone attempting to become pregnant.[12] Absorption through the skin is minimal, and observational studies have not linked topical tretinoin in pregnancy to birth defects, but avoiding it is still advised.[9] It is not known whether tretinoin passes into breast milk, and the acne label advises caution during breastfeeding.[16] Stop tretinoin if pregnancy is confirmed or suspected, and ask a clinician about alternatives.

Yes. Tretinoin may make skin more susceptible to sunburn,[16] and UV exposure drives the photoaging tretinoin is meant to treat; the AAD describes UVA rays as aging rays that can cause wrinkles and age spots.[10] The AAD recommends broad-spectrum sunscreen with SPF 30 or higher, applied every day on exposed skin when you will be outside and reapplied about every two hours outdoors.[10]

In the evening. The tretinoin labels direct once-daily application in the evening or before retiring.[12][16] Use sunscreen during the day, since tretinoin can increase susceptibility to sunburn.[16]

Ask your clinician before layering other active products. The tretinoin label advises against using other medications your doctor has not recommended and urges particular caution with products containing sulfur, resorcinol, or salicylic acid.[16] Benzoyl peroxide is a strongly recommended acne treatment in the AAD 2024 guidelines, which also support combining topical therapies with different mechanisms of action;[6] your clinician can advise how to time it with tretinoin. This guide makes no specific recommendation on pairing tretinoin with vitamin C or niacinamide serums.

A small amount. The photoaging label calls for a small pearl-sized amount, about ¼ inch (5 mm) across, for the face,[12] and the acne label notes that too much won't give faster or better results and may irritate the skin.[16] Apply it as a thin layer to clean, dry skin (the labels say to wait 20 to 30 minutes after washing), avoiding the eyes, mouth, angles of the nose, and mucous membranes.[12][16]

Yes. Tretinoin is a non-controlled prescription medication, and a licensed clinician can evaluate skin type, medical history, pregnancy status, and photoprotection habits through a telehealth visit and prescribe an appropriate strength without an in-person exam in most straightforward cases.

In a 48-week double-blind trial, tretinoin 0.1% and 0.025% produced similar clinical and histologic improvement in photoaging, but irritation was significantly greater with 0.1% (Griffiths et al., 1995).[15] Because the higher concentration did not add benefit in that trial, many clinicians start retinoid-naive patients at a lower strength and increase only if needed.[9]
