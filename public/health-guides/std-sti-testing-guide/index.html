STD and STI Testing: Which Test, When, and Where (2026 Guide)

A practical decision guide grounded in the CDC 2021 STI Treatment Guidelines and USPSTF screening recommendations. Covers routine screening, symptomatic testing, home vs clinic collection, panel selection, and follow-up.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: September 10, 2026
Read time: 13 min

What STI tests do you actually need, and how often?

Routine STI screening usually involves some combination of HIV, syphilis, chlamydia, and gonorrhea testing, and who needs which test depends on age, sex, pregnancy and sexual exposures. USPSTF recommends HIV screening for adolescents and adults aged 15 to 65 (grade A), chlamydia and gonorrhea screening for sexually active women 24 or younger and older women at increased risk (grade B), syphilis screening for people at increased risk (grade A), and hepatitis C screening for adults 18 to 79 (grade B). CDC recommends at least annual chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis and HIV screening for sexually active men who have sex with men, and chlamydia, gonorrhea and syphilis screening every 3 to 6 months for those at increased risk. Trichomoniasis and hepatitis B screening follow risk and CDC criteria, and HPV testing belongs to cervical cancer screening. Symptoms call for testing of the affected site with NAAT (nucleic acid amplification testing) and a clinician visit, not a routine screen.

🗂 Key Takeaways

  • The CDC 2021 STI Treatment Guidelines are the main US clinical reference for preventing and treating STIs, and USPSTF issues graded screening recommendations for people without symptoms.[1][2]
  • Routine screening usually centers on HIV, syphilis, chlamydia, gonorrhea. Trichomoniasis, hepatitis B and hepatitis C screening follow CDC criteria for sex, pregnancy and risk.[10]
  • Nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT) is the standard test for chlamydia and gonorrhea and should match the exposure site: urine or vaginal swab, plus rectal and throat swabs where there was receptive anal or oral sex.[11][12]
  • USPSTF recommends HIV screening for everyone aged 15 to 65 (grade A), and chlamydia and gonorrhea screening for all sexually active women 24 or younger and for older women at increased risk (grade B).[3][4] CDC frames this as annual, routine screening for women under 25.[10]
  • Syphilis screening is USPSTF grade A for all pregnant people and for nonpregnant adolescents and adults at increased risk.[5][6] US congenital syphilis cases have risen for 12 years in a row, to nearly 4,000 reported cases in 2024 (provisional data).[7][37]
  • Home-collection STI kits (mail-in or point-of-care) can be a reasonable option for asymptomatic screening, and FDA advises anyone with a positive home result to seek medical care. Symptoms, pregnancy, sexual assault, a possible HIV exposure in the last 72 hours, and prior positive tests still warrant in-person evaluation.[8][21][27]
  • Doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis (doxy-PEP) is a CDC-recommended option for men who have sex with men and transgender women with a bacterial STI in the past 12 months. See the doxy-PEP guide.[9]

What Is a Standard STI Panel?

For people without symptoms, the tests CDC recommends most often are HIV, syphilis, chlamydia, and gonorrhea, with the exact list depending on age, sex, pregnancy and sexual exposures.[10] Syphilis is diagnosed with two blood tests, a nontreponemal and a treponemal test.[13] Chlamydia and gonorrhea are tested with nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT). CDC lists first-catch urine (for men) and vaginal swabs (for women) as optimal urogenital specimens, and infections of the rectum or throat are diagnosed by testing at the site of exposure.[11][12]

What is not on every routine panel matters as much as what is. CDC suggests considering trichomoniasis screening for women receiving care in high-prevalence settings (such as STI clinics and correctional facilities) and for women at increased risk.[10] HPV tests are used for cervical cancer screening in women 30 and older, and CDC does not recommend them to screen men.[35] USPSTF recommends against routine herpes blood tests in people without symptoms (grade D), and CDC notes that the most widely used HSV-2 antibody test is often falsely positive at low index values.[32][17] Hepatitis C screening is now for all adults: USPSTF recommends it for adults 18 to 79 (grade B), and CDC recommends it at least once for all adults 18 and older.[31][28] For hepatitis B, USPSTF recommends screening adolescents and adults at increased risk (grade B), while CDC recommends screening all adults at least once with a triple panel blood test.[30][29]

Ask specifically for a "4-panel" or "5-panel" STI screen when you book a visit, and make sure the requisition covers the anatomic sites where exposure actually occurred. A urogenital-only NAAT will miss rectal and pharyngeal chlamydia and gonorrhea. CDC recommends rectal and pharyngeal testing for men who have sex with men based on receptive anal or oral sex in the past year, and testing at the exposure site for anyone with receptive anal or oral sex.[19][11]

Who Should Test, and When

Routine screening (asymptomatic)

USPSTF recommends HIV screening for adolescents and adults aged 15 to 65, and for younger adolescents and older adults at increased risk (grade A).[3] It recommends chlamydia and gonorrhea screening for all sexually active women 24 or younger and for women 25 and older at increased risk (grade B), and found the evidence insufficient to assess screening in men.[4] CDC applies the same criteria for women and advises retesting about 3 months after treatment.[10] For sexually active men who have sex with men, CDC recommends chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis and HIV screening at least annually, and chlamydia, gonorrhea and syphilis screening every 3 to 6 months for those at increased risk, such as men taking HIV PrEP, men with HIV, or men who have (or whose partners have) multiple partners; CDC suggests considering HIV testing that often as well.[10][19] For transgender and gender diverse people, CDC adapts screening to anatomy and sexual exposures.[10]

If you have symptoms

Symptoms change the algorithm. Unusual discharge, dysuria, pelvic or testicular pain, painful bowel movements, a genital or oral sore, or a new rash all warrant in-person clinical evaluation rather than a home test kit. CDC recommends NAAT testing for chlamydia and gonorrhea in men with suspected urethritis, and treatment that covers both infections at the visit in some situations, for example when same-day diagnostic tools are not available, or for women with cervicitis at increased risk when follow-up cannot be ensured.[20]

After a known or possible exposure

Testing timing matters because different tests have different window periods (see the window-period section below). If a possible HIV exposure happened within the last 72 hours, do not wait for a screening test result: CDC says PEP must be started within 72 hours and advises talking right away to a health care provider, an emergency room doctor, or an urgent care provider.[27] If the exposure was more than 72 hours ago, ask a clinician when to test for each infection.

Pregnancy

CDC recommends testing all pregnant women for HIV and syphilis at the first prenatal visit, and testing for hepatitis B surface antigen at the first prenatal visit of each pregnancy.[10] USPSTF recommends early, universal syphilis screening in pregnancy, and screening at the first available opportunity if it was missed early (grade A).[5] CDC also recommends chlamydia and gonorrhea screening for all pregnant women under 25 and older pregnant women at increased risk, and repeat syphilis testing at 28 weeks and at delivery for those at increased risk because of where they live or personal risk factors.[10] A 2023 CDC Vital Signs report found that lack of timely testing and adequate treatment during pregnancy contributed to 88% of the 3,761 congenital syphilis cases reported in 2022.[7]

First, HIV window periods depend on the test: CDC says a NAT can usually detect HIV 10 to 33 days after exposure, a lab antigen/antibody test on blood from a vein 18 to 45 days, a finger-stick rapid antigen/antibody test 18 to 90 days, and antibody tests 23 to 90 days.[26] For hepatitis C, RNA is usually detectable 1 to 2 weeks after exposure, while antibodies usually take 8 to 11 weeks after acute infection.[28] Second, most people with a reactive treponemal syphilis test stay reactive for the rest of their lives, even after adequate treatment, so a positive treponemal test in someone with a prior confirmed treated infection needs interpretation, not automatic retreatment.[13] For chlamydia, gonorrhea, trichomoniasis, syphilis and hepatitis B, ask a clinician when to test after a specific exposure, because testing too soon can miss an infection.

Home vs Clinic Testing

Home-collection STI kits fall into two categories. Mail-in kits from several companies ship a collection kit, you self-collect at home, and you mail the specimen to a lab for results. FDA has also authorized a test performed entirely at home: the Visby Medical Women's Sexual Health Test detects chlamydia, gonorrhea, and trichomoniasis from a self-collected vaginal swab, delivers results in approximately 30 minutes without a lab step, and is intended for females.[8]

When home testing is appropriate

Home testing works well for asymptomatic routine screening in low-risk adults who want privacy and convenience, for people who cannot easily reach a clinic, and for repeat testing after a known negative baseline. See the Everlywell STI test panel review and the Visby at-home STI PCR test review for detailed reviews of two of these options.

When you need a clinic instead

Symptoms, pregnancy, a positive home result, sexual assault, an exposure within the past 72 hours (for PEP), a partner with a diagnosed STI, or a history of recurrent infections all warrant in-person clinical care. Home kits do not include a physical exam, may not include rectal or throat swabs, and cannot start treatment. FDA advises anyone with a positive home result to seek medical care, and CDC recommends that sexual assault survivors be examined by an experienced clinician.[8][21]

What Has Changed Since the Last CDC Guideline Cycle

Three shifts are worth knowing about. First, congenital syphilis has surged. CDC reported that US congenital syphilis cases increased 755% from 2012 to 2021, and that lack of timely testing and adequate treatment during pregnancy contributed to 88% of 2022 cases.[7] Provisional CDC surveillance data show congenital syphilis increased for the 12th year in a row, with nearly 4,000 reported cases in 2024.[37] In 2025, USPSTF reaffirmed early, universal syphilis screening during pregnancy (grade A).[5]

Second, doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis (doxy-PEP) is now a CDC-recommended option. CDC's 2024 guidelines report that in three large randomized trials, 200 mg of doxycycline taken within 72 hours after sex reduced syphilis and chlamydia infections by more than 70% and gonorrhea by approximately 50%, and CDC recommends offering it to men who have sex with men and transgender women with a bacterial STI diagnosed in the past 12 months. CDC gives no recommendation for cisgender women because evidence is insufficient; the only trial in cisgender women found no significant reduction in bacterial STIs. Full analysis in the doxy-PEP guide.[9]

Third, fully at-home testing has arrived. In March 2025, FDA authorized the first diagnostic test for chlamydia, gonorrhea and trichomoniasis that can be purchased without a prescription and performed entirely at home, with results in approximately 30 minutes. It is intended for females and uses a self-collected vaginal swab.[8]

After a Positive Test

A positive STI result is a starting point for four actions: confirm if needed, treat per CDC 2021, notify partners, and rescreen at the correct interval.

Confirmation

Ask a clinician whether a home chlamydia or gonorrhea result needs to be repeated before treatment; FDA advises anyone with a positive home result to seek medical care.[8] A presumptive diagnosis of syphilis requires two blood tests, a nontreponemal and a treponemal test.[13] A preliminary positive HIV screening test must be followed by supplemental testing to establish the diagnosis, and CDC advises anyone with a positive HIV self-test to seek laboratory testing to confirm the result.[18][26]

Treatment (what CDC recommends, and where to get it)

Treatment is pathogen-specific and only chlamydia fits cleanly into an oral-only, no-exam telehealth workflow. The other pathogens require an intramuscular injection, an exam, or specialty follow-up that a general telehealth visit cannot substitute for. What CDC recommends, per pathogen:

  • Chlamydia: doxycycline 100 mg by mouth twice daily for 7 days. Azithromycin 1 g as a single dose is an alternative, and it is the recommended regimen in pregnancy. Oral, no exam required, telehealth-appropriate. See the chlamydia treatment guide.[11]
  • Gonorrhea: a single 500 mg intramuscular ceftriaxone injection (1 g for people weighing 150 kg or more). Because first-line treatment is an IM injection, an in-person visit at a health department STI clinic, primary care office, or urgent care is usually needed. If ceftriaxone is not available, CDC lists cefixime 800 mg by mouth as a single dose as the alternative.[12]
  • Trichomoniasis: metronidazole by mouth: 500 mg twice daily for 7 days for women, or 2 g as a single dose for men. CDC recommends treating all current sex partners and retesting sexually active women about 3 months after treatment.[16] Best paired with a clinical exam to rule out concurrent BV or PID, so handled at a health department or primary care visit rather than general telehealth. See the trichomoniasis guide.
  • Syphilis: staging drives treatment. Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM as a single dose for primary, secondary, or early latent syphilis; for late latent syphilis, 7.2 million units total, given as 3 doses of 2.4 million units IM at 1-week intervals. IM injection required, and staging usually requires clinical evaluation.[14][15]
  • Genital herpes: oral acyclovir, valacyclovir, or famciclovir; CDC says all patients with a first episode of genital herpes should receive antiviral therapy. Initial episodes typically warrant an exam; suppressive therapy is usually managed by primary care. See the genital herpes guide.[17]
  • HIV: antiretroviral therapy, which CDC says should be started as soon as possible for all persons with HIV, typically coordinated with a Ryan White clinic, ID specialist, or PrEP/PEP clinic.[18]

Partner notification and expedited partner therapy

CDC recommends that sex partners from the 60 days before the patient's symptoms or diagnosis be referred for evaluation, testing and presumptive treatment.[11] Expedited partner therapy (EPT), in which the patient delivers medicine or a prescription to a sex partner without a separate clinical assessment of the partner, is permissible in most US states according to CDC's legal-status review.[36] For gonorrhea, CDC recommends cefixime 800 mg by mouth as a single dose for partners treated through EPT.[12] Because state EPT laws vary, EPT is usually coordinated through the treating clinic or the state health department rather than a general telehealth visit. CDC suggests considering EPT, as permitted by law, when health department partner management is impractical or unavailable.[11]

Retest

CDC recommends retesting about 3 months after treatment for chlamydia and gonorrhea, and for trichomoniasis in sexually active women.[11][12][16] Retesting catches repeat infections early; in women, chlamydia and gonorrhea are usually asymptomatic and may lead to pelvic inflammatory disease.[4] HIV and syphilis follow-up follow the CDC HIV testing algorithm and syphilis staging respectively.

Cost, Insurance, and Confidentiality

Under the Affordable Care Act, Marketplace plans and many other plans must cover a list of preventive services, generally at no cost to you when provided by an in-network provider. The list includes HIV screening for everyone 15 to 65 and other ages at increased risk, hepatitis B screening for people at high risk, hepatitis C screening for adults 18 to 79, syphilis screening for adults at higher risk, and, for women, chlamydia screening for younger women and other women at higher risk and gonorrhea screening for women at higher risk.[38][39] Public options include state and local health departments, Planned Parenthood, and federally qualified health centers; CDC's GetTested locator lists HIV, STI and hepatitis testing sites and notes that many clinics provide confidential and free or low-cost testing.[40]

Cash prices change often, so check current prices before you buy. As of November 15, 2026, Visby's own store listed its women's at-home STI test kit at $179.99, shown at $149.99 with a limited-time $30 discount.[41] Confidentiality: CDC notes that every US state and the District of Columbia explicitly allows minors to consent to their own STI services, but an explanation-of-benefits statement or medical bill received by a parent might disclose the services; ask about confidential billing options if that matters.[25]

Red Flags: When to Skip the Home Kit

  • Fever, pelvic pain, or lower abdominal pain (possible pelvic inflammatory disease, needs in-person evaluation)[22]
  • Testicular pain or swelling (possible epididymitis; sudden, severe pain can mean testicular torsion, a surgical emergency, so go to an emergency department right away)[23]
  • Rectal pain, discharge, or painful or bloody bowel movements after receptive anal exposure (possible proctitis)[24]
  • New genital or oral sore, ulcer, or rash
  • Exposure within the last 72 hours (HIV PEP must be started within 72 hours)[27]
  • Pregnancy or possible pregnancy[10]
  • Sexual assault (needs forensic and medical care in the ER or a SANE program)[21]
  • A previously known positive test that has never been treated

Getting STI Testing Through Telehealth

Telehealth fits STI testing at two points in the workflow. First, a virtual visit can order a lab requisition for asymptomatic routine screening at a nearby Quest or LabCorp draw site. Second, for a confirmed chlamydia positive, telehealth can review the result, prescribe first-line doxycycline (or azithromycin where indicated), and set up a 3-month retest.

Scope note: TeleDirectMD treats adults 18 and older by video for chlamydia, trichomoniasis, genital herpes and genital warts, and offers doxy-PEP. Gonorrhea (first-line treatment is an intramuscular ceftriaxone injection), syphilis (benzathine penicillin injection) and HIV are outside what we treat and need in-person care or a specialty referral. Positive results for those are best handled by a local health department STI clinic, a primary care office that can give injections, an infectious disease clinic, or (for HIV) a Ryan White or PrEP clinic.

Telehealth of any kind, ours included, cannot substitute for in-person care when an IM injection is needed (gonorrhea ceftriaxone, benzathine penicillin for syphilis), when a pelvic or scrotal exam is essential, when the presentation could plausibly be non-STI (endometritis, ovarian pathology, prostatitis), or when acute complications like PID or epididymitis are on the differential.

Frequently Asked Questions

USPSTF recommends HIV screening for everyone aged 15 to 65 (grade A), chlamydia and gonorrhea screening for all sexually active women 24 or younger and for older women at increased risk (grade B), syphilis screening for anyone at increased risk (grade A), and hepatitis C screening for adults 18 to 79 (grade B). CDC recommends at least annual chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis and HIV screening for sexually active men who have sex with men, and chlamydia, gonorrhea and syphilis screening every 3 to 6 months for those at increased risk. See a clinician sooner if you develop symptoms.[3][4][6][31][10]

Most routine screening centers on HIV, syphilis, chlamydia, and gonorrhea. Trichomoniasis screening is considered for women in high-prevalence settings or at increased risk. CDC recommends hepatitis C screening for all adults 18 and older and hepatitis B screening for all adults at least once. HPV testing is part of cervical cancer screening, not routine STI screening. USPSTF recommends against routine herpes blood tests in people without symptoms.[10][28][29][35][32]

Window periods vary by test. CDC says an HIV nucleic acid test (NAT) can usually detect HIV 10 to 33 days after exposure, a lab antigen/antibody test on blood from a vein 18 to 45 days, a finger-stick rapid antigen/antibody test 18 to 90 days, and antibody tests 23 to 90 days. Hepatitis C RNA is usually detectable 1 to 2 weeks after exposure, while antibodies usually take 8 to 11 weeks. For chlamydia, gonorrhea, trichomoniasis and syphilis, ask a clinician when to test after a specific exposure. If a possible HIV exposure happened within the past 72 hours, do not wait: PEP must be started within 72 hours, so talk right away to a health care provider, an emergency room doctor, or an urgent care provider.[26][28][27]

In FDA's review, the Visby Medical Women's Sexual Health Test correctly identified 98.8% of negative and 97.2% of positive chlamydia samples, 99.1% of negative and 100% of positive gonorrhea samples, and 98.5% of negative and 97.8% of positive trichomoniasis samples. FDA notes that false negative results can delay treatment, that people with positive results should seek medical care, and that anyone with symptoms or concerns after a negative result should contact a health care provider for more testing. The practical limits remain: home kits may not include rectal or throat swabs, cannot include a physical exam, cannot start same-day treatment for gonorrhea or syphilis (whose first-line treatments are IM injections), and cannot substitute for in-person care in pregnancy, symptomatic infection, or after sexual assault. See the Everlywell review and Visby review for details.[8]

USPSTF recommends cervical cancer screening every 3 years with a Pap test (cervical cytology) alone for women aged 21 to 29. For women 30 to 65, it recommends a Pap test every 3 years, HPV testing alone every 5 years, or Pap plus HPV co-testing every 5 years (all grade A). This is separate from the STI screening panel discussed here, and it is not routinely part of an STI-only visit.[33]

Not routinely. USPSTF recommends against routine herpes blood tests in adolescents and adults without symptoms, including pregnant people (grade D), and CDC notes that the most widely used HSV-2 antibody test is often falsely positive at low index values. For an active sore, CDC calls HSV NAAT of the lesion the most sensitive test. Type-specific blood tests can help when you have recurrent or atypical genital symptoms with a negative PCR or culture, a clinical diagnosis without lab confirmation, or a partner with genital herpes, and CDC notes they can be useful in pregnancy and for people with HIV.[32][17]

Not routinely. HPV tests are used for cervical cancer screening in women 30 and older, and CDC does not recommend HPV tests to screen men, adolescents, or women under 30. CDC recommends routine HPV vaccination at age 11 or 12 (it can start at 9), vaccination for everyone through age 26 who was not adequately vaccinated, and shared decision-making with a clinician for some adults 27 to 45.[35][34] If your last exposure was to a partner with a new HPV diagnosis, no immediate test changes management for either of you.

Chlamydia usually can. First-line treatment is oral doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days, which fits a telehealth visit without an exam. First-line gonorrhea treatment is an intramuscular ceftriaxone injection and syphilis requires benzathine penicillin G injections, so both usually need an in-person visit. Trichomoniasis is treated with oral metronidazole and can often be managed by video, with partner treatment. HIV needs specialty or in-person follow-up. TeleDirectMD treats adults by video for chlamydia, trichomoniasis, genital herpes and genital warts; gonorrhea, syphilis and HIV are outside our scope and require in-person care or a specialty referral.[11][12][14]

Free or low-cost options include your state or local health department STI clinic, Planned Parenthood, and federally qualified health centers, and CDC's GetTested locator notes that many clinics provide confidential and free or low-cost testing. Cash prices for home kits, lab self-orders and telehealth visits vary, so check current prices before you buy; as of November 15, 2026, Visby's own store listed its women's at-home kit at $179.99, shown at $149.99 with a limited-time $30 discount. Under the Affordable Care Act, Marketplace plans and many other plans must cover recommended preventive screenings, generally at no cost when you use an in-network provider.[40][41][38][39]

CDC recommends that providers offer doxy-PEP (200 mg doxycycline within 72 hours after oral, vaginal, or anal sex, no more than 200 mg every 24 hours) to men who have sex with men and transgender women who have had a bacterial STI in the past 12 months. CDC gives no recommendation for cisgender women, cisgender heterosexual men, transgender men, or other queer and nonbinary people because the evidence is insufficient; the only trial in cisgender women found no significant reduction in bacterial STIs. The doxy-PEP guide covers eligibility, dosing, and resistance concerns.[9]
