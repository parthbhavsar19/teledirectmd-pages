Cold Sores (HSV-1)

A physician's evidence-based guide to what causes cold sores, the five stages of an outbreak, oral versus topical antivirals, the timing window that matters most, and how to reduce recurrence and spread in 2026.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: September 8, 2026 · Fully rewritten Sep 2026
22 min read

What is the best treatment for a cold sore (HSV-1) in 2026?

Cold sores, also called fever blisters, are caused by herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1), which the World Health Organization estimates infected 64% of people under age 50 worldwide in 2020. The virus lives inside nerve cells and periodically reactivates to cause outbreaks on or around the lips. For an episodic outbreak, oral antivirals are the main option, and the FDA labels for valacyclovir (2 grams twice, 12 hours apart, for one day) and famciclovir (1,500 mg as a single dose) both call for starting at the earliest symptom, such as tingling. In placebo-controlled trials by Spruance and colleagues, one-day valacyclovir shortened the median episode by 1.0 day, and single-dose famciclovir shortened healing by about 2 days. Topical creams help less: docosanol 10% shortened healing by about 18 hours and penciclovir 1% by 0.7 day in their pivotal trials, and a review by Leung and Barankin rates oral antivirals superior to topical therapy for episodic treatment. Long-term daily oral antivirals reduce recurrences, though a Cochrane review judged the benefit small. A licensed clinician, accessible in person or through telehealth, can evaluate symptoms and prescribe oral antivirals when appropriate.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated September 8, 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • Cold sores are caused by herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1), which lives inside nerve cells and alternates between inactive and active phases. The WHO estimates that 64% of people under age 50 worldwide carry it.[1]
  • An outbreak usually starts with tingling, itching or burning, then blisters that burst and crust over, and it usually clears up on its own within 10 days.[29]
  • Oral antivirals (acyclovir, valacyclovir, famciclovir) are rated superior to topical antivirals for episodic treatment, and the valacyclovir label says to start at the earliest symptom, such as tingling.[6][20]
  • Topical antiviral creams offer modest benefit: in their pivotal trials, docosanol shortened healing by about 18 hours and penciclovir by about 0.7 day.[11][10]
  • A one-day course of valacyclovir (2 grams twice, 12 hours apart) and a single 1,500 mg dose of famciclovir are the FDA-labeled short regimens for cold sores in adults.[20][30]
  • Recurrences can be triggered by illness, sunshine, menstrual periods, stress and lack of sleep. Lip sunscreen prevented cold sores caused by UV lamps in a lab trial, but a trial under natural sunlight did not show the same protection.[29][31][18][12]
  • Frequent or severe recurrences can be reduced with long-term daily oral antiviral therapy, though a Cochrane review judged the benefit small. It is an option worth discussing with a clinician.[13][12]

What Are Cold Sores, and What Causes Them?

A cold sore, or herpes labialis, is a cluster of small, fluid-filled blisters that forms on or around the lip. It is caused by herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1), the type that primarily infects the face.[32] HSV-1 is one of the most widespread human infections: the World Health Organization estimates that 3.8 billion people under age 50, or 64 percent, had HSV-1 infection in 2020, its latest available estimate.[1]

Infection is lifelong. After the first infection, the virus enters a latent (dormant) state in sensory nerve cells, particularly in the trigeminal ganglia, and current treatment does not eliminate it.[15] Periodically, in response to a trigger such as sunshine, illness, or stress, the virus reactivates and can cause another crop of blisters or be shed without any symptoms.[15][29][31]

In the United States, HSV-1 seroprevalence (the share of people with antibodies to the virus) declined for at least two decades, with the steepest declines in younger people.[2] It still rises with age: in 2015 to 2016, about 27 percent of people aged 14 to 19 had HSV-1 antibodies, compared with about 60 percent of those aged 40 to 49.[28] Infection is often latent and without symptoms, so many people never realize they carry it.[2]

It is worth distinguishing cold sores from genital herpes. Both are caused by herpes simplex viruses. Cold sores on the face are usually caused by HSV-1,[32] while a growing share of genital herpes infections are now caused by HSV-1, with oral sexual transmission increasingly common.[19][2] This guide focuses on oral cold sores. See our separate guide on genital herpes treatment for HSV-2 genital infection.

The Five Stages of a Cold Sore Episode

A cold sore does not appear all at once. It typically moves through a recognizable sequence: tingling, blisters, bursting, crusting, and healing.[29]

  1. Tingling (prodrome): A cold sore usually starts with a tingling, itching or burning feeling before any blister appears.[29] This is the most important signal: the valacyclovir label says treatment should start at the earliest symptom, and its benefit when started after a bump, blister or ulcer has formed has not been established.[20]
  2. Blistering: Over the next 48 hours, one or more painful blisters appear.[29] The blister and its fluid are contagious.[31]
  3. Weeping: The blisters burst open.[29]
  4. Crusting (scabbing): The burst blisters crust over into a scab. The sore is still contagious while it heals.[29]
  5. Healing: Cold sores usually clear up on their own within 10 days. See a clinician if a cold sore has not started to heal within 10 days.[29]

Recognizing the prodrome is the most useful skill a patient can develop. Both the valacyclovir and famciclovir labels say to start treatment at the earliest sign or symptom, and in the valacyclovir and famciclovir trials cited here, patients started their medicine themselves at the first symptoms.[20][30][8][9]

How HSV-1 Spreads and Who Is Most at Risk

HSV-1 is mainly transmitted through contact with the virus in sores, saliva, or skin surfaces in or around the mouth, for example through kissing or by sharing items that touched saliva, such as utensils, cups, or lip balm.[1][31][21] It can also be transmitted from oral or skin surfaces that look normal, because the virus can be shed without symptoms.[1][15]

HSV-1 has commonly been acquired in childhood, though this has become less common in the United States as hygiene and living conditions have improved.[2] Swollen, painful gums with sores in the mouth (gingivostomatitis), in you or your child, are a reason to see a doctor.[29]

Certain groups are at higher risk of more severe disease. People with weakened immune systems can have prolonged or severe episodes of oral herpes.[19] For anyone who has eczema (atopic dermatitis), the virus can reach open skin and spread, causing a rare infection called eczema herpeticum that can be life threatening.[27]

Two other important points about transmission. First, HSV-1 can be passed to a partner's genitals during oral sex, so avoid oral sex until a cold sore has completely healed.[29] Second, kissing a baby when you have a cold sore can lead to neonatal herpes, which is very dangerous to newborns, and anyone who is pregnant should talk with their clinician about herpes.[29][1]

Common Triggers for Recurrent Outbreaks

The virus reactivates in response to specific triggers, and identifying your own is one of the most practical forms of prevention. Commonly cited triggers include:

  • Ultraviolet (sun) light: In a controlled trial, UV lamp exposure to the lip produced cold sores in 71 percent of patients after a placebo, and none after sunscreen was applied.[18] A trial under natural sunlight, however, did not show that sunscreen prevented cold sores.[12]
  • Fever and illness: Illness is a recognized trigger for a cold sore, which is also called a fever blister.[29]
  • Emotional or physical stress, and poor sleep: Stress can reactivate the virus, and being constantly sleep deprived triggers cold sores for some people.[31]
  • Menstruation: Being on your period can trigger a cold sore in some people.[29]
  • Local trauma: Dental work, lip fillers, and laser or light procedures can trigger an outbreak, so tell whoever performs the procedure that you get cold sores.[31] A 2021 guideline on HSV-1 and cosmetic interventions notes that trauma can reactivate the virus and sets out prophylaxis plans for patients.[3]

Not every recurrence has an obvious trigger. For people with a sun trigger, the AAD advises applying lip balm with SPF 30 and broad-spectrum protection before going outside.[31]

Oral Antivirals: The Evidence

Three oral antiviral drugs are used for recurrent cold sores: acyclovir, valacyclovir, and famciclovir.[5][6] All three are nucleoside analogues that inhibit the viral DNA polymerase.[15] For episodic treatment, a review rates them superior to topical antiviral therapy and advises starting promptly, ideally during the tingling stage.[6]

Acyclovir

A 2007 expert panel review reported that recurrent cold sores can be treated effectively with oral acyclovir 400 mg three times a day, giving 3 to 5 day courses for the oral regimens it lists.[7] An evidence review found that oral antiviral medication, like antiviral cream, has a small favourable effect on the duration of symptoms if started promptly.[4] Its main drawback is dosing several times a day.

Valacyclovir

Valacyclovir is converted to acyclovir in the body,[20] and it raises acyclovir bioavailability three- to fivefold compared with oral acyclovir.[8] For cold sores, the FDA-labeled regimen is a single day of treatment: 2 grams taken twice, 12 hours apart, started at the earliest symptom.[20] In the first of two randomized, placebo-controlled trials, this one-day course, started at the first symptoms, reduced the median episode duration by 1.0 day (P = 0.001) and significantly shortened time to healing and to the end of pain, with similar results in the second trial.[8] Valacyclovir and famciclovir need less frequent dosing than acyclovir but cost more.[6]

Famciclovir

Famciclovir can be given as a single 1,500 mg dose, the FDA-labeled regimen for recurrent cold sores in adults, started at the first sign or symptom.[30] In a randomized trial in which patients took it within 1 hour of the first tingling or other early symptoms, a single dose cut median healing time to 4.4 days versus 6.2 days for placebo, about 2 days faster.[9]

The short-course advantage

Short regimens are easy to complete. In the valacyclovir trials, a two-day course shortened the median episode by 0.5 day versus 1.0 day for the one-day course, and in the famciclovir trial a single dose and a single-day regimen did not differ significantly.[8][9] A 2025 systematic review notes that short-course, high-dose regimens improve adherence while controlling symptoms.[15] Because the labels call for starting at the earliest symptom, the practical step is to have a plan before the next tingle.[20][30]

Topical Treatments: What the Evidence Honestly Shows

It is common to reach for an over-the-counter cream first, and there is a place for topicals, but their benefit is modest and it is important to be honest about what they can and cannot do. Prescription and over-the-counter topical antivirals for cold sores include:

  • Docosanol 10% cream (Abreva), sold over the counter and applied 5 times a day starting at the first tingle.[33] In its pivotal trials it shortened median healing time by about 18 hours, to 4.1 days.[11]
  • Penciclovir 1% cream (Denavir), which in its pivotal trial, applied every 2 hours while awake for 4 days, healed classic lesions 0.7 day faster than placebo (median 4.8 versus 5.5 days) and also shortened pain and virus shedding.[10]
  • Acyclovir 5% cream, a topical form of the oral drug, which may reduce the duration of lesions if applied early.[7]

Across reviews, topical antivirals reduce lesion duration and symptoms modestly when applied early, but they generally do not reduce how often cold sores recur.[15][12] For episodic treatment, a review rates oral antivirals as superior to topical therapy.[6] A network meta-analysis by Koe and colleagues found several agents effective for healing, with oral valacyclovir combined with topical clobetasol ranked best for time to heal.[14]

One combination product, acyclovir 5% with 1% hydrocortisone cream (ME-609), adds an anti-inflammatory to the antiviral. In a randomized trial, 42 percent of patients using it did not develop an ulcerative lesion, compared with 35 percent with acyclovir cream alone and 26 percent with placebo,[17] and a later analysis reported a smaller cumulative lesion area than with acyclovir alone.[16] A meta-analysis of four trials found that adding a topical corticosteroid to antiviral therapy lowered the rate of ulcerative lesions compared with antiviral therapy alone, although healing time did not differ.[25]

The practical bottom line: if you only have a topical on hand, applying it early can trim some time off healing.[15] If your goal is the shortest episode, an oral antiviral started at the first symptom is the option a review rates as superior.[6]

The Timing Window: Why It Matters Most

If there is one clinical lesson to take from this guide, it is that antiviral treatment works best when started early. Viral replication in a recurrent cold sore lasts only a brief period, which is why short treatment courses make sense.[9] A review advises starting episodic treatment promptly, ideally during the tingling stage and no later than 48 hours after lesions appear, for optimal results.[6]

The drug labels reflect this. The valacyclovir label says to start at the earliest symptom, such as tingling, itching, or burning, and states that efficacy when treatment starts after a bump, blister, or ulcer has formed has not been established.[20] The famciclovir label likewise says to start at the first sign or symptom.[30] The practical consequence is that "wait and see" is usually the wrong strategy for someone who wants a short, mild course.

This timing is why it helps to have a plan before the next outbreak. In the trials, patients kept the medicine on hand and started it themselves at the first symptoms.[8][9] A typical recurrent cold sore in an otherwise healthy adult can often be assessed by video, and a clinician can decide whether an oral antiviral is appropriate. Eye symptoms, a spreading rash over eczema, or signs of severe illness need in-person or emergency care.

Suppressive Therapy: When Daily Medication Makes Sense

For a minority of people, cold sores are frequent, painful, or both, and treating each episode reactively is not enough. Long-term daily oral antiviral therapy can prevent some outbreaks and is the preventive option with the most trial support, though a Cochrane review judged the clinical benefit small.[13][12]

Who should consider suppressive therapy? A review advises considering chronic suppressive therapy for people with severe or frequent recurrences, defined there as six or more episodes a year.[6] Antiviral prophylaxis can also be considered for people with known triggers, such as dental or cosmetic procedures.[15][3] A meta-analysis of preventive trials found support for oral acyclovir and valacyclovir, although only 1 of its 10 included studies had a low risk of bias, and a Cochrane review found that long-term use reduced recurrences.[13][12]

Regimens shown to be effective in preventive trials include oral acyclovir 800 to 1,600 mg daily and valacyclovir 500 mg daily for 4 months.[13] In one trial summarized by the Cochrane review, long-term acyclovir reduced clinical recurrences from 1.80 to 0.85 episodes per participant over 4 months.[12] Dose and duration are set by your clinician. Suppression does not cure the infection.[1]

Suppressive therapy is a conversation, not a default. The AAD notes that some people who get frequent cold sores take a low dose of antiviral medication every day.[21] For people with occasional outbreaks, prompt treatment at the first symptom is the alternative.[4]

Prevention and Reducing Transmission

Preventing your own outbreaks

Sun protection is a common preventive habit, but the evidence is mixed. Sunscreen on the lips prevented cold sores caused by UV lamps in a controlled trial, while a trial under natural sunlight did not show prevention.[18][12] The AAD still advises lip balm with SPF 30 or higher and broad-spectrum protection, which may help prevent new cold sores.[21] Managing stress and getting enough sleep may also help, since both can trigger outbreaks in some people.[31]

Preventing spread to others

Because HSV-1 is so common and can be shed without symptoms, no one can reduce transmission risk to zero. But during any active outbreak you should:

  • Avoid kissing anyone while you have a cold sore, and do not kiss a baby, because a cold sore can lead to neonatal herpes, which is very dangerous to newborns.[29]
  • Avoid sharing items that touch the mouth, such as utensils, dishes, and cups, and throw away lip balm and lipstick you used while you had the sore.[1][31][21]
  • Avoid oral sex until the cold sore has completely healed, since you could give your partner genital herpes.[29]
  • Do not touch the sore except to apply medicine, wash your hands before and after if you do, dab cream on rather than rubbing it in, and avoid touching your eyes.[29][32]

If you have a cold sore, take particular care around newborns, people with eczema, and people with weakened immune systems, who can develop serious herpes infections.[29][27][19]

What's Changed in 2024 to 2026

There has been no fundamental change in the treatment of cold sores in the last few years, but the evidence base has grown in ways that reinforce current practice. Highlights include:

  • Comparative evidence on oral and topical options. A network meta-analysis published in 2022 found several antiviral agents effective in shortening healing time, with oral valacyclovir plus topical clobetasol ranked best, but it could not show that any agent was superior for preventing recurrences.[14] A 2025 systematic review concluded that topical agents mainly shorten lesions when applied early and generally do not affect how often cold sores recur, while oral antivirals address both healing and recurrence prevention.[15]
  • Standardized outcome measures. A meta-analysis of orolabial and genital herpes trials, published in December 2025, found that trials over the last 30 years used varied, nonstandardized endpoints and called for a reassessment of endpoints.[26]
  • Guidance on cosmetic procedures. A 2021 guideline for clinicians who perform nonsurgical cosmetic procedures sets out patient screening and prophylaxis plans to reduce the risk of HSV-1 reactivation after a procedure.[3]

None of this changes the core message: the FDA labels for valacyclovir and famciclovir both call for starting treatment at the earliest sign or symptom.[20][30]

What Doesn't Work, and What Is Unproven

The internet is full of cold sore remedies, most with little or no rigorous support. Being honest about what does not work saves time, money, and prolonged pain.

  • Picking, popping or touching the sore: Leave it alone. NHS advice is not to touch a cold sore except to apply antiviral cream, and to wash your hands before and after if you do.[29]
  • Lysine supplements: Despite widespread use, a Cochrane review of prevention trials found no evidence that lysine prevents cold sores.[12]
  • Light and laser therapy: A Cochrane review found very few data on low-level laser therapy for prevention.[12] In one randomized study, laser phototherapy reduced lesion size and swelling, but the drop in monthly recurrences was not statistically significant,[23] and a systematic review of combined photodynamic therapy and photobiomodulation found only case reports, not clinical trials.[24] These are not a standard treatment.

Apply the same skepticism to any product that promises a cure. Herpes simplex infection is treatable but not curable,[1] and current antivirals reduce symptoms without eliminating the latent virus.[15]

Complications and Red Flags

Cold sores are usually benign, but HSV-1 can occasionally cause serious problems, and knowing the warning signs matters.

Eye involvement (herpes keratitis)

If HSV infects the eye (herpes keratitis), it can scar the cornea and cause vision loss, and sometimes even blindness. Symptoms include eye pain, redness, blurred vision, tearing, and sensitivity to light. A cold sore near the eye with any of these symptoms should prompt immediate evaluation by an eye doctor.[32]

Eczema herpeticum

In people with eczema, HSV can reach open skin and spread, causing eczema herpeticum, a rare infection that can be life threatening. Anyone who might have eczema herpeticum needs immediate medical care.[27]

Immunocompromised patients

People with weakened immune systems can have prolonged or severe episodes of oral herpes, and severe HSV disease can need hospital treatment with intravenous acyclovir.[19] They should see a clinician about a cold sore and may be referred to hospital for advice or treatment.[29]

Intraoral and widespread infection

Occasionally HSV-1 causes sores inside the mouth (see cold sores inside the mouth). Recurrent herpes sores inside the mouth can look atypical and resemble other conditions, and recognizing them can prevent unnecessary tests and treatments.[22] Swollen, painful gums with sores in the mouth (gingivostomatitis) are a reason to see a doctor.[29]

Seek care promptly for a cold sore that has not started to heal within 10 days, is very large or painful, or occurs with a weakened immune system,[29] and get immediate care for eye symptoms.[32]
