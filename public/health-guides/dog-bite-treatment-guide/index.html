Dog Bite Treatment Guide: Wound Care, Infection Risk & What to Do Next

What to do in the first minutes, which wounds need antibiotics, how to assess rabies risk, and when to go straight to the ER, explained clinically.

Reviewed by TeleDirectMD Medical Team
Last reviewed: Mar 2026 · Updated: Jul 2026
15 min read

How do you treat a dog bite wound and prevent infection?

Infected dog bites usually grow several kinds of bacteria, and Pasteurella species are the most frequent isolates, according to a multicenter emergency department study. Per the CDC, care starts with immediate and thorough cleansing of the wound with soap and water. The 2014 IDSA skin and soft tissue infection guideline recommends 3 to 5 days of early antibiotics for higher-risk patients and wounds, such as people who are immunocompromised or have no spleen and moderate to severe injuries, especially to the hand or face, with amoxicillin-clavulanate as the usual choice. Under the CDC rabies algorithm, rabies post-exposure prophylaxis is not indicated if the dog is healthy and stays healthy through a 10-day observation. Because bites are dirty wounds, the CDC recommends a tetanus vaccine if your last dose was 5 or more years ago. Go to the ER for deep facial lacerations or suspected tendon involvement. Most uncomplicated cases are appropriate for telehealth evaluation; complicated or red-flag presentations should be seen in person.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated July 14, 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • Wash the wound right away and thoroughly with soap and water. The CDC says all rabies post-exposure care begins with this step.[1]
  • Infected dog bites usually grow several kinds of bacteria. Pasteurella species are the most frequent, and Pasteurella canis is the most common single isolate.[2]
  • Not all dog bites need antibiotics. The IDSA recommends 3 to 5 days of early antibiotics for higher-risk patients and wounds, such as people who are immunocompromised or have no spleen, and moderate to severe injuries, especially to the hand or face.[3] Amoxicillin-clavulanate is the usual first choice.[3][4]
  • Rabies risk is low for a healthy domestic dog that can be watched. Under the CDC rabies algorithm, if the dog is still healthy after a 10-day observation, post-exposure prophylaxis is not indicated.[5]
  • Bites count as dirty wounds. The CDC recommends a tetanus vaccine if your last dose was 5 or more years ago.[6]
  • People without a spleen face a much higher risk of death from Capnocytophaga infection after a dog bite.[7]

Dog bites are common, and most are minor. But the ones that get infected, or the ones that involve the wrong bacteria in the wrong patient, can turn into something serious fast. What you do in the first hour matters as much as anything a clinician will do later.

In clinical experience treating bite wounds, the patients who do worst are almost never the ones with the biggest lacerations. They're the ones who waited two days to come in, or the ones who are on immunosuppressive medications, or the ones who didn't wash the wound because they thought it "looked clean." A puncture wound that looks innocent on the surface can seed bacteria deep into a tendon sheath.

This guide covers everything a patient needs to know: what to do immediately, how doctors think about infection risk, which wounds need antibiotics, how to assess rabies and tetanus status, and how to identify warning signs before a bite wound becomes a medical emergency.

Immediate First Aid: The First 5 Minutes

Before you call anyone, before you take a photo for insurance purposes, before you do anything else, wash the wound. This is not optional and it is not a small step.

Clean the bite right away and thoroughly with soap and running water. In animal studies, thorough wound cleansing alone, without any vaccine, markedly reduced the likelihood of developing rabies.[1] Washing also physically dilutes and removes bacteria from the wound surface.

Step-by-Step Immediate Care

  1. Control bleeding first if needed. Apply firm, direct pressure with a clean cloth. Do not use a tourniquet unless bleeding is truly uncontrollable and the wound is on a limb.
  2. Wash the wound thoroughly with soap and running water. Do not scrub aggressively with a brush, which can drive bacteria into deeper tissue layers.
  3. Apply antiseptic such as povidone-iodine solution or chlorhexidine, if available. Alcohol works in a pinch but stings.
  4. Cover with a clean bandage loosely. Do not close a puncture wound tightly, and do not apply antibiotic ointment without professional guidance on deep punctures.
  5. Seek medical evaluation. The CDC advises calling your healthcare provider immediately after being bitten by a dog or cat.[8] Even a wound that appears minor deserves clinical assessment of infection risk, tetanus status, and rabies exposure.
Note on the Attacking Animal

Try to identify the dog and its owner if it is safe to do so. Get the dog's vaccination history if possible. Do not attempt to restrain or capture a stray dog on your own. The vaccination status and whereabouts of the dog are critical information for any rabies risk assessment, so document everything you can before leaving the scene.

What's in a Dog's Mouth: The Microbiology of Dog Bites

Infected dog bites are rarely single-organism infections. In a prospective study of infected bite wounds at 18 emergency departments, cultures grew a median of 5 bacterial isolates, and both aerobes and anaerobes were isolated from 56 percent of wounds.[2] This mix is one reason broad coverage, not a narrow-spectrum antibiotic, is used when antibiotics are needed.

Pasteurella canis and Pasteurella multocida

In that study, Pasteurella species were the most frequent isolates from infected dog bites (50 percent), and Pasteurella canis was the most common isolate of dog bites, while P. multocida subspecies were the most common isolates of cat bites.[2] Patients who come in saying "it got red fast" often have a Pasteurella infection.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a rare but serious cause of infection after dog bites. The CDC notes that the disease can progress rapidly from a mild, localized infection to sepsis and death, and that asplenic patients have a 30 to 60 times greater risk of death from Capnocytophaga infections.[7] It is not limited to people with weak immune systems: a review of reported meningitis cases found that most occurred in previously healthy, immunocompetent persons.[9]

If you have no spleen, whether from surgical removal, sickle cell disease, or any other cause, a dog bite is not a minor event. Any fever after a bite requires urgent evaluation. The IDSA lists asplenia among the reasons for early (preemptive) antibiotic therapy after a bite.[3]

Other Organisms

Beyond Pasteurella, other common aerobes in infected dog and cat bites included streptococci, staphylococci, Moraxella, and Neisseria, and common anaerobes included Fusobacterium, Bacteroides, Porphyromonas, and Prevotella.[2] This polymicrobial profile is why cultures from bite wound infections often grow several different organisms.

Infection Rate and High-Risk Wounds

Some bites carry a higher risk of infection than others. A review in American Family Physician names cat bites, puncture wounds, wounds to the hand, and bites in people who are immunosuppressed as examples of high infection risk.[10]

Not all dog bites carry equal risk. Location, depth, mechanism, and patient immune status together determine whether a wound will become infected. This table summarizes the risk profile:

Wound Characteristic Infection Risk Clinical Notes
Hand bites High Dense tendon sheath anatomy limits immune response; small space allows infection to spread rapidly
Deep puncture wounds High Surface seals over, trapping bacteria; irrigation reaches poorly
Crush injuries High Devitalized tissue creates an ideal anaerobic environment
Face bites Low–moderate Excellent vascular supply reduces infection risk; primary closure generally safe
Trunk/extremity lacerations Low–moderate Lowest risk overall; good blood supply, irrigable
Immunocompromised patient (any location) High Impaired host defense elevates risk for all wound types
Wounds presenting >8 hours after bite Elevated Delayed care allows bacterial proliferation to advance before treatment

The recommendation for patients: the wound is not the only variable. A shallow bite on the forearm of a healthy person is a different clinical situation than the same wound on a patient taking methotrexate. Ask your clinician specifically about your individual risk category rather than applying general statistics to your situation.

Wound Irrigation and Debridement: What Happens at the Clinic

When you arrive at urgent care, an ER, or a telehealth triage visit, wound management is the first clinical priority, ahead of any decision about antibiotics or closure.

Copious Irrigation

At the clinic, wound irrigation goes well beyond the home faucet step. Bite wounds should be cleaned and copiously irrigated with normal saline using a 20-mL or larger syringe, or a 20-gauge catheter attached to the syringe, and explored for tendon or bone involvement and foreign bodies.[10]

The same review lists water, normal saline, or dilute povidone-iodine solution as options for copious irrigation.[10]

Debridement

CDC wound-care guidance is to clean all wounds, remove dirt or foreign material, and remove or debride necrotic material.[6] Local debridement and thorough cleaning of the wound are part of standard dog bite care.[4] What remains should be clean, well-vascularized tissue capable of supporting healing.

Wound Closure: When to Stitch, When to Leave Open

Wound closure after a dog bite is one of the more debated topics in emergency medicine, and the answer is genuinely location-dependent. There is no universal rule.

Facial Wounds: Generally Close

The IDSA does not recommend primary closure of bite wounds, with the exception of wounds to the face, which should be managed with copious irrigation and cautious debridement.[3] The face has an excellent blood supply, and cosmetic outcome matters for long-term wellbeing.

A 2025 single-center retrospective study of 212 patients with facial dog bites in China found no significant difference in surgical site infection between immediate closure (12.5%) and delayed closure (13.0%) after standardized debridement.[11] Immediate closure was associated with faster epithelialization and better scar scores at 3 months, and wound area was the only independent predictor of infection.[11]

Hand Wounds: Generally Leave Open

Hand wounds are a different story. The anatomy of the hand, with its layered tendon sheaths, tight compartments, and limited soft tissue volume, creates conditions where infection spreads readily. Wounds at high risk of infection should be left open, and healing by secondary intention should be considered for higher-risk wounds such as those on the hand.[10]

Wounds over metacarpophalangeal joints (the knuckles) deserve special attention. What looks like a simple scrape can be a joint penetration wound, particularly from a human tooth, but also possible with a dog's canine tooth on a small child's hand. Joint penetration requires surgical exploration and washout, not outpatient wound care.

Deep Puncture Wounds: Leave Open

Deep puncture wounds anywhere on the body should not be closed. Closing a puncture seals bacteria in, creates anaerobic conditions, and prevents drainage. These wounds are managed open with close follow-up.

A Note on the Evidence

High-quality trials on bite wound care are few. A 2025 systematic review found only five randomized trials, including trials of suturing versus not suturing; pooling four of them showed no significant difference in infection rates, and the authors concluded that the evidence on infection prevention remains inconclusive.[12] Location, wound size, and irrigation quality guide the decision.

Antibiotic Prophylaxis: Who Needs It and What to Take

Not every dog bite needs antibiotics. Routine prophylaxis for all bites contributes to unnecessary antibiotic exposure. The question is which wounds cross the threshold into high-risk territory.

When Prophylaxis Is Indicated

The IDSA recommends preemptive early antimicrobial therapy for 3 to 5 days for patients who:[3]

  • Are immunocompromised
  • Are asplenic (have no working spleen)
  • Have advanced liver disease
  • Have preexisting or resultant edema (swelling) of the affected area
  • Have moderate to severe injuries, especially to the hand or face
  • Have injuries that may have penetrated the periosteum (bone lining) or joint capsule

A pediatric review adds bites that have undergone primary closure, puncture wounds, facial bites, and bites to the hands, feet, or genital area.[4] A wound that already shows redness, warmth, or swelling is an infection and needs treatment, not prophylaxis.

First-Line Antibiotic: Amoxicillin-Clavulanate

For infected bites, the IDSA recommends an antimicrobial agent or agents active against both aerobic and anaerobic bacteria, such as amoxicillin-clavulanate.[3] The pediatric review likewise names amoxicillin-clavulanate as the first-line choice.[4] In the multicenter bite study, a beta-lactam antibiotic combined with a beta-lactamase inhibitor was judged appropriate therapy based on the bacteria found.[2]

Indication Drug / Dose Duration
Prophylaxis, high-risk wound Amoxicillin-clavulanate. FDA label adult dose for more severe infections: one 875 mg tablet every 12 hours[13] 3 to 5 days[3]
Treatment, established infection Amoxicillin-clavulanate or another agent active against aerobic and anaerobic bacteria[3] Set by your clinician
Pediatric dosing (under 40 kg) FDA label dose for more severe infections: 45 mg/kg/day, given every 12 hours. Children 40 kg or more are dosed as adults.[13] Set by your clinician
Unable to take oral antibiotics Intravenous therapy Inpatient, individualized

Penicillin-Allergic Patients

If you have a true penicillin allergy (an actual allergic reaction, not just stomach upset), tell your clinician. The amoxicillin-clavulanate label says it is contraindicated in patients with a history of serious hypersensitivity reactions (such as anaphylaxis or Stevens-Johnson syndrome) to amoxicillin, clavulanate, or other beta-lactam antibacterial drugs such as penicillins and cephalosporins.[13] Any alternative regimen still needs to cover both aerobic and anaerobic bacteria.[3] Your clinician will choose the specific drugs based on your allergy history and the wound.

Tetanus Prophylaxis

The CDC groups wounds containing dirt, soil, feces, or saliva, including animal bites, with dirty or major wounds. For these wounds, a tetanus vaccine is recommended if the last dose was 5 or more years ago, compared with 10 or more years for clean and minor wounds.[6]

Practical Decision Rule

  • Complete primary series and last tetanus dose less than 5 years ago: No vaccine needed.[6]
  • Complete primary series and last tetanus dose 5 or more years ago: Tetanus vaccine (Tdap or Td).[6]
  • Unknown history, never vaccinated, or incomplete primary series: Vaccinate, and tetanus immune globulin (TIG) is also indicated for a dirty or major wound.[6]
  • People with HIV or a severe immunodeficiency: TIG is indicated for a dirty or major wound. TIG is never indicated for clean and minor wounds.[6]

Tdap is preferred for people who have not previously received Tdap or whose Tdap history is unknown, because it also protects against pertussis (whooping cough).[14] Tetanus itself, caused by Clostridium tetani, grows in the anaerobic conditions of deep wounds, which is exactly the profile of a dog bite puncture.

One important clarification: the CDC says antibiotics, topical or systemic, are not recommended during wound care to protect against tetanus.[6]

Rabies Risk Assessment: The 10-Day Rule and When PEP Is Needed

Rabies is almost universally fatal once symptoms develop. This is why every dog bite needs a rabies risk assessment, even for minor wounds. In the United States, rabies is mostly found in wild animals such as bats, raccoons, skunks, and foxes, and the CDC advises that a health official familiar with rabies should conduct the risk assessment.[15]

The Three Exposure Scenarios

Animal Category Rabies Risk Recommended Action
Known domestic dog, currently healthy and available for observation Low Observe the dog for 10 days. If it remains healthy, PEP is not indicated. If it becomes unwell, contact public health immediately.[5]
Dog that is unhealthy or unavailable for observation or testing Higher PEP likely indicated; contact public health immediately for further assessment.[5]
Bat, raccoon, fox, skunk, or other high-risk wildlife High Get assessed immediately. Direct contact with a bat counts unless a bite or scratch can be definitively ruled out.[15]

The 10-Day Observation Period

CDC guidance for veterinarians is to confine and observe a healthy dog, cat, or ferret that exposes a person for 10 days, in coordination with public health authorities. If signs of illness develop during those 10 days, the situation is reported to the local health department immediately, and the animal is not vaccinated during the observation period.[16]

Rabies Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP)

When PEP is indicated for a person who has never been vaccinated against rabies, it always includes both human rabies immune globulin (HRIG) and rabies vaccine:[1]

  • Human rabies immune globulin (HRIG): the recommended dose is 20 IU/kg. If possible, the full dose is infiltrated around the wound(s), and any remaining volume is given intramuscularly at a site distant from the vaccine.
  • Rabies vaccine: 1.0 mL intramuscularly in the deltoid area, one injection each on days 0, 3, 7, and 14. People with immune disorders receive a fifth dose on day 28.
  • HRIG is never given in the same syringe or the same anatomical site as the first vaccine dose.
  • Rabies vaccine is not given in the gluteal area.

For people who were previously vaccinated against rabies, PEP consists of two vaccine doses, on days 0 and 3, and rabies immune globulin is not given.[1]

ER, Urgent Care, or Telehealth: Where to Go

One of the most practical questions after a dog bite is where to seek care. The right answer depends on the wound, the dog, and your overall health status.

Go to the Emergency Department Immediately If:
  • Active bleeding that won't stop with sustained direct pressure
  • Wounds to the face or neck involving deep structures
  • Suspected joint, tendon, or bone penetration, particularly bites over a knuckle
  • Fever, chills, or rapidly spreading redness, which are signs of systemic infection
  • A bite from a stray or wild animal, or severe bites such as multiple bites or head and neck bites, which need immediate rabies assessment[15]
  • You are asplenic, immunocompromised, or have active cancer
  • The wound is large, a crush injury, or involves significant tissue loss
Level of Care Appropriate When
Emergency Department Uncontrolled bleeding; deep face/neck wounds; potential joint involvement; systemic infection signs; stray/wildlife rabies exposure; severe crush injury; asplenic or severely immunocompromised patient
Urgent Care Moderate laceration requiring irrigation and possible closure; controlled bleeding; needs wound assessment, tetanus evaluation, and antibiotic decision in person; known domestic vaccinated dog
Telehealth Triage Minor wound, bleeding controlled, known vaccinated domestic dog; needs antibiotic guidance, rabies risk discussion, and tetanus status review; wound is clean, shallow, and not on the hand or face

Telehealth is genuinely useful here. If you've been bitten by your neighbor's vaccinated Labrador and the wound is a shallow scrape on your forearm that has stopped bleeding after washing, you don't necessarily need to sit in an urgent care waiting room for three hours. A telehealth triage visit can review the wound (via video), assess your tetanus status, determine whether antibiotics are appropriate, and advise on wound monitoring. What telehealth cannot do is irrigate the wound under pressure, suture, or administer HRIG or vaccines. Know the limits and act accordingly.

Recognizing Infection: What to Watch For

Watch the wound closely over the first several days. Infections can show up early, so do not assume a wound is safe because it looked clean on the first day.

Warning Signs Requiring Prompt Medical Attention

  • Increasing redness spreading beyond the wound margin
  • Warmth and swelling that worsen rather than improve
  • Pus or cloudy discharge from the wound
  • Red streaking extending from the wound up an arm or leg. This is lymphangitis and indicates the infection is spreading through the lymphatic system. It requires same-day treatment.
  • Fever above 100.4°F (38°C) in the days following a bite
  • Increased pain in the days after the bite (wounds should hurt less over time, not more)
  • Swollen lymph nodes near the bite site

If you have no spleen and develop any of these signs, or simply feel unwell in ways that are difficult to describe, do not wait. Capnocytophaga symptoms typically begin about 3 to 5 days after a dog or cat bite, and asplenic patients can advance to organ failure and death within 24 to 72 hours of onset.[7] A 2015 review of about 484 reported cases of C. canimorsus infection found a case-fatality rate of about 26%.[17]

Reporting Requirements

Dog bite reporting requirements vary by state and jurisdiction. In California, for example, bites should be reported to the local animal control agency. Reporting is not meant to get the animal or its owner in trouble; it is a way for local officials to make sure the biting animal is healthy, licensed, and up to date on its rabies vaccinations.[18]

In most states, physicians are required by law to report animal bites.[10] As a patient, reporting the incident independently protects you as well. An official record establishes the dog's observation period and creates documentation if you need medical care later that is linked to the bite.

The specific reporting agency, whether animal control, the county health department, or both, depends on where you live. Your clinician or local health department website can confirm the requirements for your area.

The Psychological Impact: What Patients Don't Always Discuss

The physical wound heals. The emotional aftermath often takes longer, and it is frequently overlooked in clinical encounters focused on wound management.

Dog bite injuries, especially in children, can have psychological consequences. A 2024 review of 23 studies in BMJ Paediatrics Open found that the most common psychological consequences of dog bites in children were post-traumatic stress disorder, dog phobia, nightmares, and symptoms of anxiety and avoidance behaviours.[19] The authors noted that studies focused on these consequences and their management are sparse.[19]

Children who develop dog phobia after a bite face real limitations: avoiding parks, friends' homes with pets, and outdoor activities. Parents should watch for these behavioral changes in the weeks after an incident and not dismiss them as a temporary reaction that will self-resolve.

Adults are not immune. Fear of specific breeds or environments following a traumatic bite is normal and often proportionate to the severity of the experience. If anxiety or avoidance behaviors are affecting your daily life, ask your clinician about a referral for psychological care.

At TeleDirectMD, Dr. Bhavsar routinely asks about the emotional impact during follow-up visits. Physical healing matters, and so does getting back to life without fear. If the psychological component was not addressed at your initial visit, bring it up.
