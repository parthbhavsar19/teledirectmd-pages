Cyclosporiasis Outbreak 2026: Symptoms, Testing & Treatment

A physician-reviewed guide to the parasite behind the 2026 "explosive diarrhea" outbreak: what to watch for, the test to ask for, and how it's treated.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Published: July 14, 2026 · Tracker updated continuously

What is cyclosporiasis and how is the 2026 outbreak treated?

Cyclosporiasis is an intestinal infection caused by the parasite Cyclospora cayetanensis. It spreads through food or water contaminated with feces, and direct person-to-person spread is unlikely, per the CDC. For the 2026 season (May 1 to August 31), the CDC has received reports of 20,996 laboratory-confirmed cases acquired in the United States, with 1,161 hospitalizations and 2 deaths (CDC case data updated October 23, 2026), compared with 1,180 cases in the same period of 2025. Those figures include a multistate outbreak that the CDC and FDA linked, on epidemiologic and traceback data, to iceberg lettuce from Taylor Farms de Mexico: 12,883 illnesses in 21 states. The CDC declared that outbreak over on September 11, 2026, and the FDA has closed its investigation (FDA page current as of October 2, 2026). The hallmark is watery diarrhea that can last from a few days to a month or longer and may come and go. Cyclospora testing is not routine in most U.S. labs, so clinicians must specifically request it. The treatment of choice is TMP-SMX (Bactrim): one double-strength tablet (TMP 160 mg plus SMX 800 mg) twice daily for 7 to 10 days. A licensed clinician, by telehealth or in person, can order testing and prescribe treatment.

In 2026, the CDC received far more reports of U.S.-acquired cases than in the same season of 2025.[7] This guide, reviewed by a board-certified physician, explains what the parasite is, why it is easy to miss, how it is diagnosed and treated, and how to protect your household. What Is Cyclosporiasis?

Cyclosporiasis is an intestinal illness caused by the single-celled parasite Cyclospora cayetanensis. It spreads when people eat food or drink water contaminated with human feces. Importantly, direct spread from person to person is unlikely, because freshly passed parasites are not yet infectious: the CDC says it takes around 1 to 2 weeks outside the body for them to become infectious.[2]

The time between infection and illness is usually about one week but can range from 2 days to 2 weeks or more.[2] It also takes about six weeks from the start of symptoms until the CDC receives a case report,[7] which is one reason reported case counts lag behind current illness and outbreaks are slow to trace.

The Parasite, and Why It's So Hard to Wash Off

One quirk of Cyclospora's biology explains much about this illness: why it is unlikely to spread from person to person, and why outbreaks come from food and water. When the parasite is passed in stool, its egg-like oocysts are not yet infectious. They likely need days to weeks outside a host to become infectious.[8]

Three practical points follow:

  • It's unlikely to spread person to person. Because freshly passed oocysts are not yet infectious, the CDC says direct spread from person to person is unlikely.[2]
  • Cases peak in summer. The CDC defines the U.S. cyclosporiasis season as May 1 through August 31, the period with the highest number of cases in a typical year.[7]
  • Washing can't be trusted to remove it. The CDC says washing alone can't guarantee the parasite's removal, and its travel advice notes that routine treatment of food or water is unlikely to kill Cyclospora.[4]

Heat is the CDC's answer: it advises cooking produce to a temperature of at least 158 °F (70 °C).[4]

Symptoms: What Cyclospora Feels Like

The Cyclospora parasite infects the small intestine and can cause watery and sometimes frequent diarrhea.[2] What sets it apart from ordinary "stomach bugs" is its duration and its pattern: untreated, the illness may last from a few days to a month or longer, and it may follow a remitting-relapsing course, in which symptoms fade, you think you are better, and then they return.[1]

Common symptoms, per the CDC clinical overview, include:[1]

  • Watery diarrhea
  • Loss of appetite and weight loss
  • Abdominal cramping and bloating, and increased gas
  • Nausea and prolonged fatigue
  • Less often: vomiting, body aches, headaches, and low-grade fever

The clinical clue that matters most

Diarrhea that lasts more than a few days or keeps coming back in waves, especially during the May to August season, is the pattern that should prompt a conversation about Cyclospora-specific testing. The CDC advises clinicians to consider Cyclospora as a potential cause of prolonged diarrheal illness.[1]

Cyclospora vs. Viral Gastroenteritis ("Stomach Bug")

Most short-lived diarrhea is viral gastroenteritis, the common "stomach bug" caused by norovirus, rotavirus, and similar viruses. Because the early symptoms overlap, cyclosporiasis can be mistaken for an ordinary stomach bug, and that mistake matters: Cyclospora can last for weeks and come back in waves,[1] and it has a specific antibiotic treatment.[3] The distinguishing features below are what separate the two in practice.

The practical takeaway: if watery diarrhea has lasted more than a few days, keeps relapsing, or comes with notable weight loss and fatigue, particularly during the May to August season, do not assume it is a passing stomach bug. That pattern is the signal to seek care and specifically ask about Cyclospora testing. What Your Doctor Is Thinking: The Clinical Reasoning

When a patient describes several days of watery diarrhea, a clinician runs through a mental differential, and in a typical week Cyclospora would sit low on that list. Most acute diarrhea is viral and self-limited. What moves Cyclospora up the list is a specific combination of clues, and knowing them helps you advocate for the right workup.

The features that raise suspicion:

  • Duration and relapse. Diarrhea that has lasted more than about a week, or that improved and then relapsed, is not typical of a viral stomach bug. The CDC advises clinicians to consider Cyclospora as a potential cause of prolonged diarrheal illness.[1]
  • The clinical company it keeps. Loss of appetite, weight loss, and prolonged fatigue are among the common symptoms the CDC lists for cyclosporiasis.[1]
  • Timing and exposure. Illness during May–August, and any history of eating fresh produce like berries, leafy greens, or fresh herbs, fits the seasonal, produce-borne pattern.
  • Case volume. The 2026 season brought far more reported U.S. cases than 2025,[7] so a compatible illness is worth discussing even without a known exposure.

A clinician also weighs the alternatives that can mimic it: Giardia (another parasite causing prolonged, sometimes relapsing diarrhea and bloating), bacterial causes, and, in anyone recently on antibiotics, C. difficile. The reason this matters to you: Cyclospora is missed unless the clinician specifically requests testing for it.[1]

Why It's Missed: Diagnosis Requires a Specific Test

Here is the single most important thing for patients to understand: Cyclospora testing is not routine. The CDC says testing for Cyclospora is not routinely conducted in most U.S. laboratories, even in stool being tested for parasites, and not all gastrointestinal PCR panels include a target for Cyclospora.[5] Clinicians should specifically request testing for Cyclospora when it is indicated.[1] If a clinician does not specifically think of it and order the right test, the diagnosis is missed.

Testing methods the CDC describes include:[5]

  • Stool PCR that specifically includes a Cyclospora target (molecular testing for the parasite's DNA)
  • Modified acid-fast or modified ("hot") safranin staining, or ultraviolet fluorescence microscopy, under which the oocysts appear blue or green

Two practical caveats from the CDC: even people with symptoms might not shed enough oocysts in their stool to be readily detected,[1] and patients might need to submit several specimens collected on different days.[5] If your symptoms fit and the first test is negative, ask your clinician whether to repeat it.

Treatment: What Actually Works

The good news is that cyclosporiasis is treatable. The treatment of choice is the antibiotic trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX), sold as Bactrim, Septra, or Cotrim. The standard adult regimen, per the CDC, is:[3]

Standard adult regimen (CDC)

TMP 160 mg + SMX 800 mg (one double-strength tablet), by mouth, twice a day, for 7–10 days. Patients with weakened immune systems may need longer courses of therapy.

A few important points on treatment:

  • Allergy or intolerance to TMP-SMX: No highly effective alternative has been identified. Approaches the CDC describes include observation and symptomatic treatment, or nitazoxanide and ciprofloxacin if TMP-SMX is not feasible, though the effectiveness of either drug is uncertain. Desensitization should be considered only for selected patients who require treatment, have been evaluated by an allergist, and do not have a life-threatening allergy.[3] This is a decision to make with a clinician.
  • Recovery without treatment: The CDC notes that most patients with healthy immune systems will recover without treatment,[3] but untreated illness may last from a few days to a month or longer and may relapse.[1]
  • Rest and fluids matter. The CDC advises resting and drinking plenty of fluids if you have diarrhea.[2]

Prevention: Protecting Your Household

In 2026, the CDC and FDA linked a large multistate outbreak to iceberg lettuce from Taylor Farms de Mexico, based on epidemiologic and traceback data.[6][13] That outbreak has ended, and the CDC says the contaminated lettuce is no longer available in stores or restaurants.[13] General produce safety still matters, because outbreaks of cyclosporiasis have been linked to various types of fresh produce.[2] The most important thing to understand is that washing reduces risk but cannot guarantee removal of Cyclospora. The CDC says washing alone can't guarantee the parasite's removal and advises cooking produce to at least 158 °F (70 °C).[4]

Practical steps from CDC guidance:[4]

  • Wash hands with soap and water before and after handling or preparing raw fruits and vegetables.
  • Wash all fruits and vegetables thoroughly under running water before eating, cutting, or cooking.
  • Scrub firm produce such as melons and cucumbers with a clean produce brush under running water.
  • Cut away any damaged or bruised areas.
  • Cook produce to at least 158 °F (70 °C) when you want the most certainty, since washing alone can't guarantee the parasite's removal.
  • Refrigerate cut, peeled, or cooked fruits and vegetables as soon as possible (within two hours).

Historically, U.S. cyclosporiasis outbreaks have been linked to fresh produce including raspberries, fresh basil, cilantro, and lettuce-based salad mixes (see the table below).[11][14][15][9][12] The 2026 multistate outbreak, tied to iceberg lettuce from Taylor Farms de Mexico on epidemiologic and traceback evidence, fits that history of contaminated leafy greens.[6]

High-Risk Foods: What Three Decades of Outbreaks Tell Us

The 2026 lettuce-linked outbreak fits a long pattern. In the last two decades, an increasing number of foodborne outbreaks have been associated with fresh produce that is difficult to clean thoroughly and is eaten without processing.[10] The recurring culprits in the U.S. outbreaks below are raspberries, fresh herbs (basil, cilantro), and lettuce-based salad mixes.

Two patterns stand out. First, the implicated foods are fresh produce usually eaten without cooking.[10] Second, even careful traceback can fail to pin down a source: in the large 2020 bagged-salad outbreak, the FDA completed its traceback but did not determine the cause or source.[12] In 2026, by contrast, the FDA and CDC traced the multistate outbreak to a specific product.[6][13]

Recovery, Relapse, and What to Expect After Treatment

For most people who are not immunocompromised, the outlook is good: the CDC notes that most patients with healthy immune systems will recover without treatment.[3] Untreated, though, the illness may last from a few days to a month or longer and may relapse,[1] and TMP-SMX is the treatment of choice.[3] Energy and appetite can take time to return after a prolonged illness.

A few things to expect and watch for:

  • Finish the full course. Complete the prescribed 7–10 day TMP-SMX course, and tell your clinician if diarrhea returns after treatment. Patients with weakened immune systems may need longer courses of therapy.[3]
  • Rest and rehydrate. Rest and drink plenty of fluids while you have diarrhea, as the CDC advises.[2]
  • Lingering gut symptoms such as bloating or irregular bowel habits can persist briefly after any prolonged intestinal infection; new or worsening symptoms warrant a re-check.

Where to Get Care: Telehealth, In-Person, or the ER

Cyclosporiasis is, for most healthy adults, an outpatient illness, but knowing the right door to knock on saves time and worry.

Because Cyclospora requires a specific stool test and a specific antibiotic, neither of which is part of routine self-care, a prolonged or relapsing illness is genuinely worth a visit rather than waiting it out. A synchronous video visit with a physician is an efficient way to get the right test ordered and, when appropriate, treatment prescribed.

Who Is Most at Risk

Anyone can be infected. Most otherwise healthy people recover, with or without treatment, though the illness can drag on for weeks.[3] The CDC notes that children, people with weakened immune systems, and older people may experience more severe illness or complications from severe dehydration.[2] Patients with weakened immune systems may need longer courses of therapy.[3] Among the 20,996 laboratory-confirmed U.S.-acquired cases reported for the 2026 season, the CDC lists a median age of 47 years, with a range of 1 to 99 years (case data updated October 23, 2026).[7]

When to See a Doctor

Contact a clinician if watery diarrhea lasts more than a few days, keeps returning after seeming to improve, or comes with significant weight loss, dehydration, or profound fatigue, and ask specifically about Cyclospora testing. Seek urgent in-person care for signs of severe dehydration (dizziness, very little urine, rapid heartbeat), high fever, bloody stools, or an inability to keep fluids down.
