Out-of-Pocket Maximum, Explained: The 2026 Limits, What Counts, and What Does Not

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Published: September 14, 2026 · Updated: September 14, 2026
1,575 words

Citable summary: The out-of-pocket maximum is the most you have to pay for covered in-network services in a plan year. Once you have spent that amount on deductibles, copayments, and coinsurance for in-network care, your plan pays 100% of covered benefits. For 2026, the federal limit is no more than $10,600 for an individual or $21,200 for a family. Your monthly premium does not count toward it, and neither does out-of-network care or amounts above a plan's allowed amount. (HealthCare.gov glossary) (HealthCare.gov glossary)

What the out-of-pocket maximum is

The out-of-pocket maximum, sometimes called the out-of-pocket limit, is the most you have to pay for covered in-network services in a plan year. The clearest definition comes from the federal Marketplace: "The most you have to pay for covered services in a plan year. After you spend this amount on deductibles, copayments, and coinsurance for in-network care and services, your health plan pays 100% of the costs of covered benefits." (HealthCare.gov glossary)

It is a ceiling, not a goal, and not a single bill. It is the running total of the cost sharing you pay across the plan year.

What counts toward it

Three kinds of cost sharing count: your deductible, your copayments, and your coinsurance, all for in-network care and services. (HealthCare.gov glossary) Once those combined amounts reach the limit, the plan takes over for covered benefits.

What does not count toward it

Four categories are excluded, and they explain most of the surprises people hit. The federal Marketplace lists them as your monthly premium, anything you spend for services your plan does not cover, out-of-network care and services, and costs above the allowed amount for a service that a provider may charge. (HealthCare.gov glossary)

The last item, "costs above the allowed amount," is the one that catches people. If a provider bills more than what your plan considers the allowed amount, that difference is not a covered benefit and does not count toward your cap. This is the balance-billing problem, which the No Surprises Act addresses in specific situations.

The 2026 limits: $10,600 and $21,200

For 2026, an ACA-compliant plan may set its out-of-pocket maximum no higher than $10,600 for an individual or $21,200 for a family. The same federal glossary that defines the term lists these figures. (HealthCare.gov glossary)

These caps were revised higher for 2026 after a federal methodology change for the premium-adjustment percentage, and the change carries forward. The figure that governs your plan is whatever your plan actually set, which cannot be higher than the federal cap. (CMS 2027 payment parameters guidance)

How the maximum is set and revised each year

The federal limit is updated annually with an inflation-linked formula, and a methodology change raised the 2026 figures after they were first announced. The limit is set by increasing the 2014 baseline of $6,350 by the year's premium-adjustment percentage, with the family limit at twice the individual level, rounded down to the nearest $50. (CMS payment parameters guidance) The change to that formula produced the revised, higher 2026 caps of $10,600 and $21,200.

The practical point is that the number changes every year, so a limit quoted in an older article can already be stale. Confirm the figure against your own plan year.

Individual versus family limits

The federal family limit is set at twice the individual limit. In practice, plans must apply an embedded individual limit so that no single member of a family plan is exposed to the entire family amount for their own covered care. This means one difficult year for one person does not automatically trigger the family-level ceiling.

What happens once you reach it

After you hit the out-of-pocket maximum, "your health plan pays 100% of the costs of covered benefits" for in-network care. (HealthCare.gov glossary) This is the strongest protection in the policy, and it is why the maximum matters most during a major illness, surgery, or a chronic condition with recurring costs.

Out-of-pocket maximum versus deductible

The deductible is the entry threshold: pay that much first, then cost sharing begins to be split. The out-of-pocket maximum is the exit threshold: once your total cost sharing reaches it, you stop paying for covered care. The deductible is one component of the maximum, not a separate pool of money.

Cost-sharing reductions: a lower ceiling for lower incomes

If you buy a Silver Marketplace plan and your household income is below certain federal-poverty-level thresholds, cost-sharing reductions lower your out-of-pocket maximum further. In 2026 a typical Silver plan caps cost sharing at $10,600, but with a cost-sharing reduction the ceiling drops to no more than $3,500 for incomes at or below 200% of the federal poverty level, and no more than $8,450 for incomes between 201% and 250%. (KFF cost-sharing reductions)

These reduced limits apply only to Silver plans, and you qualify through the Marketplace application based on income. They are a big reason a lower-income enrollee can face far less exposure than the headline $10,600 number suggests.

HDHP out-of-pocket maximums are lower

Plans that qualify for a health savings account have their own, lower ceiling. For 2026, an HSA-qualified high-deductible health plan must cap out-of-pocket expenses, excluding premiums, at $8,500 for self-only coverage or $17,000 for family coverage. (IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-19)

This is one reason an HDHP can be more protective than people assume: while its deductible is higher, its total worst-case out-of-pocket cap is lower than the general ACA limit.

Metal tiers and cost sharing

The metal category signals the average split of costs. A Bronze plan pays about 60% of covered costs on average and you pay about 40%, so in an expensive year you reach the out-of-pocket maximum sooner. A Platinum plan pays about 90% and you pay about 10%, with lower cost sharing along the way. Silver plans with cost-sharing reductions can shift the split to between 73% and 96% paid by the plan. (HealthCare.gov plan categories)

How the maximum has grown

The 2026 individual limit of $10,600 is up from $9,200 in 2025, a jump driven partly by the formula change and partly by premium growth, while the family limit rose from $18,400 to $21,200. (CMS payment parameters guidance)

The ceiling protects you, but a higher ceiling also means a larger possible bill before full protection kicks in. That is why the out-of-pocket maximum deserves a close look when you compare plans year over year.

Surprise bills and the out-of-pocket maximum

Because out-of-network charges and amounts above the allowed amount do not count toward the maximum, a surprise bill can arrive even after you have met your cap. The No Surprises Act, which took effect in 2022, protects against certain surprise medical bills for emergency services, some out-of-network care at in-network facilities, and out-of-network air ambulance services. (CMS No Surprises Act fact sheet)

If you receive a large bill, first determine whether it is a covered in-network charge, an out-of-network balance bill, or a service your plan excludes. That distinction decides whether the out-of-pocket maximum applies. For help working through a bill you cannot afford, see our guide to getting help with a medical bill.

How to find your out-of-pocket maximum

It is stated in your Summary of Benefits and Coverage, alongside the deductible and the out-of-pocket limit for the plan. The summary also shows whether the plan uses an embedded individual limit and whether certain services are excluded from the calculation. The number in your member portal should match the summary.

Planning around the out-of-pocket maximum

If you know a large expense is coming, such as an eligible surgery, the timing within the plan year matters. Care completed in one plan year counts toward one limit; the same care split across a plan-year boundary can leave you paying toward two. The maximum is a safety net, not a discount, so a plan with a low maximum and low premium is often the stronger financial position for someone with predictable high medical use.

Common misunderstandings

  • Myth: "Meeting the maximum means everything is free." A plan pays 100% of covered in-network benefits, but non-covered services and out-of-network care can still bill you.
  • Myth: "The maximum includes my premium." It does not; the premium is paid on top of all cost sharing.
  • Myth: "Only the deductible counts." Copays and coinsurance count too, which is how many people reach the limit faster than they expected.
