Shingrix Vaccine Guide: Who Needs It, Effectiveness, Side Effects, and Timing

A physician-reviewed 2026 guide to Shingrix recommendations, dosing, effectiveness, side effects, contraindications, coverage, and the end of Zostavax.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MDLast reviewed August 10, 202611 min read

Who should get Shingrix, and how effective is the shingles vaccine?

CDC recommends Shingrix, a two-dose, non-live vaccine, for immunocompetent adults age 50 and older and for adults age 19 and older who are or will be immunocompromised. Pivotal trials found 97.2% efficacy against shingles in adults age 50 and older and 91.3% in pooled adults age 70 and older. CDC's vaccine recommendations, the ZOE-50 trial and the ZOE-70 trial provide the core evidence.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated August 10, 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • Shingrix is a non-live, two-dose vaccine. ACIP preferred it over the older live vaccine, Zostavax, which is no longer available in the United States. [3][4][8]
  • Adults age 50+ and immunocompromised adults age 19+ are eligible under ACIP recommendations. [3][4]
  • The standard dose interval is 2 to 6 months, with a 1 to 2 month option for immunocompromised adults who would benefit from a shorter schedule. [4]
  • Trial efficacy was 97.2% in adults age 50+ and 91.3% in pooled adults age 70+. [1][2]
  • A sore arm and short-term systemic reactions are common; CDC and ACIP advise getting the second dose even after such a reaction. [1][3][10]
  • Coverage is plan-specific, so confirm your cost with the insurer or pharmacy.

For general shingles diagnosis, treatment, and the 72-hour window, see the complete shingles guide.

What Is Shingrix?

Shingrix is a recombinant, adjuvanted zoster vaccine. It is not a live vaccine. Its purpose is to prevent shingles and related complications, the most common of which is postherpetic neuralgia (PHN). In 2017, ACIP voted to prefer it over the older live zoster vaccine. [3][4]

Who Should Get the Shingles Vaccine?

ACIP recommends two Shingrix doses for immunocompetent adults age 50 and older. In October 2021 (published in January 2022), ACIP extended the recommendation to adults age 19 and older who are or will be immunodeficient or immunosuppressed, because the risk of shingles and related complications is generally higher in these groups. CDC's current recommendations say the same. [3][4][8]

How Effective Is Shingrix?

The ZOE-50 trial found 97.2% vaccine efficacy against shingles in adults age 50 and older. In pooled data from ZOE-50 and ZOE-70, efficacy in adults age 70 and older was 91.3%, and efficacy against PHN was 88.8% in that age group. [1][2] An interim analysis of an open-label follow-up study found that efficacy remained high about 10 years after vaccination. [6] In the final analysis, which compared vaccinated participants with historical placebo data, efficacy against shingles was 87.7% from one month after the second dose through the end of follow-up in adults 50 and older, and 82.0% in the eleventh year after vaccination. [7]

Trial results are estimates from the ZOE-50 and ZOE-70 studies.[1][2]

Shingrix Dosing Schedule

The standard series is two doses, 2 to 6 months apart. For adults who are or will be immunocompromised and would benefit from a shorter schedule, the second dose may be given 1 to 2 months after the first. A clinician can help choose timing around immune-suppressing treatment. [3][4][9]

The shortened interval is for immunocompromised adults who would benefit from completing the series sooner.[4][8]

Safety and Expected Side Effects

Local and systemic reactions are common. In the FDA prescribing information, solicited reactions in adults age 50 and older included injection-site pain (78%), redness (38%) and swelling (26%), muscle aches (45%), fatigue (45%), headache (38%), shivering (27%), fever (21%) and gastrointestinal symptoms (17%). These reactions had a median duration of 2 to 3 days, and CDC advises getting the second dose even after one of them. [1][9][10]

The Shingrix label warns that postmarketing studies found a small increased risk of Guillain-Barré syndrome, a rare nerve disorder, in the 42 days after vaccination: about 3 extra cases per million doses in adults 65 and older. Seek care promptly for new weakness or tingling in the legs or arms in the weeks after a dose.[9]

Shingrix in Immunocompromised Adults

Evidence and ACIP guidance support Shingrix in immunocompromised adults age 19 and older. A phase 3 randomized trial also evaluated immunogenicity and safety in adults with hematologic malignancies who were vaccinated during or after immunosuppressive cancer treatment. ACIP advises vaccinating before immune suppression starts when possible, or otherwise during periods of lower immunosuppression and stable disease, so timing should be coordinated with the treating team. [4][5]

Shingrix Compared With Zostavax

Zostavax was a single-dose, live attenuated zoster vaccine. Shingrix is non-live, and ACIP preferred it because of higher and longer-lasting efficacy. Zostavax has not been available for use in the United States since November 18, 2020. CDC recommends Shingrix for eligible adults whether or not they previously received Zostavax. [3][8]

Cost, Coverage, and Contraindications

Coverage varies. Confirm whether a plan uses a pharmacy benefit, medical benefit, deductible, or preferred pharmacy. Shingrix should not be given to anyone who has had a severe allergic reaction, such as anaphylaxis, to a vaccine component or after a previous dose. It is generally deferred during active shingles or a moderate to severe acute illness. [3][4][9]
