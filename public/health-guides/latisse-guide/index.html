Latisse

How does Latisse (bimatoprost) work for eyelash growth, and does it actually work?

Latisse (bimatoprost ophthalmic solution 0.03%) is a prescription drug approved by the FDA in December 2008 to treat hypotrichosis of the eyelashes (inadequate or not enough lashes) by increasing their growth, including length, thickness and darkness. Bimatoprost was first developed as a glaucoma eye drop. Applied once nightly to the skin of the upper eyelid margin with a sterile applicator, it is believed to work by increasing the percentage of lashes in the anagen (growth) phase and the length of that phase. In the pivotal trial of 278 adults, 78% of bimatoprost-treated patients had at least a one-grade improvement on the Global Eyelash Assessment at 16 weeks, versus 18% with vehicle (Smith et al., J Am Acad Dermatol 2012). According to the FDA-approved label, mean eyelash length rose 25% from baseline versus 2% with vehicle, the effect is not significant in most patients until 2 months, and eyelash growth is expected to return to its pre-treatment level after stopping. The most common side effects (about 3% to 4% of patients) are eye itching, eye redness and skin darkening, and the label warns that increased brown iris pigmentation can occur and is likely to be permanent. A licensed clinician can prescribe Latisse after reviewing eye health and medical history, including any glaucoma eye drops.

This guide is a physician's evidence-based look at how bimatoprost works, what the pivotal clinical trial actually showed, how to use it correctly, what side effects to expect, and how it compares with off-label latanoprost and over-the-counter eyelash serums in 2026. What Is Eyelash Hypotrichosis?

Eyelash hypotrichosis is the clinical term for having an inadequate amount of eyelashes. Lashes can also be lost for specific reasons, including alopecia areata, infection, thyroid disease, some medications (for example, antimetabolite drugs), radiation or trauma.[6] It is a cosmetic and sometimes psychosocial concern rather than a dangerous condition, but it is common enough that an entire consumer product category, eyelash growth serums, has emerged around it. As cosmetics, these products do not require FDA approval and have not been critically evaluated for efficacy.[6]

How common is it, and who is affected

Eyelash thinning becomes more noticeable for many people with age, and it can also follow eyelid trauma or blepharitis (eyelid margin inflammation). Eyelash loss after chemotherapy is a recognized and distressing consequence of cytotoxic treatment.[13] Bimatoprost has randomized trial evidence both in idiopathic eyelash hypotrichosis and in chemotherapy-related eyelash loss in adults.[2][14]

Many patients seeking eyelash growth treatment do not have a formal medical diagnosis at all. They simply have lashes they consider too short, too sparse, or too pale, often after years of mascara use, lash extensions, or curling that can cause mechanical breakage over time. The FDA-approved indication for Latisse, hypotrichosis of the eyelashes defined as having inadequate or not enough eyelashes, is intentionally broad enough to cover this population as well as patients with a clearer underlying cause. This matters practically because it means a specific diagnosis is not required to qualify for treatment. A clinician mainly needs to confirm the eyes are healthy enough for treatment and that expectations are realistic.

Trichotillomania and traumatic lash loss

Trichotillomania, a hair-pulling disorder that can affect eyelashes as well as scalp and eyebrow hair, is a distinct cause of lash loss that benefits from behavioral treatment addressing the underlying pulling behavior alongside any pharmacologic approach to regrowth. Bimatoprost can support regrowth once pulling has stopped, but it does not address the compulsive behavior itself, and patients with suspected trichotillomania are generally better served by a combined approach involving a mental health professional. Eyelid trauma, including from prior blepharoplasty or eyelid surgery, is another cause where regrowth potential depends on whether the hair follicles themselves were structurally damaged, since bimatoprost can only stimulate follicles that remain viable.

Bimatoprost has an FDA-approved indication for eyelash growth

Many eyelash serums are marketed as clinically proven or dermatologist recommended. Bimatoprost 0.03% has an FDA-approved indication for eyelash hypotrichosis and a published Phase 3 randomized controlled trial.[1][2] Cosmetic eyelash products do not require FDA approval and have not been critically evaluated for efficacy or long-term safety.[6]

How Bimatoprost Works: The Mechanism

The eyelash hair cycle

Like scalp hair, eyelashes cycle through three phases: anagen (active growth), catagen (transition), and telogen (resting, followed by shedding). The eyelash anagen phase lasts roughly one to two months, and its duration largely determines how long lashes grow.[6] Only about 41% of upper eyelash follicles are active at any given time, compared with 84% of scalp follicles.[6]

Prostaglandin receptor activation

Bimatoprost is a synthetic prostamide analog that was first developed as a glaucoma eye drop, approved in 2001 to lower elevated eye pressure.[6] Its effect on lashes was a chance discovery: patients in the glaucoma trials commonly reported eyelash growth, which was recorded as a side effect.[6] The label states that the exact mechanism is unknown, but eyelash growth is believed to occur by increasing the percentage of hairs in, and the duration of, the anagen phase.[9]

Follicle size and pigmentation

Beyond extending anagen, bimatoprost appears to increase the size of the dermal papilla and hair bulb and to stimulate melanogenesis (pigment production), which would explain why treated lashes become thicker and darker as well as longer.[3][6] These follicle effects were largely demonstrated in animal studies.[3] A separate small randomized study of 19 subjects, which applied bimatoprost in a gel suspension to the base of the eyelashes for 6 weeks, found mean lash growth of 2.0 mm versus 1.1 mm with placebo.[7]

The Pivotal Phase 3 Trial: What Latisse Actually Does

Study design

The pivotal trial was a multicenter, randomized, double-masked, vehicle-controlled, parallel-group study of 278 adults with minimal or moderate eyelash prominence.[2][6] Participants applied either bimatoprost 0.03% (n=137) or vehicle (inactive base solution, n=141) once daily to the upper eyelid margins for 16 weeks of treatment.[2]

Primary endpoint: Global Eyelash Assessment

The primary endpoint was at least a one-grade increase on the Global Eyelash Assessment (GEA), a four-point scale of overall eyelash prominence rated by investigators.[9] At week 16, 78 percent of bimatoprost-treated patients achieved at least a one-grade improvement on the GEA, compared with 18 percent of vehicle-treated patients (P < .0001).[2]

Secondary endpoints: length, thickness, darkness

Digital image analysis of secondary endpoints showed that, from baseline to week 16, eyelash length increased 25 percent, thickness (fullness) 106 percent, and darkness 18 percent with bimatoprost, compared with 2, 12 and 3 percent with vehicle.[9] These three components, length, thickness, and darkness, are exactly the qualities most eyelash serums claim to improve but rarely measure in a controlled trial.

What's Changed in 2024 to 2026

The core evidence for bimatoprost has been stable since its 2008 approval. A few points matter for patients considering treatment now.

Data in chemotherapy-related eyelash loss. A randomized, double-masked, placebo-controlled study in patients with significant eyelash loss after chemotherapy found that once-daily bimatoprost restored eyelash growth and prominence more quickly than the natural recovery seen with vehicle.[13] The current label also describes a 16-week study in children and adolescents with no new safety issues; in its small post-chemotherapy group, 85% of Latisse-treated and 100% of vehicle-treated patients improved, so that group did not show a benefit over natural regrowth.[9] This guide covers adults.

Generic bimatoprost is available. The FDA’s Drugs@FDA database lists generic bimatoprost 0.03% topical solutions as therapeutically equivalent to Latisse (rated AT).[1] Prices vary by pharmacy, so check a current price before filling.

Telehealth prescribing. Because bimatoprost is not a controlled substance[9] and a straightforward prescription for healthy adults does not require an in-person slit-lamp exam, Latisse and generic bimatoprost can be prescribed through a synchronous telehealth visit with a clinician licensed in the patient’s state, following a review of eye health history and any glaucoma or intraocular pressure concerns.

None of this changes the core evidence base. Generic options and telehealth visits can make access easier for many candidates without an in-person ophthalmology visit.

How to Apply Latisse Correctly

Sterile applicator technique

Latisse ships with sterile, single-use applicator brushes. One drop is placed on the applicator, which is then drawn along the skin of the upper eyelid margin at the base of the lashes, similar to applying liquid eyeliner, not dropped into the eye itself.[9] A fresh applicator is used for each eye because reusing applicators increases the potential for contamination and infection.[9]

Once-nightly dosing

The labeled dose is one application to the upper eyelid margin of each eye, once nightly, on a clean face after removing contact lenses and makeup. Excess solution beyond the upper eyelid margin should be blotted away with a tissue, because repeated contact with other skin, such as the cheek, can cause hair growth outside the treatment area.[9]

What to avoid: eye contact and lower lid

Bimatoprost should not be applied to the lower eyelash line.[9] According to the label, if a small amount gets into the eye itself it will not cause harm, and the eye should not be rinsed. Additional applications will not increase eyelash growth, so more than the labeled one drop per eye adds nothing.[9]

Order of operations: makeup, skincare, and other eye drops

Latisse is applied to a clean face after makeup and contact lenses are removed.[9] If you use a prescription eye drop for glaucoma or eye pressure, especially a prostaglandin-type drop such as bimatoprost or latanoprost, talk with your physician before starting Latisse, and expect your eye pressure to be monitored.[9]

Consistency matters more than perfect technique

If you miss a night, do not double up the next night: the label states that additional applications will not increase eyelash growth.[9] The pivotal trial used once-daily application for 16 weeks,[2] and results with irregular use were not studied in that trial.

Contact lens users: remove before application

Contact lenses should be removed before applying bimatoprost and can be reinserted 15 minutes after application, because the solution contains benzalkonium chloride, a preservative that may be absorbed by and discolor soft contact lenses.[9]

What to Expect: The Treatment Timeline

Weeks 1 to 4

Most people see little change in the first month. In the pivotal trial, 15% of bimatoprost-treated patients had at least a one-grade improvement by week 4, compared with 8% on vehicle.[9] Mild itching or redness can occur; a 2010 review noted that these mild effects often seem self-limited and can improve with continued treatment.[6]

Weeks 4 to 16

The label states that the onset of effect is gradual and is not significant in most patients until 2 months.[9] In the pivotal trial, the share of bimatoprost-treated patients with at least a one-grade improvement rose to 50% at week 8, 69% at week 12 and 78% at week 16, compared with 15%, 20% and 18% on vehicle. Improvements in length, thickness and darkness were significantly greater than with vehicle at weeks 8, 12 and 16.[9]

Maintenance dosing after 16 weeks

The label directs once-nightly application.[9] In a 12-month randomized trial of daily application, the effect seen by month 6 was maintained or enhanced at 12 months.[14] Whether a less frequent maintenance schedule works as well has not been established in the trials cited here; a 2010 review listed it as a topic for future investigation.[6]

Discontinuation reverses effects

Bimatoprost's effect on the eyelash hair cycle is not permanent. The label states that eyelash growth is expected to return to its pre-treatment level after stopping.[9] In a 12-month trial, the effect in idiopathic hypotrichosis was maintained for about 2 months after stopping and was markedly diminished 4 to 6 months after stopping.[14]

Side Effects: The Evidence

Local ocular side effects

In the 278-patient pivotal trial, the most frequently reported adverse reactions were eye pruritus (itching), conjunctival hyperemia (eye redness), skin hyperpigmentation, ocular irritation, dry eye symptoms, and periorbital erythema (redness around the eye), each in less than 4% of patients; the most common occurred in about 3% to 4%.[9] Only conjunctival hyperemia was significantly more common than with vehicle.[2] A 2010 review noted that mild effects such as redness, irritation or itching often seem self-limited and can improve with continued treatment.[6] A small open-label study of 28 women who applied bimatoprost to the upper lid margin for 12 weeks reported no serious or unexpected adverse events.[4]

Eyelid hyperpigmentation

Darkening of the eyelid skin and eyelashes can occur. The label says this pigmentation is expected to increase as long as bimatoprost is used, but has been reported to reverse after stopping in most patients.[9] The risk of these skin changes can be reduced by limiting contact of the solution with the skin, which is why careful application and blotting matter.[6]

Periorbital fat atrophy

Another clinically important effect associated with bimatoprost is periorbital fat atrophy, a deepening of the upper eyelid sulcus that can subtly change eyelid contour and appearance. It was first described in glaucoma patients using bimatoprost eye drops,[5][12] and the Latisse label lists periorbital fat atrophy with deepening of the eyelid sulcus and eyelid ptosis among postmarketing reports, where frequency cannot be reliably estimated.[9] In a glaucoma case series, the changes were partially reversible after the drop was stopped, so recovery is not guaranteed to be complete.[5]

Iris pigmentation: what the label says

The Latisse label warns that increased iris pigmentation has occurred when bimatoprost solution was administered, and that patients should be advised about the potential for increased brown iris pigmentation, which is likely to be permanent. Iris hyperpigmentation is listed among adverse reactions seen in clinical trial experience with Latisse.[9] Iris color changes may not be noticeable for several months to years; typically the brown color around the pupil spreads outward and the iris becomes more brownish. The label states that treatment can be continued in patients who develop noticeably increased iris pigmentation, so this is a point to discuss before starting rather than an automatic reason to stop.[9]

Who Should Not Use Latisse

Contraindications

Bimatoprost is contraindicated in patients with a known hypersensitivity to bimatoprost or any ingredient of the product. The label advises caution in patients with active intraocular inflammation, such as uveitis, because the inflammation may be made worse. People using a prostaglandin eye drop to lower eye pressure should use Latisse only after consulting their physician and should have their eye pressure monitored, because using both may interfere with the pressure-lowering effect.[9]

Precautions: uveitis, macular edema, cystoid macular edema

Macular edema, including cystoid macular edema, has been reported with bimatoprost eye drops used for glaucoma. The Latisse label advises caution in people without a natural lens (aphakia), people with an artificial lens and a torn posterior lens capsule, and people with known risk factors for macular edema.[9] People in these groups, or with active intraocular inflammation such as uveitis, should be evaluated by an eye care professional before starting treatment.

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

There are no adequate and well-controlled studies of Latisse in pregnant women, and the label advises use during pregnancy only if the potential benefit justifies the potential risk to the fetus. The label also notes no increase in the risk of major birth defects or miscarriage based on postmarketing experience with bimatoprost. It is not known whether enough is absorbed to appear in human milk.[9] Given the purely cosmetic indication, it is reasonable to discuss deferring treatment until after pregnancy and breastfeeding.

Contact lens wearers

Latisse should not be applied while contact lenses are in place. Contact lenses should be removed before application and can be reinserted 15 minutes afterward, because the preservative benzalkonium chloride may be absorbed by and discolor soft contact lenses.[9] This is a simple step that is easy to forget in a nightly routine.

Active eye conditions and recent eye surgery

The label advises patients who develop a new eye condition, such as trauma or infection, or who have eye surgery, to seek their physician’s advice about continuing Latisse.[9] In practice, it is sensible to have active blepharitis, conjunctivitis, or another eyelid or ocular surface infection treated before starting bimatoprost, since a new product on inflamed skin can make it harder to tell a drug side effect from the infection, and to wait for clearance from the eye surgeon after recent intraocular surgery, including cataract surgery.

Darker eye color and pigmentation risk counseling

Because the label warns that increased brown iris pigmentation can occur and is likely to be permanent, this deserves a direct conversation before starting, whatever your eye color. The label describes the typical pattern as brown color around the pupil spreading outward until the whole iris, or parts of it, become more brownish.[9] Patients who would be troubled by a permanent change in eye color should weigh that before choosing treatment.

Latisse vs Latanoprost: The Off-Label Alternative

Latanoprost 0.005% is another prostaglandin analog, approved to lower elevated eye pressure in open-angle glaucoma or ocular hypertension.[16] Its label lists gradual eyelash changes in the treated eye, including increased length, thickness and number of lashes,[16] and a small study of 17 glaucoma patients measured longer lashes after latanoprost.[8] However, its use for cosmetic purposes is not FDA-approved, and a 2010 review noted that applying latanoprost to the eyelid margin for eyelash growth had not been evaluated in controlled clinical trials, whereas bimatoprost has a 278-patient randomized trial.[6][2] Formulation, concentration, and preservative differences between glaucoma-dose latanoprost bottles and a dedicated eyelash product mean the off-label approach carries more uncertainty about optimal dosing for this specific cosmetic use.

Why some patients still choose latanoprost

The practical case for off-label latanoprost usually comes down to convenience rather than superior evidence. A patient already using latanoprost eye drops for glaucoma may notice eyelash changes as a known effect of the drop,[16] but should not add Latisse without talking to their physician first.[9] For patients without glaucoma, the lower concentration (0.005% versus 0.03%) and a formulation intended for use in the eye rather than on the eyelid margin mean that dosing from bimatoprost trials does not directly transfer.[9][16]

Comparative potency and receptor binding

Head-to-head data for eyelash growth are limited. In a three-month trial in glaucoma patients that compared the two as eye drops, increased eyelash growth was reported significantly more often with bimatoprost than with latanoprost; a 2010 review noted that whether the same difference holds for eyelid-margin application had not been studied.[6] Latanoprost has also been reported to stimulate eyelash growth in glaucoma patients, and prostaglandin analogs have been raised as possible future treatments for other forms of hair loss.[10]

Bimatoprost has also been tested on eyebrows. A randomized, double-blind pilot study of 20 people with mild to moderate eyebrow hypotrichosis found that daily bimatoprost improved eyebrow appearance more than vehicle.[11] This eyebrow data is a useful reference point for patients curious whether the same approach could apply to other areas, though eyebrow application is off-label and outside the scope of Latisse's approved indication.

OTC "Eyelash Serums": Peptides, Panthenol, and Marketing Claims

Over-the-counter eyelash products are marketed as eyelash stimulators, enhancement serums or conditioners, and contain ingredients such as proprietary peptides, natural extracts, vitamins and, in some cases, prostaglandin analogs. Because cosmetic products do not require FDA approval, these products have not been critically evaluated for efficacy, and their short- and long-term safety has not been fully studied.[6]

Some OTC serums achieve a visual effect through conditioning ingredients that reduce lash breakage and improve the appearance of existing lashes, which is a legitimate but fundamentally different mechanism from stimulating new anagen-phase growth. Patients should understand that marketing language like clinically tested or dermatologist recommended does not imply the same regulatory standard as an FDA-approved drug indication.

Some OTC serums contain prostaglandin analogs,[6] the same drug class as bimatoprost, but are sold without the dosing instructions and safety warnings of an approved drug label. Reading the full ingredient list, not just the marketing claims, matters for these products specifically.

What the research actually shows for peptides and biotin

Peptide complexes marketed for lash growth are generally derived from research on skin barrier function or scalp hair biology, then extrapolated to eyelashes without dedicated trial data. Biotin (vitamin B7) deficiency can cause hair thinning, but true biotin deficiency is uncommon in people eating a typical diet, and supplementing biotin in someone who is not deficient has not been shown to meaningfully accelerate eyelash growth in controlled research. Panthenol is primarily a humectant and conditioning agent that can make hair look and feel healthier by reducing surface damage and improving light reflection, which is a real but cosmetic effect rather than a follicular growth effect.

Reading serum marketing claims critically

Phrases like clinically proven, dermatologist tested, and visible results in 4 weeks appear frequently on OTC serum packaging without a citation to a specific published trial that a patient or clinician can verify. When a product does cite a study, it is worth checking whether the study was placebo-controlled, randomized, and published in a peer-reviewed journal, the same standard that Latisse's pivotal trial met. Many serum studies are small, unblinded, sponsor-funded, and unpublished outside a company website, which does not carry the same evidentiary weight as an FDA-registration trial.

Cost, Insurance, and Generic Bimatoprost

Because eyelash growth is a cosmetic indication, insurance coverage varies by plan, so check with your insurer before filling. Prices vary by pharmacy and change over time, so this guide does not list them. Generic bimatoprost 0.03% topical solution, which the FDA rates as therapeutically equivalent to Latisse, is an option to ask about.[1]

Latisse is supplied as a 3 mL bottle with 70 sterile applicators or a 5 mL bottle with 140 applicators.[9] At one applicator per eye each night, 70 applicators cover 35 nights. For longer-term use, a 12-month randomized trial found daily bimatoprost effective and well tolerated in idiopathic and chemotherapy-related eyelash hypotrichosis, with no drug-related serious adverse events reported.[14]

What Doesn't Work: Castor Oil, Vaseline, and Other Home Remedies

Castor oil, coconut oil, and petroleum jelly (Vaseline) are widely recommended online for eyelash growth, but no randomized controlled trial supports an actual growth-stimulating effect for any of them. These products may make existing lashes appear shinier and reduce breakage by coating and conditioning the hair shaft, and improved cosmetic appearance of existing lashes can be mistaken for true growth, but they do not prolong the anagen phase or increase follicle activity the way bimatoprost does.

Eyelash extensions and lash lift procedures are cosmetic services that alter the appearance of existing lashes through adhesive attachment or a chemical perm, not treatments that stimulate the patient's own eyelash growth. They carry their own risks: the glues used to attach artificial lashes commonly contain methacrylate, which can cause allergic reactions in susceptible people.[6]

Red Flags: When to Contact a Clinician

Most bimatoprost side effects are mild and local, but certain symptoms warrant prompt evaluation rather than continued self-treatment:

  • Significant eye pain, vision changes, or new visual disturbance
  • Signs of eye infection: purulent discharge, significant swelling, or worsening redness that does not improve
  • Progressive or unexplained change in iris color
  • Noticeable deepening of the upper eyelid sulcus or change in eyelid contour that concerns the patient
  • Any known history of uveitis, macular edema, or intraocular surgery that was not previously discussed with a clinician before starting treatment

Patients with any of these findings should contact their prescribing clinician or an eye care professional promptly rather than waiting to see if symptoms resolve on their own. The label advises seeking a physician’s advice about continuing Latisse after a new eye condition such as trauma or infection, a sudden decrease in vision, eye surgery, or any eye reaction, particularly conjunctivitis and eyelid reactions.[9]

What a same-day telehealth visit can and cannot address

A synchronous video visit is well suited to evaluating mild itching, redness, or skin darkening at the lash line, reviewing application technique, and deciding whether to continue, pause, or stop treatment based on a description and, when helpful, photos of the affected area. It is not a substitute for an in-person slit-lamp eye examination when there is concern for intraocular inflammation, a corneal problem, or a vision change, and a clinician conducting a video visit should have a low threshold to refer those specific concerns to same-day, in-person ophthalmologic or optometric care rather than trying to fully evaluate them remotely.

It is not known whether it passes into human milk.[9] Given the purely cosmetic indication, it is reasonable to discuss deferring treatment until after pregnancy and breastfeeding.

A licensed clinician can review eye health history and prescribe bimatoprost through a synchronous telehealth visit, provided the clinician is licensed in the patient's state.

Prices vary by pharmacy and change over time, so check a current price before filling. Generic bimatoprost 0.03% topical solution is available, and the FDA rates it as therapeutically equivalent to Latisse.[1] Because it is a cosmetic indication, insurance coverage varies by plan.

Generic bimatoprost 0.03% topical solution contains the same active ingredient at the same strength as brand-name Latisse, and the FDA's Drugs@FDA database rates the approved generics as therapeutically equivalent to Latisse (code AT).[1] Inactive ingredients can differ between manufacturers, so ask the pharmacist if you have a known sensitivity.

Not as an FDA-approved indication. Latisse is approved only for eyelash hypotrichosis, and eyebrow use is off-label. It has been studied specifically for eyebrows, though: a Phase 3, randomized, vehicle-controlled trial of 357 adults found that bimatoprost 0.03% applied once or twice daily significantly improved eyebrow fullness and darkness over vehicle, with visible change starting around month 1 to 2 of the 7-month treatment course, and no cases of skin or iris hyperpigmentation or conjunctival hyperemia reported in that trial population.[15] An earlier, smaller pilot study reported a similar improvement.[11] Because eyebrow use is not part of the FDA-approved indication, the evidence base is real but thinner than for eyelashes, and off-label use is best discussed with a prescribing clinician rather than assumed to carry an identical safety profile.

Latisse contains bimatoprost, an FDA-approved drug with Phase 3 randomized controlled trial evidence of eyelash growth.[1][2] OTC eyelash serums typically contain peptides, natural extracts or vitamins and are sold as cosmetics, which do not require FDA approval and have not been critically evaluated for efficacy or long-term safety.[6] Some may improve the appearance of existing lashes through conditioning effects.
