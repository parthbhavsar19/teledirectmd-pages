Impetigo

A physician's guide to symptoms, contagiousness, treatment, healing time, and when to see a doctor.

Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD
Last reviewed: September 7, 2026
12 min read

What is the best treatment for impetigo?

The best treatment depends on how much skin is involved. For mild impetigo in adults, bullous or nonbullous, a topical antibiotic such as mupirocin 2% or retapamulin 1% twice daily for 5 days is first line, and a Cochrane review found topical mupirocin as effective as, or more effective than, oral antibiotics. For numerous lesions, outbreaks, ecthyma, or impetigo that does not respond to a topical antibiotic within 3 to 5 days, oral antibiotics such as dicloxacillin or cephalexin are recommended, with doxycycline, clindamycin, or TMP-SMX when MRSA is suspected or confirmed. Antibiotic treatment also limits spread to others. A licensed clinician can confirm the diagnosis and prescribe appropriate therapy, in person or via telehealth.
Medically reviewed by Parth Bhavsar, MD. Updated September 7, 2026.

Key Takeaways

  • Impetigo is a contagious superficial skin infection. It is most common in children ages 2 to 5, but adults get it too, and more than 3 million U.S. cases occur each year.[3][11]
  • It is caused by Staphylococcus aureus and group A Streptococcus. About 70 percent of cases are nonbullous (honey-colored crusts) and about 30 percent are bullous (blisters caused by toxin-producing S. aureus).[3][5]
  • Mild impetigo, bullous or nonbullous, is usually treated first with mupirocin 2% or retapamulin 1% ointment. A Cochrane review found topical mupirocin as effective as, or more effective than, oral antibiotics.[1][3][2]
  • Oral antibiotics, usually a 7-day course of dicloxacillin or cephalexin, are recommended for numerous lesions, outbreaks, ecthyma, or impetigo that does not respond to a topical antibiotic within 3 to 5 days. Doxycycline, clindamycin, or sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim (TMP-SMX) is used when MRSA is suspected or confirmed.[1][3]
  • Antibiotics limit spread to others. CDC guidance allows a return to work or school at least 12 hours after starting antibiotics with sores covered. Wash clothing, linens, and towels daily.[5]
  • One reference text estimates that treated impetigo is cured within 10 days, while untreated impetigo heals in 14 to 21 days and about 20 percent of cases resolve on their own.[7]
  • Complications are rare but include post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (a kidney problem), cellulitis, and ecthyma, a deeper ulcerating form of impetigo.[4][10]

Impetigo is the kind of skin infection that spreads through a household, a team, or any close-contact group faster than people expect, largely because no one realizes how contagious it is until a second or third case shows up. It is a superficial infection, meaning it stays in the top layers of the skin rather than spreading deeper, and in most patients it is mild and clears with simple treatment. What makes it worth taking seriously is not the severity of the sores themselves but how easily they move from person to person and, in a small number of cases, a delayed kidney complication that can follow the skin infection.

This guide is written for adults 18 and older and covers what impetigo is, why it appears, how long it lasts, the difference between the two main types, when topical cream is enough versus when you need pills, and when the infection warrants a same-day visit. If you are asking about a child, see our separate guide to impetigo in children. Clinical claims are cited to the sources listed at the end.

What Is Impetigo?

Impetigo is a common, highly contagious bacterial infection of the superficial layers of the epidermis, the outer layer of the skin.[7] It is also called school sores in some places.[6] It starts as red, itchy sores that break open, leak clear fluid or pus for a few days, and then form a crusty yellow or honey-colored scab.[4]

The bacteria responsible are group A Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes) and Staphylococcus aureus, alone or together.[4] What matters for treatment is that antibiotics should target both organisms.[5] Diagnosis is made mainly by looking at the skin; a culture is usually not needed, but it is worth considering when treatment is not working, the infection keeps coming back, or the diagnosis is uncertain.[3]

Nonbullous vs Bullous Impetigo

Impetigo comes in two forms that look different and have different causes.

For treatment, the distinction matters less than many older sources suggest. The IDSA guideline and a 2026 American Family Physician review both recommend topical mupirocin or retapamulin for mild bullous as well as nonbullous impetigo, with oral antibiotics for numerous lesions, outbreaks, or disease that does not respond.[1][3] Some reference texts still advise oral antibiotics for every case of bullous impetigo, so your clinician may choose pills based on how much skin is involved.[7]

What Causes Impetigo and Who Gets It?

Impetigo develops when staph or strep bacteria get into the skin through a break in the skin's surface. In many cases the break is something small, such as a cut, a scratch that barely breaks the skin, a bug bite, a rash, a sore, or a burn. Scratching itchy eczema or chickenpox can also break the skin and let bacteria in.[6] Sometimes impetigo develops on unbroken skin.[6]

Because the bacteria enter through damaged skin, certain conditions and situations raise the risk. Risk factors include disruptions to the skin barrier, poor hygiene, crowded living environments, hot and humid climates, malnutrition, and diabetes.[3] Crowded settings where it spreads include daycare centers and schools, detention or correctional facilities, homeless shelters, and military training facilities.[4] Contact sports such as wrestling and football also carry a higher risk because of skin-to-skin contact.[6] In adults, impetigo may follow another skin problem or develop after a cold or other virus,[9] and older adults, people with diabetes, and people with a weakened immune system are more likely to get it.[10]

Is Impetigo Contagious? How It Spreads

Yes, and this is the single most important thing to understand about impetigo. Contact with someone who has impetigo, including contact with drainage from the sores, is the most common way people get it.[5] If someone has impetigo, the bacteria often spread to other people in their household.[4]

It can also spread indirectly through shared towels, clothing, bed linens, or sports equipment.[6][10] A person can also spread the infection to other parts of their own body, which is why covering lesions matters.[5]

The incubation period, the time from exposure to first sores, is about 10 days when the cause is group A strep.[4] That lag means the source of an infection is not always obvious.

What Does Impetigo Look Like?

Impetigo can occur anywhere on the body but most often affects exposed skin, commonly the arms, legs, mouth, and nose.[4] The typical sequence looks like this:

  • First, red, itchy sores appear.[4]
  • Over four to six days, red bumps turn into blisters that rupture[3] and leak a clear fluid or pus for a few days.[4]
  • The ruptured sores then crust over with a golden, honey-colored, or yellowish-brown scab.[5][11]
  • In bullous impetigo, small blisters grow into larger, loose blisters filled with clear or yellow fluid before they rupture.[7]

The crusts are the most recognizable sign and the reason impetigo is easy for a clinician to identify at a glance. Scarring is rare, though some people notice changes in skin color afterward.[4][7]

How Long Does Impetigo Last?

One reference text estimates that untreated impetigo heals in 14 to 21 days, that about 20 percent of cases resolve on their own, and that with treatment, cure occurs within 10 days.[7] Research on untreated impetigo is less tidy: a 2021 systematic review of placebo groups in trials found that at about 7 days, between 13 and 74 percent of people had resolved, while 16 to 41 percent had failed to improve.[8] The more practical reason to treat is not the speed of healing alone; antibiotic treatment limits your ability to pass the bacteria to others.[5]

Impetigo vs Cold Sore vs Hand, Foot, and Mouth

Several common skin conditions get mistaken for impetigo, and the distinction changes how they are treated. Impetigo is bacterial; cold sores and hand, foot, and mouth disease are viral.

ConditionCauseDistinguishing featuresTreatment
ImpetigoBacteria (staph, strep)Thick, honey-colored crusts, most often on the face and limbs; highly contagious[5][7]Topical or oral antibiotics[1]
Cold sore (herpes simplex)Virus (usually HSV-1)[14]Small blisters on the lips or mouth, often preceded by itching, burning, or tingling; can turn yellow and crusty as they heal[14]Goes away on its own in 1 to 2 weeks; antivirals can help[14]
Hand, foot, and mouthVirus (most often coxsackievirus A16)[15]Ulcers in the mouth and throat plus small blisters on the hands and feet; fever[15]Symptom relief; recovery in 5 to 7 days[15]

If you are unsure which one you are looking at, papules that progress to ruptured blisters with honey-colored crusts point toward impetigo,[3] but cold sores can also crust yellow as they heal,[14] so it is worth having a clinician look, especially given how contagious impetigo is.[9]

Topical Treatment: Mupirocin and Retapamulin

For mild impetigo with only a few sores, a topical antibiotic is the usual first treatment, and this applies to bullous as well as nonbullous impetigo.[1][3][5] The two agents recommended by U.S. guidelines are:

  • Mupirocin 2% ointment. The IDSA guideline recommends applying it twice daily for 5 days;[1] the FDA label directs use 3 times daily for up to 10 days, with re-evaluation if there is no response within 3 to 5 days.[16]
  • Retapamulin 1% ointment, applied twice daily for 5 days.[1][17] Its label covers only methicillin-susceptible staph (not MRSA) and limits treatment in adults to a total area of up to 100 square centimeters.[17]

The evidence supporting a topical-first approach is solid. The 2012 Cochrane review of interventions for impetigo found that topical antibiotics cured more people than placebo and that topical mupirocin and fusidic acid were as effective as, or more effective than, oral antibiotics. Because few studies included people with extensive impetigo, the review could not say whether pills work better in that group.[2] The IDSA guideline recommends mupirocin or retapamulin twice daily for 5 days for both bullous and nonbullous impetigo and recommends oral therapy for numerous lesions or outbreaks.[1] A newer topical quinolone, ozenoxacin 1% cream, was approved by the FDA in 2017, but it has been discontinued in the United States as of September 2025.[3]

Two practical points. First, removing the crust with soap and water before applying the ointment helps the antibiotic reach the skin beneath.[7] Second, wear gloves when applying the ointment and wash your hands thoroughly afterward, since the tool of treatment can also be a tool of spread.[10]

Oral Antibiotics: When and Which

Oral antibiotics are recommended when topical treatment is not enough: when there are numerous lesions, during outbreaks affecting several people, for ecthyma, or for severe, multilesional disease that does not respond to topical therapy within 3 to 5 days.[1][3]

Because staph from impetigo is usually methicillin susceptible, the standard first choices are dicloxacillin or cephalexin, given as a 7-day course.[1] Oral antibiotics should target both group A strep and S. aureus.[3] When MRSA is suspected or confirmed, the IDSA guideline recommends doxycycline, clindamycin, or sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim (TMP-SMX) instead.[1] MRSA is an increasing cause of impetigo, and a lab sample from the skin can show whether MRSA is the cause.[9]

Regardless of which antibiotic is used, take it for the full length of time prescribed, even if the sores look better in a few days, to help prevent reinfection.[11]

The split is straightforward: mild disease, bullous or not, usually gets a cream; numerous lesions, outbreaks, ecthyma, or no response in 3 to 5 days get pills; and anything with fever or spreading redness deserves prompt medical attention rather than self-treatment.

When Can I Return to Work or School?

This is one of the first questions adults ask, and the guidance is reassuringly specific. People with impetigo can return to work or school once they have started antibiotic treatment and covered all sores on exposed skin.[4] CDC clinical guidance says to wait at least 12 hours after starting antibiotics, with lesions covered, and notes that staying home for at least 24 hours should be considered for health care workers or during a group A strep outbreak.[5] A 2026 American Family Physician review suggests not returning until 12 to 24 hours after starting antibiotics or until clinical improvement occurs.[3] If you are asking about a child returning to school or daycare, see our impetigo in children guide.

Complications and Red Flags

Most cases of impetigo are mild and resolve without lasting harm. Complications are uncommon but worth knowing because one of them develops late, after the skin has already healed.

  • Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (PSGN). A rare delayed kidney complication that most often appears one to two weeks after the original infection resolves, thought to result from an immune response triggered by the strep bacteria.[5] It usually causes protein in the urine and high blood pressure, and can cause dark, reddish-brown urine, less urine, and swelling, especially in the face, around the eyes, and in the hands and feet. PSGN is more common in children, but its rare long-term kidney complications are more common in adults.[12]
  • Ecthyma. A deep form of impetigo in which ulcers penetrate through the epidermis into the dermis and look punched out.[7] The sores are painful and can leave scars,[10] and ecthyma should be treated with an oral antibiotic.[1]
  • Cellulitis. A potentially life-threatening infection of the tissues under the skin that may spread to the lymph nodes and bloodstream.[10] Signs include redness that gets bigger as the infection spreads, pain or tenderness, warm skin, and fever with chills.[13]
See a clinician promptly if

You have fever, spreading redness, worsening pain, many lesions, or deep, painful sores, or the sores are not improving after 3 to 5 days of treatment.[13][1][10][16] Get care early if you have diabetes or a weakened immune system.[10][13] Also seek care if you notice swelling of the face, around the eyes, or in the hands and feet, dark reddish-brown (tea-colored) or bloody urine, or less urine in the weeks after a skin infection, as these can signal the kidney complication PSGN.[12][11] Seek emergency care if you feel very sick, for example with a very high fever, low blood pressure, or vomiting that does not stop, or if the infection is around your eyes.[13]

Prevention and Home Care

Because impetigo spreads so easily, prevention is mostly about interrupting the hand-to-sores cycle. Cover the sores, practice good hand hygiene,[5] avoid touching sores that are oozing, and wash your hands thoroughly after touching infected skin.[9] Wash clothing, linens, and towels used by the infected person every day, and do not share them with others in the household until they have been washed.[5] Do not share razors or other personal care products.[9]

At home, clipping fingernails to avoid scratching and gently washing the skin a few times a day with antibacterial soap to remove crusts and drainage can help.[11] The Cochrane review found little evidence for disinfectant (antiseptic) solutions, and topical antibiotics worked better than disinfectants in pooled studies, so antiseptics are not a substitute for antibiotic treatment.[2]
